    Aim, theory and procedure

    Aim

    (a) To prepare a temporary mount of a leaf peel (or a leaf impression) to show stomata.
    (b) To study the distribution of stomata on the upper and lower surfaces of a leaf by finding the stomatal frequency of each surface.

    Apparatus

    A fresh leaf, forceps, a needle, a brush, watch glass, water, safranin solution, glycerine, glass slides, coverslips, blotting paper, a compound microscope, a transparent ruler with mm divisions. For the impression method: clear nail polish and clear sticky tape.

    Theory

    Stomata are tiny pores in the epidermis of leaves. Each pore is surrounded by two guard cells. Guard cells contain chloroplasts and have a thick inner wall next to the pore. When they take in water they become turgid and bow apart, so the pore opens; when they lose water they become flaccid and the pore closes. Stomata allow exchange of gases (CO₂ in, O₂ out during photosynthesis) and the loss of water vapour in transpiration.

    In dicot leaves the guard cells are kidney (bean) shaped. In grasses (monocots such as maize and wheat) they are dumb-bell shaped. Most dicot leaves have far more stomata on the shaded lower surface, which reduces water loss; many monocot leaves, which grow upright, have nearly equal numbers on both surfaces; floating leaves (water lily) have them only on the upper surface.

    The diameter of the field of view is inversely proportional to the objective magnification, so

    D₄₀ = D₁₀ × 10/40  ·  A = π (D₄₀/2)²

    Stomatal frequency = n / A  (stomata per mm²)

    A related measure is the stomatal index, I = S/(S + E) × 100, where S and E are the numbers of stomata and epidermal cells in the same area.

    Procedure

    1. Calibrate: place a transparent mm ruler on the stage, focus at 10× and count how many millimetres fit across the diameter of the field. Divide by 4 to get the field diameter at 40×.
    2. Leaf peel: fold the leaf and tear it obliquely; a thin, colourless layer (the epidermis) sticks out at the torn edge. Lift it with forceps from the surface you want.
    3. Put the peel in a watch glass of water so it does not dry.
    4. Add 2–3 drops of safranin and leave for about 2 minutes. Wash off the extra stain in water.
    5. Place the peel in a drop of glycerine on a clean slide. Lower a coverslip slowly with a needle at 45° so no air bubbles are trapped. Remove extra glycerine with blotting paper.
    6. Leaf impression (alternative): paint a thin patch of clear nail polish on the leaf, let it dry, press clear tape on it, peel off the impression and stick the tape flat on a slide.
    7. Focus first at 10× with the coarse and then the fine knob. Centre a clear area, change to 40× and use only the fine knob.
    8. Count the stomata in the field. Count a stoma on the edge only if more than half of it is inside the field.
    9. Move to a new field and repeat; count at least three fields on each surface. Calculate the stomatal frequency and compare the two surfaces.

    Precautions

    • Use a fresh leaf, and take a thin peel; a thick peel has overlapping layers.
    • Never let the peel dry: keep it in water and mount it in glycerine.
    • Do not over-stain; wash off extra safranin.
    • Lower the coverslip gently with a needle to avoid air bubbles; wipe off extra glycerine.
    • Focus at low power first. At high power use only the fine adjustment so the objective does not hit the coverslip.
    • Count fields that do not overlap, avoid fields with air bubbles, and count every field in sharp focus.

    Sources of error

    • Stomata are not evenly spread, so a few fields may not represent the whole leaf.
    • Stomata on the edge of the field may be counted wrongly.
    • A stoma slightly out of the focal plane may be missed (the depth of field at 40× is very small).
    • Error in reading the field diameter from the ruler is squared in the area.
    • Air bubbles, folds or a torn peel can hide stomata.
