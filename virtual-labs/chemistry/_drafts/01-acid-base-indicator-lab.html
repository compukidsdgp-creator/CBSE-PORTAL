    Aim, theory and procedure

    Aim

    To study the properties of acids and bases (dilute HCl and dilute NaOH) by their reaction with (a) litmus solution (blue and red) and other indicators, (b) zinc metal and (c) solid sodium carbonate.

    Apparatus and materials

    Test tubes, test-tube stand, test-tube holder, droppers, spatula, cork with a bent delivery tube, spirit lamp, wooden splinter and matchbox; dilute HCl, dilute NaOH, blue and red litmus solution, phenolphthalein, methyl orange, zinc granules, solid sodium carbonate and freshly prepared lime water.

    Theory

    Indicators change colour with the nature of the solution. Acids give H⁺(aq) ions in water; bases (alkalis) give OH⁻(aq) ions.

    IndicatorIn acid (HCl)In base (NaOH)
    Blue litmusturns redno change
    Red litmusno changeturns blue
    Phenolphthaleinstays colourlessturns pink
    Methyl orangeturns redturns yellow

    Zinc displaces hydrogen from dilute HCl in the cold. It also reacts with sodium hydroxide, but only on warming, to form sodium zincate:

    Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑

    Zn(s) + 2NaOH(aq) —heat→ Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑

    Hydrogen burns with a pop sound when a burning splinter is brought near it.

    Metal carbonates react with acids to give a salt, water and carbon dioxide, which turns lime water milky. Sodium carbonate does not react with sodium hydroxide, because both are basic.

    Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑

    Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l)

    If too much CO₂ is passed, the milkiness disappears, because soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate forms:

    CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)

    Procedure

    1. Indicators: take about 2 mL of sample A in a clean test tube and add 2–3 drops of blue litmus solution. Shake gently and note the colour. Repeat with sample B.
    2. Repeat step 1 in fresh test tubes with red litmus solution, phenolphthalein and methyl orange.
    3. Zinc: put two zinc granules in a test tube and add about 2 mL of sample A. Watch the surface of the zinc for bubbles.
    4. Close the mouth of the tube with a cork for a few seconds so that the gas collects. Remove the cork and at once bring a burning splinter near the mouth. Note what happens.
    5. Repeat with sample B. If nothing happens in the cold, hold the tube in a holder and warm it gently over the spirit lamp, then test the gas again.
    6. Sodium carbonate: put a spatula-full of solid sodium carbonate in a test tube and add about 2 mL of sample A.
    7. At once fit the cork with the delivery tube and dip its other end into freshly prepared lime water. Watch the lime water.
    8. Repeat with sample B. Record every observation and write your inference.

    Precautions

    • Handle acids and alkalis carefully. Never taste or smell them; wash any spill with plenty of water.
    • Use clean test tubes and a separate dropper for each reagent, so that samples are not mixed.
    • Use small quantities: a test tube should not be more than about one-third full.
    • While warming, hold the tube with a holder, heat it gently and point its mouth away from yourself and others. Never heat a corked tube.
    • Use freshly prepared, clear lime water and pass the gas only while the effervescence is brisk.

    Sources of error

    • A dirty tube or a shared dropper can carry acid into the base (or the reverse) and give a misleading colour.
    • Reading a colour before the indicator has spread through the liquid.
    • The oxide coat on zinc delays the first bubbles by a few seconds; a quick look may suggest no reaction.
    • The hydrogen escapes quickly from an open tube, so the pop test may fail if the gas is not collected first.
    • Passing CO₂ for too long can make milky lime water clear again; stale lime water may not turn milky at all.
