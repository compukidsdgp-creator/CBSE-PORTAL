      Aim, theory and procedure

      Aim

      To determine the molarity and strength of a given solution of sodium hydroxide by titrating it against a standard M/10 solution of oxalic acid.

      Apparatus and materials

      Burette (50 mL) with stand and clamp, 10 mL pipette, conical flask (100 mL), funnel, white glazed tile, wash bottle with distilled water, beakers; standard M/10 oxalic acid solution, the given NaOH solution, phenolphthalein indicator.

      Theory

      Oxalic acid is a weak dibasic acid. It reacts with sodium hydroxide, a strong base, in the ratio 1 : 2:

      (COOH)₂ + 2NaOH → (COONa)₂ + 2H₂O

      At the equivalence point, moles of NaOH = 2 × moles of oxalic acid. Using the molarity equation with n₁ = 1 (acid) and n₂ = 2 (base):

      M₁V₁ / 1 = M₂V₂ / 2  ⇒  M₂ = 2M₁V₁ / V₂

      Strength (g L⁻¹) = Molarity × Molar mass of NaOH (40 g mol⁻¹)

      Sodium oxalate formed at the equivalence point hydrolyses slightly, so the solution is weakly alkaline (pH ≈ 8.3). Phenolphthalein, which changes from colourless to pink between pH 8.2 and 10, therefore marks the endpoint. Methyl orange (pH 3.1–4.4) would change far too early.

      Example: if V₂ = 20.0 mL, M₂ = 2 × 0.1 × 10.0 / 20.0 = 0.100 M and strength = 0.100 × 40 = 4.00 g L⁻¹.

      Procedure

      1. Rinse the burette with distilled water and then with a little of the NaOH solution. Clamp it vertically.
      2. Fill the burette with NaOH using a funnel; remove the funnel. Open the stopcock briefly to drive out any air bubble from the jet.
      3. Note the initial reading at the lower meniscus, with your eye level with it.
      4. Rinse the pipette with the oxalic acid solution and pipette 10.0 mL of it into a conical flask rinsed with water only. Do not blow out the last drop.
      5. Add 1–2 drops of phenolphthalein. The solution stays colourless.
      6. Place the flask on a white tile under the burette. Add NaOH while swirling the flask. As you near the endpoint, the pink where each drop falls takes longer to disappear: now add one drop at a time.
      7. Stop when one drop gives a light pink colour that stays for about 30 seconds. Note the final reading.
      8. The first (rough) titration gives an idea of the volume. Repeat the titration, running in quickly to about 1 mL short of the rough value and then drop by drop, until two readings agree within 0.1 mL (concordant readings).
      9. Calculate M₂ and the strength from the concordant volume.

      Precautions

      • Rinse the burette and pipette with the solutions they will hold; rinse the conical flask with distilled water only.
      • Remove the funnel and the air bubble from the jet before taking the initial reading.
      • Read the lower meniscus for colourless solutions, keeping your eye at the level of the meniscus.
      • Swirl the flask throughout and add the last few drops one at a time.
      • Do not blow out the last drop from the pipette.
      • Use a white tile so the faint pink at the endpoint is easy to see.
      • Keep the NaOH bottle closed: NaOH absorbs CO₂ from the air.

      Sources of error

      • Parallax in reading the burette if the eye is not level with the meniscus.
      • Drop size: the endpoint can only be found to within about one drop (≈ 0.05 mL).
      • Judging the first permanent pink; overshooting gives a dark pink and too large a titre.
      • Drainage error in the pipette or burette if they are not clean (drops sticking to the walls).
      • NaOH absorbing CO₂ from the air, which changes its concentration and makes the pink fade.
      • In this simulation, the depth of the pink is a visual model; the volumes come from the real 1 : 2 stoichiometry.
