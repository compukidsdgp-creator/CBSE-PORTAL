      Aim, theory and procedure

      Aim

      (a) To find the pH of dilute hydrochloric acid, dilute sodium hydroxide, dilute ethanoic acid, lemon juice, water and dilute sodium hydrogen carbonate solution using pH paper (universal indicator). (b) To compare how the pH of a strong acid and a weak acid of the same concentration changes on dilution.

      Apparatus and materials

      Universal pH paper strips (range 1–14) with colour chart, narrow-range pH paper (0.5–5.0), the six samples and two unknown samples in labelled reagent bottles, test tubes and a test-tube stand, a glass rod (or droppers), a white glazed tile, a beaker of distilled water, a wash bottle and tissue paper.

      Theory

      The pH of a solution tells us how acidic or basic it is. It is defined as

      pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]    and    pH + pOH = 14 (at 25 °C)

      Pure water has [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ mol L⁻¹, so its pH is 7 (neutral). pH below 7 is acidic and above 7 is basic. A fall of one pH unit means [H⁺] has become ten times larger.

      Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators, so it shows a different colour at each pH: red (1–2), orange (3–4), yellow (5), yellow-green (6), green (7), blue-green (8–9), blue (10–11) and violet/purple (12–14).

      Strong acid (HCl) is almost fully ionised, so [H⁺] = C and pH = −log C: diluting it 10 times raises pH by 1. Weak acid (CH₃COOH) is only partly ionised:

      CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺    Kₐ = [H⁺]² / (C − [H⁺]) ≈ [H⁺]² / C

      pH ≈ ½ pKₐ − ½ log C

      So for a weak acid each 10-fold dilution raises pH by only about 0.5, and the degree of ionisation α rises as the solution gets more dilute. Sodium hydrogen carbonate is slightly basic because HCO₃⁻ ions take H⁺ from water: HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻.

      Procedure

      1. Put a strip of pH paper on a clean, dry white tile. The white background makes the colour easy to judge.
      2. Rinse the glass rod with distilled water and wipe it dry with tissue paper.
      3. Dip the rod into the first sample and touch it once to the middle of the strip. Do not rub.
      4. Wait a few seconds for the colour to develop fully, then compare the centre of the spot with the colour chart in good light. Note the colour and the pH.
      5. Rinse and wipe the rod again and use a fresh strip for every sample, so no sample carries into the next.
      6. Repeat for all six samples and the two unknown samples X and Y. Classify each as acidic, neutral or basic.
      7. Dilution (Class 11): take 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M CH₃COOH. Dilute 1 mL to 10 mL with distilled water to get 0.01 M, and repeat to get 10⁻³ M and 10⁻⁴ M. Find the pH of each with narrow-range paper.
      8. Plot pH against −log C for both acids and find the slopes. From the ethanoic acid line, pKₐ = 2 × intercept.

      Precautions

      • Rinse the glass rod with distilled water and wipe it before dipping it into the next sample.
      • Use a fresh pH strip for each sample, and touch the strip only with a clean, dry glass rod — never with fingers.
      • Compare the colour within a few seconds, after it has developed but before the paper dries.
      • Compare colours in good daylight against a white background.
      • Handle HCl and NaOH carefully; they are corrosive. Wash your hands after the experiment.
      • Keep the pH paper and chart dry and away from acid or ammonia fumes.

      Sources of error

      • pH paper gives only an approximate value (to the nearest whole number for universal paper, 0.5 for narrow-range paper).
      • Matching colours by eye depends on the light and on the person.
      • A rod that is not rinsed carries some of the previous sample, which shifts the colour.
      • Reading too early (colour not developed) or too late (paper drying, colour fading) gives a wrong value.
      • Distilled water left open absorbs CO₂ from air and becomes slightly acidic (pH ≈ 6).
      • At very low concentrations the formula pH ≈ ½ pKₐ − ½ log C is less accurate, so the pKₐ from the graph is approximate.
