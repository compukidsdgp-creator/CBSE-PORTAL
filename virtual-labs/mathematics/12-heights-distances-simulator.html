    Aim, theory and procedure

    Aim

    To make a clinometer and use it to find the height of an object (a school flagpole) by measuring the angle of elevation of its top from different distances.

    Apparatus

    A protractor, a drinking straw, a thread with a small weight (a nut or washer) as a plumb bob, cello tape, a 30 m measuring tape, a height chart or metre scale, a pencil and the practical file.

    Theory

    The angle of elevation of the top of an object is the angle between the horizontal line through your eye and the line of sight to the top, when the top is above your eye.

    The eye, the point on the pole level with the eye, and the top of the pole form a right triangle. So

    tan θ = (H − h) / d  ⇒  H = h + d · tan θ

    Reading a clinometer: the straw lies along the straight edge of the protractor and the plumb line always hangs vertically. When the straw is level the thread falls on 90°. Tilting the straw up by θ moves the thread to 90° − θ on one scale (90° + θ on the other), so θ = 90° − reading (or reading − 90°).

    Two stations (foot unreachable): if the angles from two points a distance s apart on the same line are α (farther) and β (nearer), then

    H − h = s · tan α · tan β / (tan β − tan α)

    Procedure

    1. Make the clinometer: tape the straw along the straight edge of the protractor. Tie the thread at the centre hole and hang the weight from it, so the thread swings freely across the scale.
    2. Stand straight against the height chart and ask a friend to mark the level of your eye. Read your eye height h.
    3. Lay the measuring tape on level ground with its zero at the foot of the flagpole.
    4. Stand at a point on the tape. Look through the straw and tilt the protractor until you see the top of the pole in the middle of the straw.
    5. Hold still until the plumb line stops swinging. Ask your friend to read the scale where the thread crosses it (or pinch the thread against the protractor and read it yourself).
    6. Read the distance d on the tape, directly below your eye.
    7. Repeat from at least six different distances, for example from about 6 m to 30 m.
    8. Work out θ = 90° − reading, tan θ and H = h + d tan θ for each reading. Plot θ against d and tan θ against 1/d.

    Precautions

    • The ground between you and the pole should be level, and the tape should be stretched straight, not sagging.
    • Sight the very top of the pole, not the flag or the rope.
    • Read the protractor only after the plumb line has come to rest.
    • Keep the protractor in a vertical plane; a twisted protractor gives a wrong reading.
    • Use the same eye height h that you measured, and add it to every value of d tan θ.
    • Avoid very steep (above 70°) or very shallow (below 15°) angles, where a small angle error gives a big height error.

    Sources of error

    • The protractor can be read only to about 1°; near 70° this alone changes the height by more than half a metre.
    • The hand shakes, so the straw may not point exactly at the top.
    • The plumb line may still be swinging, or rub against the protractor face.
    • The ground may not be perfectly level, and the eye may not be exactly above the point read on the tape.
    • The straw may not be taped exactly parallel to the 0°–180° line of the protractor.
