    Aim, theory and procedure

    Aim

    To verify Pythagoras theorem by measuring the sides of right-angled triangles, and by the method of paper cutting and pasting. To check the converse using a triangle that is not right-angled.

    Apparatus

    Graph paper or white sheets, coloured glazed paper, a geometry box (15 cm ruler, protractor, set squares, compasses), a sharp pencil, an eraser, scissors and glue.

    Theory

    Pythagoras theorem: in a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the sum of the squares on the other two sides. If ∠C = 90° in ΔABC, then

    AB² = BC² + CA²  i.e.  c² = a² + b²

    Converse: if in a triangle the square of one side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, the angle opposite the first side is a right angle.

    If ∠C is more than 90°, you will find c² > a² + b²; if ∠C is less than 90°, c² < a² + b². So the equality holds only for a right angle.

    Why the paper cutting works (Perigal’s dissection): two cuts through the centre of the square on the longer leg, one parallel and one perpendicular to the hypotenuse, give four congruent pieces. These four pieces and the square on the shorter leg can be slid, without turning, to cover the square on the hypotenuse exactly. Area is not lost or gained, so c² = a² + b².

    You will use this result again and again in Class 10: the distance formula, the identity sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, and heights and distances all rest on it.

    Procedure

    A. By measurement

    1. Draw a few right-angled triangles ABC of different sizes with ∠C = 90°, and one triangle where ∠C is not 90°. (In this lab they are already drawn: Δ1 to Δ4. One of them is not right-angled; find it.)
    2. Place the ruler along each side with its zero exactly on the starting vertex. Read the scale at the other vertex to the nearest millimetre.
    3. Place the protractor with its centre on C and its base line along CB. Read ∠C on the scale that starts from 0 on CB.
    4. Record a, b, c and ∠C. Work out a², b², a² + b² and c², and compare.

    B. By paper cutting and pasting

    1. On coloured paper, draw squares outwards on all three sides of the right triangle Δ1.
    2. On the square on the longer leg (CA), join the diagonals to find its centre O. Through O, draw one line parallel to AB and one perpendicular to AB.
    3. Cut out the square on BC and the four pieces of the square on CA.
    4. Without turning them over or rotating them, place the five pieces on the square on AB so that they cover it exactly, and paste them.

    Precautions

    • Use a sharp pencil so that lines are thin and vertices are clear points.
    • Keep the zero of the ruler exactly on the vertex, not the end of the ruler (the end is often worn).
    • Keep your eye directly above the mark you read, to avoid parallax.
    • Put the centre of the protractor exactly on the vertex and its base line exactly along one arm; read the scale that starts from 0 on that arm.
    • Cut exactly along the lines and do not rotate the pieces while fitting them.

    Sources of error

    • A ruler can only be read to the nearest millimetre, so each length may be out by up to 0.05 cm. Squaring makes this bigger: an error of 0.05 cm in a 10 cm side changes c² by about 1 cm².
    • The thickness of the pencil line makes the exact vertex uncertain.
    • The protractor can be read only to about 1°, and a small misplacement of its centre changes the reading.
    • Paper pieces cut slightly off the line leave tiny gaps or overlaps.
    • Measurement only verifies the theorem for the triangles you drew; it is not a proof. The paper-cutting method illustrates a proof by areas.
