      Aim, theory and procedure

      Aim

      To obtain the conditions for consistency of a pair of linear equations in two variables by the graphical method, and to solve a consistent pair graphically.

      Apparatus

      Graph paper (mm squares), a sharp pencil, a 15 cm ruler (scale) from the geometry box, an eraser, and three pairs of linear equations.

      Theory

      A pair of linear equations in two variables can be written as

      a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0  and  a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0

      The graph of each equation is a straight line. Every point on a line is a solution of that equation, so a point common to both lines is a solution of the pair. Two lines in a plane can do only one of three things:

      Compare the ratiosThe linesSolutionsThe pair is
      a₁/a₂ ≠ b₁/b₂intersect at one pointexactly one (unique)consistent
      a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂are parallelnoneinconsistent
      a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂coincideinfinitely manydependent (consistent)

      Why it works: if a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂, the two lines have the same slope −a/b, so they are parallel or the same line. If c₁/c₂ is also equal, one equation is just a multiple of the other.

      Procedure

      1. Draw the x-axis and y-axis on the graph paper and choose a scale (here 1 cm = 1 unit on both axes).
      2. For the first equation, choose three values of x and work out y. Pick x so that y is a whole number or a half — such points are easy to plot exactly.
      3. Plot the three points. They must lie in a straight line; if one does not, recheck its calculation.
      4. Join the points with a ruler and extend the line across the paper. Label it with its equation.
      5. Repeat steps 2–4 for the second equation on the same axes.
      6. See whether the lines intersect, are parallel or coincide. If they intersect, drop perpendiculars to the axes and read the coordinates of the point of intersection.
      7. Work out a₁/a₂, b₁/b₂ and c₁/c₂ and compare them. Repeat for all three pairs and record the results.

      Precautions

      • Use a sharp pencil so that points and lines are thin.
      • Use the same scale on both axes and mark it on the graph paper.
      • Plot at least three points for each line; the third point checks the other two.
      • Keep the points well apart so the ruler line is accurate.
      • Write each equation in the form ax + by + c = 0 before finding the ratios, and keep the signs.

      Limitations and sources of error

      • The graphical method gives only approximate values; a pencil line has thickness, so the crossing can be read only to about 0.1 unit.
      • If the solution has awkward values (like 7/3) or lies off the paper, the graph cannot show it accurately; algebraic methods (substitution, elimination) give the exact answer.
      • A small error in plotting one point tilts the line and moves the point of intersection.
      • Lines that are nearly parallel meet at a very narrow angle, which makes the crossing hard to locate.

      Viva voce

      Questions examiners often ask on this activity. Try to answer before you open each one.

      1. What is the general form of a pair of linear equations in two variables?

      a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0, where the a’s, b’s and c’s are real numbers and a₁² + b₁² ≠ 0, a₂² + b₂² ≠ 0 (a and b are not both zero).

      2. What does the graph of a linear equation in two variables look like?

      A straight line. Every point on the line is a solution of the equation, and every solution is a point on the line.

      3. When is a pair of linear equations called consistent?

      When it has at least one solution — either a unique solution (intersecting lines) or infinitely many solutions (coincident lines).

      4. If a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂, what can you say about the lines and the solutions?

      The lines are parallel. They never meet, so the pair has no solution and is inconsistent.

      5. How many solutions does a pair of coincident lines have? What is such a pair called?

      Infinitely many — every point on the line is a solution. The pair is called dependent, and it is consistent.

      6. Why do we plot three points for each line when two points are enough to fix a line?

      The third point is a check. If it does not lie on the line through the other two, there is a mistake in a calculation or in plotting.

      7. Without drawing, is the pair x − 2y = 0 and 3x + 4y − 20 = 0 consistent?

      a₁/a₂ = 1/3 and b₁/b₂ = −2/4 = −1/2. Since 1/3 ≠ −1/2 the lines intersect, so the pair is consistent with a unique solution, (4, 2).

      8. For what value of k will 2x + 3y = 5 and 4x + ky = 10 have infinitely many solutions?

      We need 2/4 = 3/k = 5/10. So 3/k = 1/2, which gives k = 6.

      9. Where does the line x + 2y = 4 cut the two axes?

      Put y = 0: x = 4, so it cuts the x-axis at (4, 0). Put x = 0: y = 2, so it cuts the y-axis at (0, 2).

      10. Why is the graphical method not always reliable for finding the solution?

      Readings are limited by the scale and by the thickness of the lines, so non-integer solutions such as (7/3, 5/4) can only be estimated. Substitution or elimination gives the exact solution.

      11. Can two distinct straight lines meet at two different points?

      No. Two distinct lines meet in at most one point. If they share two points, they are the same line (coincident).
