🏛️ History: India and the Contemporary World
Nationalism · Rise of Socialism · Industrialisation · Trade & Globalisation · Print Culture

📖 Core Theory

Ch 1: The Rise of Nationalism in Europe

Nationalism emerged as a powerful force in the 19th century Europe. The French Revolution (1789) spread ideas of liberty, equality, and fraternity. Napoleon's conquests carried these ideas across Europe. The unification of Germany (1871) under Bismarck and unification of Italy under Garibaldi and Cavour are key events. The concept of nation-state replaced multi-ethnic empires.

Nationalism
A sense of belonging to a nation united by common history, culture, language, or territory. Led to movements for independence and unification in 19th century Europe.
Romanticism
Cultural movement that stressed emotions, folk culture, and vernacular languages as the basis for national identity. Poets, artists glorified the "spirit of the nation."
Zollverein (1834)
German customs union created by Prussia, abolishing tariff barriers. United German states economically before political unification — economic nationalism preceding political nationalism.
Balkans Crisis
Break-up of Ottoman Empire led to conflict in Balkans. Slavic nationalism, supported by Russia, opposed Austria-Hungary. Contributed directly to World War I.

Ch 2: Nationalism in India

The Indian nationalist movement was shaped by: Non-Cooperation Movement (1920-22), Civil Disobedience Movement (1930), Quit India Movement (1942). Gandhi's methods — satyagraha (truth-force), non-violence, hartals — unified diverse groups. The partition of Bengal (1905) triggered the Swadeshi movement. Distinct identities of dalits, tribals, women were incorporated.

Satyagraha
Gandhi's method: insistence on truth; resist injustice through non-violent civil disobedience. Powerful moral force that delegitimised British rule.
Non-Cooperation
1920-22: Indians to return titles, boycott civil services, courts, legislature, schools, foreign goods. Called off after Chauri Chaura violence (Feb 1922).
Civil Disobedience
1930: Gandhi's Dandi March (March 12) to make salt, defying the Salt Law. Spread across country. Peasants, tribals, merchants all participated.
Quit India
1942 Bombay Resolution: "Do or Die" — demanded immediate end to British rule. Mass arrests followed; underground resistance organised.

🔑 Key Dates — Nationalism

1789: French Revolution — ideas of liberty spread across Europe
1848: Revolutions across Europe — "Spring of Nations" (failed)
1871: Unification of Germany under Bismarck; Italy unified under Cavour/Garibaldi
1905: Partition of Bengal → Swadeshi movement
1920: Non-Cooperation; 1930: Civil Disobedience (Dandi March 12 March); 1942: Quit India

Ch 3: Nationalism in Indo-China (Vietnam)

French colonised Indo-China (Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia). Aimed to "civilise" through education, but Vietnamese resisted. The Scholars Revolt (1868), Huynh Phu So's Hoa Hao movement, and Ho Chi Minh's Communist Party led anti-colonial struggle. USA involvement → Vietnam War. Fall of Saigon (1975) reunified Vietnam.

Domino Theory
US feared that if one country became communist, neighbours would follow like dominoes. Led to massive US military intervention in Vietnam (1960s-70s).
Agent Orange
Toxic chemical herbicide used by US forces to destroy forests and crops where Viet Cong hid. Caused long-term health damage; symbol of brutal US tactics.

Ch 5: The Age of Industrialisation

Before the industrial revolution, "proto-industrialisation" existed — cottage industries. The British textile industry (cotton, wool) was first to industrialise. James Watt's steam engine (1784) transformed production. New cities — Manchester, Leeds — grew around factories. In India, Bombay and Ahmedabad became centres of the cotton textile industry (Dwarkanath Tagore, Dinshaw Petit).

Proto-industrialisation
Pre-factory system: merchants gave raw material to rural/urban craftsmen who produced finished goods at home. Widespread in England and Europe before factories.
Staple
Coarse wool with short fibres, used to make ordinary cloth. Britain's woollen industry dominated before cotton. Combers and weavers worked in countryside.

Ch 7: Print Culture and the Modern World

Printing came to Europe via Gutenberg's printing press (c.1448). Enabled Reformation (Martin Luther's 95 Theses, 1517 — 5,000 copies in weeks). Newspapers, pamphlets created public opinion. In India, printing arrived with European missionaries (Goa, 16th century). Newspapers: Bengal Gazette (James Hicky, 1780). Print spread ideas of reform — Ram Mohan Roy, Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

Gutenberg Press
c.1448: Moveable metal type. Made printing fast and cheap. 180 copies of the Bible printed first. Within 50 years, 20 million books in Europe.
Vernacular Press Act
1878: British tried to control Indian language press that criticised colonial rule. Shown up the threat of print to authority.

Example 1 — Nationalism in India (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Why did Gandhi call off the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922?

The movement had spread widely and threatened to turn violent.
2

Case — Nationalism and Gandhi: "Mahatma Gandhi recognised that this was a possibility. He was the one who had made the Indian national movement into a mass movement. From 1915 onwards, he had set up ashrams, recruited a band of followers, and experimented with the idea of satyagraha... He travelled across the country, understanding the people, their needs, their beliefs." — NCERT India and the Contemporary World

Q1 (1M) What do you understand by 'satyagraha'?
Ans: Satyagraha (truth-force) is Gandhi's method of non-violent resistance — using peaceful means, truth, and moral force to resist unjust laws, without hatred of the oppressor.
Q2 (2M) Why was Gandhi's movement called a 'mass movement'?
Ans: Earlier nationalist movements were limited to educated elites. Gandhi brought in peasants, workers, women, lower castes — making nationalism truly national. He used local language, religious symbols (Ram Rajya), and connected struggles (Khilafat, non-cooperation) to appeal to all sections of Indian society.
Q3 (2M) Name two satyagraha movements Gandhi launched before the Non-Cooperation Movement.
Ans: (1) Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — indigo farmers in Bihar against oppressive tinkathia system. (2) Kheda Satyagraha (1918) — peasants in Gujarat demanding remission of land revenue during crop failure.
SA (3M) Explain the role of print culture in the growth of nationalism in India.
Ans: (1) Spread of ideas: Newspapers like Kesari (Tilak), Indian Opinion (Gandhi) spread nationalist ideas in local languages, creating a shared national consciousness. (2) Criticism of British rule: The press exposed racial discrimination, economic exploitation (drain of wealth) and oppressive laws, mobilising public opinion. (3) Unity: Regional newspapers in Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Urdu created a sense of pan-Indian identity among people who had never met. However, the British countered with press censorship acts (Vernacular Press Act, 1878).
LA (5M) Describe the main features of the Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–34). Why was it more significant than Non-Cooperation?
Ans: Features: (1) Salt March (Dandi March, March 12 – April 6, 1930): Gandhi marched 240 miles to make salt from seawater, breaking the Salt Law — the trigger for mass civil disobedience. (2) Illegal salt making across the country. Merchants refused to sell foreign goods, lawyers boycotted courts, students left schools. (3) Wider participation: Rich peasants, women in large numbers, tribal communities (forest laws), merchants affected by tariff policy all joined. (4) Poona Pact (1932): Agreement between Gandhi and Ambedkar on reserved seats for Dalits within general electorate. (5) Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931): Gandhi called off CDM; Congress attended Round Table Conference in London. More significant than NCM because: (1) Women participated massively. (2) The poor participated — salt symbolised common people's exploitation. (3) It showed that the British could not govern without Indian cooperation. (4) Even though it was called off twice, it consolidated the idea that independence was achievable.

🌍 Geography: Resources and Development
Resources · Agriculture · Water Resources · Minerals · Manufacturing · Lifelines

📖 Core Theory

Ch 1: Resources and Development

Resources are everything in our environment that satisfies our needs. Classified by: origin (biotic/abiotic), exhaustibility (renewable/non-renewable), ownership (individual/community/national/international), development status (potential/developed). Land use is influenced by physical factors (climate, soil) and human factors (population, tech).

Biotic vs Abiotic
Biotic: obtained from biosphere (plants, animals). Abiotic: non-living things (rocks, metals, water).
Renewable Resources
Can be renewed or reproduced in short time — solar energy, wind, water. Non-renewable: coal, petroleum (take millions of years to form).
Resource Planning
3 stages: (i) identification and inventory; (ii) evolving a planning structure; (iii) matching resource development with national development plans.
Agenda 21
Adopted at Earth Summit 1992 (Rio de Janeiro): plan for sustainable development, combating environmental damage, poverty, disease. Signed by 178 nations.

Ch 4: Agriculture

India: 2/3 of population depends on agriculture. Types of farming: subsistence (primitive subsistence, intensive subsistence) and commercial (plantations). Major crops: Kharif (June-Sept; paddy, maize, cotton, groundnut, jute); Rabi (Oct-March; wheat, barley, peas, mustard). Green Revolution increased wheat, rice production dramatically.

Kharif Crops
Sown in rainy season (June); harvested in autumn (Sept-Oct). Paddy, maize, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut, soyabean.
Rabi Crops
Sown in winter (Oct-Nov); harvested in summer (April-May). Wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard.
Plantation Agriculture
Single cash crop on large area with capital-intensive technology. Tea (Assam, WB), coffee (Karnataka), rubber (Kerala). Initially by British companies.
Green Revolution
1960s-70s: HYV seeds + fertilisers + irrigation. Transformed Punjab, Haryana, western UP into India's wheat bowl. M.S. Swaminathan, Norman Borlaug.

Ch 3: Water Resources

Only 2.5% of world water is fresh; most is locked in glaciers and ice caps. India has 4% of world's water but 16% of world's population. Multipurpose river projects: Bhakra-Nangal (Sutlej), Hirakud (Mahanadi), Nagarjunasagar (Krishna). Rainwater harvesting: ancient tradition — kuls, johads, tankas, bawolis, surangams.

Multipurpose Projects
Dams serving irrigation, power, flood control, fish farming, navigation, recreation. Jawaharlal Nehru called them "temples of modern India." Critics: displacement, ecological damage.
Rainwater Harvesting
Traditional water collection systems. Kuls (Western Himalayas), johads (Rajasthan), tanka (Rajasthan rooftop cistern), surangam (Kerala tunnels), kul/katha (Maharashtra).

🔑 Key Facts — Geography

India's land area: 3.28 million sq km — 2.4% of world's area but 16% of world's population
Net sown area of India: about 140 million hectares
Iron ore reserves: Jharkhand, Orissa, Chhattisgarh — Jharkhand alone has 25% of India's reserves
Largest coalfield: Gondwana (Jharkhand, Bengal, MP) — 80% of India's total reserves

A-R 1 A: Overuse of land resources leads to land degradation.
R: Deforestation, mining, overgrazing, unscientific farming and chemical fertilisers destroy soil structure, increase salinity, waterlogging, and soil erosion — making land unproductive. This reduces biodiversity and increases desertification.
Ans: (A)

Case — Water Scarcity: India receives approximately 400 million hectare metres of rainfall annually, yet faces water scarcity. Some states like Rajasthan have traditionally used khadin, tankas, and johads for water conservation. A village in Rajasthan revived a community johad and within two years, the local river began flowing again.

Q1 (1M) What is a 'johad'?
Ans: A johad is a traditional community rainwater harvesting structure — an earthen check dam that collects and stores rainwater, allowing groundwater recharge.
Q2 (2M) Why does India face water scarcity despite high rainfall?
Ans: (1) Uneven spatial distribution — some areas get very high rainfall, others very low. (2) Seasonal concentration — 75% of rain in 3-4 months (monsoon). (3) Rapid runoff — little percolates into groundwater. (4) Overexploitation of groundwater for agriculture (tube wells). (5) Population pressure and increasing per-capita demand.
Q3 (2M) Name two traditional water harvesting methods and their states.
Ans: (1) Kul (HP/J&K) — diversion channels from glacier streams to terraced fields. (2) Bamboo drip irrigation (Meghalaya) — 200-year-old system channelling spring water to fields. Also: Tankas (Rajasthan), Ahar-pyne (Bihar), Zabo (Nagaland).
SA (3M) Explain the distribution of coal in India. Why is it called the backbone of Indian industry?
Ans: Distribution: India has two types: (1) Gondwana coal (about 98% reserves) in Damodar Valley (WB, Jharkhand), Warora-Chandrapur (Maharashtra), Talcher (Odisha), Singrauli (MP). (2) Tertiary coal (younger, found in NE) in Assam, Meghalaya, Nagaland. Backbone of industry: Coal provides energy for thermal power plants (generates 70% of India's electricity), is used in iron-and-steel production (metallurgical coal), in railways (historically), and as raw material in chemicals. Without coal, India's industrial production would collapse.
LA (5M) Describe the types of farming in India and identify the factors that affect agricultural productivity.
Ans: Types: (1) Subsistence farming: Small holdings, primitive tools, output mainly for family. Intensive subsistence (paddy, wheat) in densely populated areas; primitive subsistence (shifting cultivation/jhum) in tribal areas. (2) Commercial farming: Crops grown for market — sugarcane, cotton, jute. Large mechanised farms, use of HYV seeds and fertilisers. (3) Plantation farming: Tea (Assam), coffee (Karnataka), rubber (Kerala) — export-oriented, large estates. Factors affecting productivity: (1) Natural: Soil quality (alluvial best), rainfall, temperature, relief. (2) Institutional: Land tenure — small fragmented holdings reduce productivity. Land reforms (abolition of zamindari) improved it. (3) Technological: HYV seeds (Green Revolution), irrigation, fertilisers, machinery. (4) Infrastructure: Credit, storage, transport, market access. India's productivity remains low for most crops vs global levels — irrigation coverage and mechanisation need to improve.
⚖️ Political Science: Power Sharing & Democracy
Power Sharing · Federalism · Democracy · Political Parties · Outcomes of Democracy

📖 Core Theory

Ch 1: Power Sharing

Power sharing avoids conflict and instability. Belgium example: equal representation of Dutch and French communities; Brussels has separate government. Sri Lanka example: Sinhala-only policy (1956) marginalised Tamils, leading to civil war. Power sharing forms: between organs of government, different levels, social groups, political parties.

Horizontal Power Sharing
Among organs of government: Legislature (makes laws), Executive (implements), Judiciary (interprets). System of checks and balances. None can act unchecked.
Vertical Power Sharing
Among levels of government: Central, State, Local (panchayat/municipality). Federalism: power constitutionally divided between national and sub-national units.
Community Government
Belgium's third tier: elected by Dutch, French, German-speaking communities. Has power over education, culture, language — irrespective of residence area.

Ch 2: Federalism

Features of India's federalism: two or more levels of government; own jurisdictions; constitutionally guaranteed. Union List (97 subjects: defence, foreign affairs), State List (66 subjects: police, trade, agriculture), Concurrent List (47 subjects: education, forests). Decentralisation: 1992 amendment — 3rd tier of panchayati raj and urban local bodies.

Union List
97 subjects of national importance (defence, banking, communications, currency). Only Parliament can legislate.
State List
66 subjects of state and local importance (police, courts below HC, trade within state, agriculture). State legislatures legislate.
Concurrent List
47 subjects (education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption). Both Parliament and State Legislatures can make laws; in conflict, Parliament prevails.

Ch 3: Democracy and Diversity + Political Parties

Political parties: organised groups competing for political power. Functions: contest elections, form governments, shape public opinion, provide alternatives. India's multi-party system. Challenges: dynastic succession, money and criminal power, no internal democracy, ideology giving way to electoral win.

National Party
Recognised in at least 4 states (2% seats in Lok Sabha from those states, or 6% of valid votes in state elections + 4 seats in LS). Current: BJP, Congress, NCP, BSP, CPI(M), TMC, NPP.
Pressure Groups
Organisations that seek to influence government without contesting elections (trade unions, FICCI, BAMCEF). Section-based or cause-based. Differ from political parties.

🔑 Key Distinctions — Political Science

Coming together federation: USA, Switzerland — independent states combine voluntarily
Holding together federation: India, Spain, Belgium — large country divides power to accommodate diversity
Coalition government: No single party majority → parties form alliance to govern (e.g. UPA, NDA)

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1 — Federalism (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Mention any three features of federalism as practised in India.

1
Two or more levels of government: Central + State + Local. Each level has its own jurisdiction guaranteed by the Constitution.
2
Powers divided in three lists: Union (defence), State (police), Concurrent (education). Residual powers with Centre.
3
Independent judiciary (Supreme Court) to resolve disputes between Centre and States about division of powers.
✅ Three features: multiple levels, constitutional division of power, independent judiciary.

(A) Second (B) Fifth (C) Seventh (D) Tenth

A-R 1 A: Federalism works well in India despite its diverse population.
R: India's federal system has a strong Centre, independent judiciary, and Constitutional provisions for both Central and State lists — balancing unity and diversity. Linguistic reorganisation of states (1956) also helped by giving each major linguistic group its own state administration.
Ans: (A)

Case — Democracy and Participation: In a village panchayat election, 78% of eligible voters voted. Out of elected representatives, 35% were women and 22% were from scheduled castes. The elected Gram Sabha meets quarterly to discuss development works. However, a study found that most decisions were taken by the sarpanch alone, without actually consulting the Gram Sabha.

Q1 (1M) What is a Gram Sabha?
Ans: All registered voters in a village who are on the electoral roll of that panchayat form the Gram Sabha — the general body of the panchayat system.
Q2 (2M) What challenge to democracy does the passage illustrate?
Ans: The passage shows a gap between formal democracy and substantive democracy. While elections happen (high turnout), real decision-making power remains with the sarpanch (individual), not with the Gram Sabha (collective). This is a challenge of concentration of power and weak decentralisation in practice.
Q3 (2M) Suggest two measures to strengthen grassroots democracy.
Ans: (1) Regular, mandatory Gram Sabha meetings with quorum requirements and recorded minutes — ensuring collective decision-making. (2) Social audit — independent committees including ordinary citizens examining panchayat expenditure records publicly, ensuring transparency and accountability.
SA (3M) Distinguish between the Union List and the State List in India's federal structure.
Ans: Union List (97 subjects): Subjects on which only Parliament can make laws — defence, foreign affairs, currency, banking, railways, atomic energy, communication. These require national uniformity. State List (66 subjects): Subjects on which only State legislatures can make laws — police, trade, commerce within state, agriculture, irrigation, public health. These vary by regional need. Concurrent List (47 subjects): Both Parliament and State legislatures can legislate — education, forests, marriage, adoption. In case of conflict, Parliament's law prevails. (Residual powers with Centre.)
LA (5M) What is a political party? Describe the main functions of political parties in a democracy.
Ans: A political party is an organised group of people who share similar political views and work together to contest elections and gain political power to implement their policies and programmes. Functions: (1) Contest elections: Parties select candidates, run campaigns, and seek votes. In India, parties choose candidates; voters choose parties. (2) Form government: The party winning majority forms the government; the second largest party forms the opposition — essential for accountability. (3) Make policies and programmes: Parties present their vision (manifesto) and implement policies if they come to power. Parties represent different social interests (farmers, labour, business). (4) Perform opposition: Opposition parties criticise government failures, present alternatives, question policies — keeping the government accountable. (5) Shape public opinion: Parties organise rallies, use media, communicate with citizens — shaping what people think about public issues. (6) Form links between government and citizens: Parties are the link between society and state — citizens approach parties to get their problems resolved by the government. Challenges: Lack of internal democracy, dynastic politics, money and criminal power in politics undermine these functions.
💰 Economics: Development and Sectors
Development · Sectors · Money & Finance · Globalisation · Consumer Rights

📖 Core Theory

Ch 1: Development

Development means different things to different people: farmers want better prices; urban unemployed want jobs. Development involves contradictory goals across groups. National Development: total income (GDP) is commonly used to compare countries but ignores distribution. HDI (Human Development Index) considers health (life expectancy), education (literacy, enrolment), and income.

Per Capita Income
Total income of country ÷ population. World Bank criterion: per capita income above $12,236 p.a. (2019) = rich; below $1,035 = low income. India is lower-middle income.
HDI
Human Development Index (UNDP): combines per capita income, health (life expectancy), education (literacy + enrolment). Better measure of development than GDP alone.
Sustainable Development
Development that meets present needs without compromising ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Balances economic growth, social equity, environmental protection.

Ch 2: Sectors of the Indian Economy

Three sectors: Primary (agriculture, mining, fishing — uses natural resources); Secondary (manufacturing, construction — transforms raw materials); Tertiary (services — banks, transport, communication, trade). GDP = sum of all final goods and services produced in a year within a country.

GDP
Gross Domestic Product: value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. Does not include intermediate goods (counted only in final product value).
Organised Sector
Jobs registered with government; workers get job security, provident fund, paid leave, ESI. Banks, government offices, large factories. Unorganised: small shops, farm labour — less protection.
NREGA 2005
National Rural Employment Guarantee Act: government must provide 100 days paid work per year to every rural household adult willing to do unskilled manual work.

Ch 3: Money and Credit

Barter had limitations (double coincidence of wants). Money as medium of exchange solves this. Functions of money: medium of exchange, store of value, unit of account, standard of deferred payment. Credit: can enable investment but create debt trap for small borrowers.

Double Coincidence
Barter requires both parties to want what the other has. Example: shoe-maker wants wheat, but wheat farmer must also want shoes. Money eliminates this requirement.
Collateral
Asset (land, property, deposits) pledged as guarantee for a loan. If borrower fails to repay, lender seizes collateral. Small farmers often lack collateral → depend on moneylenders.
Self-Help Groups
Groups of 15-20 rural poor (usually women). Save small amounts, pool savings, give internal credit. After regular savings, can borrow from banks. Grameen Bank model.

🔑 Key Facts — Economics

India's HDI rank: around 132/193 countries (medium human development)
Tertiary sector: contributes about 55% of India's GDP but employs fewer people than primary
Primary sector: employs about 50% of India's workforce — structural change needed
Consumer Rights: Right to Safety, Information, Choice, Heard, Redressal, Consumer Education

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1 — Sectors (Board 2022, 4M)

Q: Compare the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors by activity and example.

1
Primary: directly uses natural resources. Examples: farming, fishing, mining, forestry. About 50% of workforce.
2
Secondary: transforms raw material into products. Examples: cotton → cloth, iron → steel, food processing. Manufacturing.
3
Tertiary (Services): provides services to primary and secondary. Examples: banks, transport, IT, trade, education.
✅ Primary = natural resources; Secondary = manufacturing; Tertiary = services.

📝 Practice Questions — Economics

MCQ 1 Which sector of the economy directly uses natural resources?
(A) Secondary (B) Tertiary (C) Primary (D) Service
Ans: (C) Primary — agriculture, fishing, mining.
MCQ 2 The Bretton Woods institutions established after WWII include:
(A) WTO and IMF (B) IMF and World Bank (C) WTO and World Bank (D) UNCTAD and IMF
Ans: (B) IMF and World Bank
MCQ 3 MNC stands for:
(A) Major National Company (B) Multinational Corporation (C) Multi-National Commerce (D) Managed National Company
Ans: (B) Multinational Corporation
A-R 1 A: Globalisation has not benefited all sections of society equally.
R: While MNCs and skilled workers in export industries gained, small domestic producers (textiles, electronics, toys) lost to cheap imports; agricultural smallholders lost to subsidised imports from developed nations; unskilled workers faced job insecurity as companies relocated to cheaper labour markets. Benefits concentrated among educated, skilled, urban populations.
Ans: (A)

Case — Credit and Debt Trap: Swapna, a cotton farmer in Telangana, took a loan from a moneylender at 3% monthly interest to buy seeds and fertilisers. The harvest failed due to unseasonal rains. Unable to repay, the interest accumulated. She borrowed more to repay the old loan. Within three years, she owed three times her original loan.

Q1 (1M) Why did Swapna borrow from a moneylender instead of a bank?
Ans: Poor farmers often lack collateral (land documents, property) required by banks. Moneylenders provide credit without collateral — but at very high interest rates.
Q2 (2M) What is a 'debt trap'? How does it occur?
Ans: A debt trap is a cycle where a borrower takes new loans to repay old ones — unable to break free from debt. It occurs when: (1) interest rate is too high relative to income; (2) income falls (crop failure); (3) lender adds penalties for default. Swapna's case shows how compound interest rapidly multiplies the original debt.
Q3 (2M) Suggest two ways to provide better credit to farmers like Swapna.
Ans: (1) Self Help Groups (SHGs): Women pool small savings → borrow from the group at low rates → use for income-generating activities. SHGs can also get bank loans. (2) Priority sector lending: RBI mandates banks to give at least 40% of loans to priority sectors including agriculture — but banks need to simplify procedures and reduce collateral requirements for small farmers.
SA (3M) What is globalisation? Explain three factors that have enabled globalisation.
Ans: Globalisation is the process of rapid integration and interconnection of countries through trade, investment, movement of people, and technology — resulting in countries becoming deeply interdependent. Enabling factors: (1) Technology: Internet, mobile communication, faster transport (container shipping) drastically reduced costs of communication and trade. (2) Trade liberalisation: WTO and bilateral agreements reduced tariffs, import quotas and trade barriers — allowing goods and services to move freely across borders. (3) Foreign Investment: MNCs invest in production across countries seeking cheaper labour/resources — integrating national markets into a global production network.
LA (5M) Describe India's economic development since independence. What are the main challenges that remain?
Ans: Achievements: (1) GDP grew from ₹2.7 lakh cr (1950) to over ₹200 lakh cr. India is now the 5th largest economy. (2) Green Revolution (1960s-70s) made India food self-sufficient — end of famines. (3) Literacy rose from 18% (1951) to over 77% (2021). (4) Life expectancy rose from 32 to 70 years. (5) IT and services sector made India a global hub (Bengaluru, Hyderabad). (6) Poverty declined significantly — HDI improved. Challenges: (1) Inequality: Rich-poor gap widened despite growth; top 1% hold 40% of national wealth. (2) Unemployment: Educated youth unemployment especially high; informal sector employs 90% without social security. (3) Agriculture: 47% employed but only 17% of GDP; farmer distress, low productivity, water scarcity. (4) Infrastructure: Inadequate roads, power, healthcare — especially in rural areas. (5) Human Development: Despite growth, India ranks 132 on Human Development Index — poor health and education outcomes for the poor.

📜 PYQ Bank CBSE 2022–2024
2024 5 Marks Nationalism in India
Explain the role of the Non-Cooperation Movement in the growth of Indian nationalism.
1. Mass participation: For the first time, peasants, workers, students, women joined the freedom movement — not just elites.
2. Boycott of British goods: Swadeshi movement revived — foreign cloth burned, Indian goods promoted.
3. Surrender of titles and honours: Indian leaders returned British awards — symbol of rejection of colonial rule.
4. Hartals and strikes: Brought economic and administrative life to a standstill in many areas.
5. Unified diverse groups: Hindus, Muslims (Khilafat linked), different regions participated together — rare unity.

The movement transformed nationalism from an elite idea to a mass movement.
2023 4 Marks Agriculture
Distinguish between subsistence farming and commercial farming. Give two examples of commercial crops.
Subsistence Farming: Food grown for personal consumption, small plot, traditional methods, low surplus.
Commercial Farming: Crops grown for market sale, large scale, modern machinery, high capital input.

Commercial crops: Cotton, Sugarcane, Tea, Coffee, Jute (any two). These are sold in national or international markets for profit.

Key distinction: Purpose — personal use vs market sale.
2024 3 Marks Power Sharing
Explain the Belgian model of power sharing. Why is it considered a successful example?
Belgium has three linguistic communities: Dutch (Flemish, 59%), French (40%), German (1%).

Belgian model:
— Equal number of Dutch and French ministers in central cabinet
— Separate community governments for each linguistic group
— State governments for geographical regions (Brussels = special)
— Community governments have exclusive powers over cultural/educational matters

Success: Prevented linguistic civil war unlike Sri Lanka; accommodation over confrontation → peaceful coexistence.
2023 5 Marks Globalisation
How has globalisation impacted the lives of workers and farmers in developing countries? Evaluate both positive and negative effects.
Positive effects:
— MNCs provide employment in manufacturing sectors
— Access to better technology and inputs for farmers (Bt cotton)
— Export markets for agricultural produce
— Higher wages in export industries compared to local

Negative effects:
— MNCs prefer flexible/contract labour — workers lack job security
— Competition from cheap imports hurts small farmers
— Profits leave the country — limited local reinvestment
— Many small industries unable to compete — closures, unemployment

Globalisation has benefited the skilled but marginalised the unskilled and small producers.
⚠️ Mistake Bank Common Errors

Confusing Civil Disobedience with Non-Cooperation Movement

Students mix up two separate Gandhian movements.

❌ WRONG: "The Non-Cooperation Movement included the Salt March and breaking of salt laws."
✅ CORRECT: Salt March (1930) was part of the Civil Disobedience Movement. Non-Cooperation (1920–22) included boycott of schools/courts, surrendering titles, Khilafat support. They are separate movements with different methods.

Map Work — Confusing Rivers and Mountain Passes

Map questions lose marks due to incorrect location marking.

❌ WRONG: Marking the Nathula Pass in the Western Himalayas or confusing Godavari with Krishna river.
✅ CORRECT: Practice marking all rivers, passes, peaks, states from the NCERT maps. Nathula Pass = Sikkim (Eastern Himalayas). Godavari flows through Maharashtra, Telangana, AP. Revise map list — 4 marks are purely map-based.

Belgium vs Sri Lanka: Mixing Up Outcomes

Students attribute Sri Lanka's accommodation model to Belgium and vice versa.

❌ WRONG: "Belgium followed majoritarianism like Sri Lanka and this led to conflict."
✅ CORRECT: Belgium = power sharing (accommodation) → peaceful coexistence. Sri Lanka = Sinhala majoritarianism (1956 Official Language Act) → Tamil alienation → civil war. They are contrasting case studies.

Formal vs Informal Credit Sector

Students list money-lenders and chit funds in formal sector.

❌ WRONG: "Money-lenders and self-help groups are part of the formal credit sector."
✅ CORRECT: Formal sector = banks, cooperative societies, RBI-regulated institutions. Informal = money-lenders, traders, relatives, chit funds. Self-Help Groups are informal unless linked to banks for microcredit.

MNC Definition Error

Students think any foreign company is an MNC.

❌ WRONG: "An MNC is any company that sells its goods in another country."
✅ CORRECT: An MNC is a company that owns or controls production in more than one country through foreign investment (FDI). Exporting alone does not make a company an MNC. The key is production ownership/control abroad.

Soil Types — Wrong Region Association

Students assign black soil to wrong states.

❌ WRONG: "Black soil (Regur) is found mainly in Rajasthan and Punjab."
✅ CORRECT: Black soil = Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, parts of Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh (Deccan Plateau). Ideal for cotton. Rajasthan/Punjab have alluvial soil. Remember: Black soil → Deccan → Cotton.

HDI vs GDP Distinction

Students use GDP and HDI interchangeably for development.

❌ WRONG: "Kerala has a higher per capita income than Punjab, so it has a higher level of development."
✅ CORRECT: Development is multidimensional. Kerala has lower per capita income than Punjab but better HDI (higher literacy, infant mortality, life expectancy). Per capita income alone doesn't measure development — HDI considers health and education too.

Federalism — Writing that India is a Federation

India's exact federal nature is often misstated.

❌ WRONG: "India is a pure federal state with complete sovereignty for states."
✅ CORRECT: India is a "Union of States" (quasi-federal) — federal features + strong centre. States don't have the right to secede. Centre can make laws on Concurrent List, impose President's Rule, change state boundaries. This makes it federal with a centralising tendency.
🎯 Examiner's Eye Scoring Tips

🎯 Tip 1 — Map Work: 4 Marks Easy to Lose

Map work carries 4 marks in SST and many students score 0–2 due to poor practice. Mark all rivers, crops, minerals, historical sites from the NCERT list every day. Label neatly — unclear handwriting loses marks even when placement is correct.

🎯 Tip 2 — Source-Based Questions: Quote the Source

In source-based questions, always refer back to the given text. Write: "As the source states, '...' — this shows that..." Examiners check whether you've used the source. Answering without referencing it costs the comprehension mark.

🎯 Tip 3 — History: Always Mention the Year

In every historical event answer, mention the year. "In 1920, Gandhi launched..." is better than "Gandhi launched..." The year demonstrates factual precision, which is specifically rewarded in History answers. Missing a year in a 5-mark answer can cost half a mark.

🎯 Tip 4 — Economics: Always Define Before Explaining

For terms like GDP, HDI, Globalisation, Formal sector — write a one-line definition first. "GDP is the total monetary value of all goods and services produced within a country's borders in a year." Then explain the concept. Definition = 1 guaranteed mark.

🎯 Tip 5 — Civics: Use Specific Country Examples

Power Sharing, Federalism, and Democracy questions require country examples. "Belgium uses power sharing successfully" is weaker than "Belgium's constitutional amendment of 1970 created community governments..." Use specific facts — they demonstrate depth.

🎯 Tip 6 — Geography: Draw a Neat Sketch Map

Even when not asked, adding a small sketch map of a region (like India's monsoon pattern, or resource distribution) as a supporting illustration earns bonus impression marks with many examiners. Label axes and title it.

🎯 Tip 7 — Compare/Contrast: Always Use a Table

For "distinguish between" questions — formal vs informal sector, subsistence vs commercial farming, Unitary vs Federal — use a 3-column comparison table with 3–4 bases. This is faster to write and guarantees full marks for the distinguishing section.

🎯 Tip 8 — Long Answer: Begin with a Definition or Context

For 5-mark questions, the first sentence should set context. "Nationalism is the sense of shared identity and collective belonging that binds people of a nation." Starting with a definition or historical context signals a structured answer to the examiner and earns the opening mark.
