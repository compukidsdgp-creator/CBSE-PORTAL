🌿 Chapter 1: Reproduction in Organisms
Asexual · Sexual · Life Span · Vegetative Propagation

📖 Core Theory

1.1 Types of Reproduction

Reproduction ensures continuity of species. Two fundamental modes exist.

Asexual Reproduction
Single parent, no gametes. Offspring genetically identical (clones). Binary fission (Amoeba), budding (Hydra, yeast), spore formation (Rhizopus), fragmentation (Spirogyra), vegetative propagation (plants).
Sexual Reproduction
Two parents, gamete fusion. Genetic variation. All sexually reproducing organisms have gametogenesis → gamete transfer → fertilisation → development.
Life Span
Duration from birth to natural death. Rose: few months; Banyan: hundreds of years; Parrot: 150 yr; Crow: 15 yr; Butterfly: few weeks; Tortoise: 100+ yr. NOT related to size.
Vegetative Propagation
Runner (grass), rhizome (ginger), tuber (potato), bulb (onion), leaf buds (Bryophyllum). Produces plants with characters of parent; rapid, no seeds needed.

1.2 Events in Sexual Reproduction

🔑 Phases of Sexual Reproduction

Pre-fertilisation: gametogenesis (gamete formation) + gamete transfer
Fertilisation: syngamy (fusion of gametes) → zygote
Post-fertilisation: embryogenesis (zygote → embryo → offspring)
Parthenogenesis: development without fertilisation (bees, some lizards, Apis — drones from unfertilised eggs)
Oestrus cycle (non-primate mammals) vs menstrual cycle (primates — including humans)

🌿 Chapter 2: Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants
Microsporogenesis · Megasporogenesis · Pollination · Double Fertilisation

📖 Core Theory

2.1 Flower Structure and Development

Flower is the site of sexual reproduction in angiosperms. Stamen (male: anther + filament) and carpel/pistil (female: stigma + style + ovary) are essential organs.

Microsporogenesis
Formation of microspores (pollen) in anther. Microsporocyte (2n) → meiosis → 4 microspores (n) = pollen tetrad. Tapetum nourishes developing pollen. Pollen grain has 2 layers: exine (sporopollenin) and intine (cellulose-pectin).
Megasporogenesis
Formation of megaspore in ovule. MMC (2n) → meiosis → 4 megaspores; only 1 functional (Polygonum type = most common). Functional megaspore → 3 mitotic divisions → 8-nucleate embryo sac.
Embryo Sac (7-celled, 8-nucleate)
Egg cell (1) + synergids (2) at micropylar end; antipodal cells (3) at chalazal end; central cell with 2 polar nuclei (1 cell). Egg + 2 synergids = egg apparatus.
Double Fertilisation
Unique to angiosperms. One male gamete + egg cell → zygote (2n). Second male gamete + 2 polar nuclei → primary endosperm nucleus (3n, PEN). PEN → endosperm (food for embryo).

🔑 Pollination and Outbreeding Devices

Self-pollination (autogamy): pollen from same flower — e.g. wheat, rice, pea
Cross-pollination (allogamy): pollen from different plant — promotes variation
Agents: wind (anemophily), water (hydrophily), insects (entomophily), birds (ornithophily), bats (chiropterophily)
Outbreeding devices: dicliny, dichogamy, self-incompatibility, heterostyly
Apomixis: seed formation without fertilisation — dandelion, grasses; polyembryony: multiple embryos in one seed

🌿 Chapter 3: Human Reproduction
Gametogenesis · Menstrual Cycle · Fertilisation · Implantation · Parturition

📖 Core Theory

3.1 Male Reproductive System

Testes (in scrotum at 2–2.5°C below body temp for normal spermatogenesis) produce sperms and testosterone. Accessory glands: seminal vesicles, prostate, Cowper's gland — produce seminal plasma.

Spermatogenesis
Spermatogonia (2n) → Primary spermatocyte → Meiosis I → Secondary spermatocytes (2) → Meiosis II → Spermatids (4) → Spermiogenesis → Spermatozoa (4 haploid). Takes ~74 days.
Sperm Structure
Head (acrosome + condensed nucleus), middle piece (mitochondria — energy for motility), tail (flagellum). Acrosome contains hydrolytic enzymes for zona pellucida penetration.
Oogenesis
Oogonia (2n) → Primary oocyte → Meiosis I (arrested at birth in prophase I) → Secondary oocyte + 1st polar body → Meiosis II (arrested at metaphase II, completed only after fertilisation) → Ovum + 2nd polar body.
Menstrual Cycle
~28 days. Menstrual phase (1–5, lining shed), Follicular/Proliferative (6–13, FSH → Graafian follicle, estrogen surge), Ovulation (day 14, LH surge), Luteal (15–28, LH → corpus luteum → progesterone).

🔑 Fertilisation and Development

Fertilisation: ampullary region of fallopian tube. Acrosomal reaction → sperm penetrates zona pellucida
Cortical reaction: prevents polyspermy (blocks zona pellucida to other sperms after entry of one)
Cleavage: mitotic divisions of zygote → 2, 4, 8, 16 cells (morula) → blastocyst
Implantation: blastocyst embeds in endometrium (day 6–10 after fertilisation) — trophoblast helps
Parturition: triggered by oxytocin (positive feedback loop); uterine contractions → delivery (~38 weeks)

Oogenesis vs Spermatogenesis (Board 2024, 3M)

4

5

🌿 Chapter 4: Reproductive Health
Problems · STIs · Contraception · ART · MTP

📖 Core Theory

4.1 Reproductive Health and STIs

Reproductive health (WHO) = total well-being in all aspects of reproduction — physical, emotional, social. India: first country to launch National Family Planning Programme (1951).

STIs (Sexually Transmitted Infections)
HIV/AIDS (retrovirus), gonorrhoea (Neisseria), syphilis (Treponema), genital herpes (HSV), hepatitis-B (HBV), chlamydiasis, genital warts (HPV). Transmitted via sex, blood, mother → foetus. HIV destroys T-helper cells.
Contraceptive Methods
Natural (periodic abstinence, coitus interruptus, lactational amenorrhoea <6 months postpartum). Barrier (condoms, diaphragm, cervical caps). IUDs (CuT, LNG-20). Oral pills (combined estrogen+progestin). Surgical (vasectomy, tubectomy).
Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)
Legal in India up to 20 weeks (MTP Act 1971, amended 2021 — up to 24 weeks in special cases). Used for unwanted pregnancies or foetal anomalies. NOT a contraceptive method.
Assisted Reproductive Technologies (ART)
IVF-ET (test tube baby — Edwards, Steptoe, 1978; first: Louise Brown). GIFT (gamete intrafallopian transfer). ZIFT (zygote intrafallopian transfer). AI (artificial insemination). ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection).

🔑 IUDs and Hormonal Contraception

Non-medicated IUDs: Lippes loop — increase phagocytosis of sperm; interfere with uterine motility
Cu-releasing IUDs (CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375): Cu ions suppress sperm motility; fertilisation prevented
Hormone-releasing IUDs (LNG-20, Progestasert): thicken cervical mucus; prevent implantation
Emergency contraceptives: high-dose progestin within 72 hours → prevent/delay ovulation or implantation
Amniocentesis: foetal chromosomal analysis (fluid from amniotic sac) — for genetic disorders, NOT sex determination (illegal)

🌿 Chapter 5: Principles of Inheritance and Variation
Mendelism · Chromosomal Theory · Linkage · Sex Determination · Mutations

📖 Core Theory

5.1 Mendel's Laws

Gregor Mendel (1865) worked on garden pea (Pisum sativum) — 7 pairs of contrasting traits, each controlled by a single gene with 2 alleles (dominant/recessive).

Law of Dominance
In a heterozygous individual, only dominant allele is expressed in F1 (phenotype). Recessive allele remains latent but present in genotype.
Law of Segregation
During gamete formation, alleles of a gene separate so each gamete has one allele. Explains 3:1 ratio in F2 of monohybrid cross.
Law of Independent Assortment
Genes for different traits assort independently (if on non-homologous chromosomes). Explains 9:3:3:1 ratio in F2 of dihybrid cross. Does NOT apply to linked genes.
Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance
Sutton and Boveri (1902): genes located on chromosomes. Experimental proof: Morgan's fruit fly (Drosophila) linkage groups correspond to chromosome number.

🔑 Extensions of Mendelism

Incomplete dominance: F1 intermediate (RR×rr → Rr pink); F2 = 1:2:1 (red:pink:white)
Co-dominance: both alleles expressed (AB blood type — IAIA × IBIB → IAIB)
Polygenic inheritance: multiple genes control one trait — skin colour, height (continuous variation)
Pleiotropy: one gene affects multiple traits — sickle cell anaemia (HbS gene)
Sex-linked inheritance: haemophilia (X-linked recessive); females: carrier (XHXh) or affected (XhXh); all sons of carrier mother have 50% chance

🌿 Chapter 6: Molecular Basis of Inheritance
DNA Structure · Replication · Transcription · Translation · Regulation

📖 Core Theory

6.1 DNA — Structure and Packaging

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the genetic material in most organisms. Proof: Griffith's transformation experiment → Avery, MacLeod, McCarty (1944) showed DNA is transforming principle; Hershey-Chase experiment (1952) with radioactive S³⁵ (protein) and P³² (DNA) confirmed DNA is genetic material.

DNA Double Helix (Watson-Crick, 1953)
Two antiparallel polynucleotide strands. Bases pair: A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C (3 H-bonds). Pitch = 3.4 nm; 10 bp/turn; diameter 2 nm. Sugar-phosphate backbone on outside; bases inside.
DNA Packaging
Nucleosome: 200 bp DNA wound around histone octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, H4 × 2; H1 linker). Nucleosomes → 30 nm fibre → loops → chromosomes. Human genome: 3.1 billion bp; ~30,000 genes.
Replication
Semi-conservative (Meselson-Stahl, 1958, E. coli in ¹⁵N→¹⁴N medium). At origin: helicase unwinds, primase makes RNA primer, DNA Pol III elongates (5'→3'), DNA Pol I removes primer, ligase joins fragments. Leading strand continuous; lagging strand: Okazaki fragments.
Central Dogma
DNA → (Transcription) → RNA → (Translation) → Protein. Reverse transcription (retroviruses: RNA → DNA) is an exception (Temin).

🔑 Transcription and Translation

Transcription: template strand (3'→5') → mRNA (5'→3'); RNA Pol II in eukaryotes; promoter has TATA box
mRNA processing (eukaryotes): 5' capping, 3' poly-A tail, splicing (introns removed, exons joined)
Genetic code: 64 codons; 61 code amino acids; 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA); start = AUG (Met)
Code is degenerate (1 AA ≥ 2 codons), non-overlapping, non-ambiguous, comma-less, universal
Jacob-Monod lac operon: structural genes (lacZ, lacY, lacA) + operator + promoter; regulated by repressor (lacI gene)

Q: In eukaryotes, hnRNA is produced in the nucleus but mRNA reaches the ribosome. What processing converts hnRNA to mRNA?

▶ Show Answer

🌿 Chapter 7: Evolution
Origin of Life · Darwinism · Evidence · Human Evolution

📖 Core Theory

7.1 Origin of Life

Earth formed ~4.5 billion years ago. First life ~3.8 billion years ago (prokaryotes). Oparin-Haldane hypothesis: chemical evolution — simple molecules → organic molecules → proto-cells.

Urey-Miller Experiment (1953)
Simulated primitive Earth atmosphere (CH₄, H₂, NH₃, H₂O, no O₂) with lightning (sparks). Produced amino acids, sugars, urea etc. Proved abiotic synthesis of organic molecules possible.
Darwinian Theory
Natural selection: organisms with favourable variations survive and reproduce more (survival of the fittest). Variations arise randomly (not directed). Evolution = gradual accumulation of variations over generations.
Evidence for Evolution
Homologous organs (same origin, different function — forelimbs of whale, bat, human, horse — common ancestry), Analogous organs (different origin, similar function — wings of birds and insects — convergent evolution), Fossils, Biochemical similarity (cytochrome c sequence).
Hardy-Weinberg Principle
In a large, randomly mating population with no mutation/selection/migration — allele frequencies remain constant. p+q=1; p²+2pq+q²=1. Deviations = evolution occurring.

🔑 Mechanisms of Evolution

Genetic drift: random change in allele frequency in small population (founder effect, bottleneck effect)
Gene flow: transfer of alleles between populations via migration — reduces differentiation
Mutation: random change in DNA → new alleles; ultimate source of variation
Industrial melanism (pepper moth): dark Biston betularia survived better on soot-darkened trees (natural selection)
Drug resistance: mutation + antibiotic pressure → resistant strains selected (not Lamarckian)

🌿 Chapter 8: Human Health and Disease
Pathogens · Immunity · AIDS · Cancer · Drugs

📖 Core Theory

8.1 Innate and Adaptive Immunity

Two types of immunity: Innate (non-specific) — present from birth, first line of defense (skin, mucus, phagocytes, natural killer cells, inflammation, fever) and Adaptive (acquired/specific) — develops after exposure to antigen; has memory.

Humoral Immunity (B-cells)
B lymphocytes produce antibodies (immunoglobulins). 5 classes: IgG, IgA, IgM, IgD, IgE. Structure: 2 heavy + 2 light chains (Y-shape). Fab region binds antigen. Fc region determines function.
Cell-mediated Immunity (T-cells)
T helper (CD4⁺): coordinate immune response; targets of HIV. T cytotoxic (CD8⁺): kill virus-infected/tumour cells. T regulatory: suppress immune response (prevent autoimmunity). Memory T-cells: rapid response on re-exposure.
Active vs Passive Immunity
Active: body makes own antibodies (natural infection or vaccination); slow but long-lasting (memory). Passive: readymade antibodies given (antitoxin, colostrum, Ig injection); immediate but no memory.
Allergies
Exaggerated response to non-harmful antigens (allergens). IgE antibodies + mast cells release histamine → sneezing, watery eyes, rash, asthma. Anaphylaxis = severe allergic reaction. Antihistamines block H₁ receptors.

🔑 HIV/AIDS, Cancer and Drugs

HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus): retrovirus (RNA genome + reverse transcriptase). Infects CD4⁺ T-cells → immune collapse → AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome).
ELISA: Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay — used to confirm HIV infection (detects antibodies)
Cancer: uncontrolled cell division due to proto-oncogene → oncogene activation or tumour suppressor gene loss
Carcinogens: chemical (tobacco), physical (UV, X-rays, gamma), biological (viruses — HPV, EBV)
Opioids: morphine, heroin bind opioid receptors → euphoria + addiction; depresses CNS; withdrawal painful

🌿 Chapter 9: Strategies for Enhancement in Food Production
Plant Breeding · Animal Husbandry · SCP · Tissue Culture · Hybridisation

📖 Core Theory

9.1 Plant Breeding and Green Revolution

Scientific plant breeding = produce improved varieties with higher yield, disease resistance, better nutrition. Steps: germplasm collection → evaluation → selection → hybridisation → evaluation → release as variety.

Green Revolution
Semi-dwarf wheat varieties (Norman Borlaug, Mexico) introduced to India in 1960s by M.S. Swaminathan. Semi-dwarf rice (IR-8, Philippines). Result: India's food production tripled by 1980s. Key gene: Norin10 (dwarf gene in wheat).
Biofortification
Breeding for improved nutritional content: Atlas 66 wheat (high protein+lysine), Maize hybrids (lysine+tryptophan), Golden Rice (β-carotene via psy + crt1 genes), IARI spinach, bitter gourd, carrot (iron, vitamins).
Tissue Culture / Micropropagation
Totipotency: each plant cell can regenerate whole plant. Explant (shoot tip/axillary bud/root tip) → callus (unorganised mass on agar) → shoot/root initiation → plantlet → field transfer. Meristem culture: virus-free plants. Somaclonal variation possible.
Somatic Hybridisation
Protoplasts (cell walls removed by cellulase + pectinase) of two species fused by PEG or electrofusion → somatic hybrid. Example: Pomato (potato + tomato). Overcomes sexual incompatibility barriers.

9.2 Animal Husbandry and SCP

Animal Husbandry
Scientific management of farm animals. Inbreeding (same breed, 4-6 gen): fixes desirable traits, increases homozygosity; exposes recessive defects. Outbreeding (different breeds/species): hybrid vigour (heterosis) — greater milk yield, disease resistance.
Single Cell Protein (SCP)
Microbial biomass as protein source. Spirulina (60-70% protein; on sewage, waste water), Methylophilus methylotrophus (on methanol; ICI UK). Reduces land/water needed vs conventional protein. Addresses protein hunger.

🔑 Plant Breeding for Resistance

Disease resistance: Himgiri wheat (rust-resistant); TN1, Pankaj rice (bacterial blight, brown planthopper resistant)
MBER (Multiple Biotic and Abiotic Stress Resistance) varieties developed by IARI
Mutation breeding: induce mutations by X-rays, UV, mutagens → screen for desirable traits
Polyploidy induction (colchicine blocks spindle → allopolyploids); triticale (wheat × rye — first man-made crop)

10.1 Microbes in Food and Industry

Microbes serve crucial roles in food, medicine, agriculture and environment. Many industrial processes depend on microbial metabolism.

Fermented Foods
Bread: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (CO₂ leavens). Curd/yoghurt: Lactobacillus (lactic acid). Dosa/Idli: Leuconostoc mesenteroides (ferments rice+urad). Cheese: Penicillium roquefortii (Roquefort blue veins); Propionibacterium shermanii (Swiss cheese holes + flavour).

10.2 Biogas, Biofertilisers and Biocontrol

Biogas
Methanogens (Methanobacterium) anaerobically digest cattle dung/organic waste → CH₄ (60-70%) + CO₂ + H₂S. KVIC (floating drum) and Janata (fixed dome) designs. Slurry = excellent fertiliser. Used for cooking+lighting in rural India.
Biofertilisers

Biological Control
Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): produces Cry proteins → kill insect larvae. Nucleopolyhedrovirus (NPV): baculovirus specific to insects, harmless to others — narrow spectrum, eco-friendly. Trichoderma: biocontrol agent against plant pathogens. Predators: Ladybird beetle (Coccinella) eats aphids.

🔑 BOD and Sewage

BOD = Biochemical Oxygen Demand = O₂ consumed by bacteria to decompose organics in 5 days at 20°C
High BOD = high pollution; treated sewage should have BOD <20 mg/L before discharge
Flocs in secondary treatment = masses of bacteria + fungal filaments forming mesh that traps organic matter
Anaerobic sludge digestors: bacteria digest sludge → biogas (CH₄+CO₂); methanogens are strict anaerobes

🌿 Chapter 11: Biotechnology — Principles and Processes
rDNA · Restriction Enzymes · Vectors · PCR · Gel Electrophoresis · Transformation

📖 Core Theory

11.1 Tools of Genetic Engineering

Genetic engineering: deliberate manipulation of DNA to alter the phenotype of an organism using recombinant DNA technology. Three core requirements: (1) restriction enzymes to cut DNA, (2) vector to carry DNA, (3) host organism to express foreign gene.

Restriction Endonucleases
Molecular scissors — cut DNA at specific palindromic sequences. EcoRI: G↓AATTC (recognises 6-bp palindrome); creates 4-base sticky ends. BamHI: G↓GATCC; HindIII: A↓AGCTT. Same enzyme cuts vector + insert → complementary sticky ends → ligation by DNA ligase (molecular glue).
Cloning Vectors
Vehicle to carry insert into host cell. Plasmids (pBR322, pUC19): small circular self-replicating DNA; must have ori (origin of replication), selectable marker (ampR, tetR), MCS (multiple cloning site). Bacteriophage (λ, M13), cosmids, BAC/YAC for large inserts (chromosomes).
PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)
Amplify specific DNA in vitro. Kary Mullis (Nobel 1993). 3 steps per cycle: (1) Denaturation 94°C (strands separate), (2) Annealing ~55°C (primers bind), (3) Extension 72°C (Taq polymerase extends 5′→3′). After n cycles: 2ⁿ copies. Taq from Thermus aquaticus — heat-stable.
Gel Electrophoresis
Separate DNA/RNA/protein by size. Agarose gel; negative charge → DNA migrates toward anode. Smaller fragments travel farther. EtBr stain — orange bands under UV. Used to verify restriction digest fragment sizes. Southern blotting (DNA), Northern (RNA), Western (protein).

11.2 Gene Transfer and Selectable Markers

Transformation
Introduction of foreign DNA into host cell. Chemical method: CaCl₂ treatment makes bacteria competent (porous). Electroporation: electric pulse opens pores. Biolistics (gene gun): DNA-coated gold/tungsten particles fired into plant/animal cells. Microinjection: DNA injected into animal cell nucleus.
pBR322 Selectable Markers
ampR (ampicillin resistance) and tetR (tetracycline resistance). Recombinant selection: insert goes into tetR gene (insertional inactivation) → recombinants: ampR but tetR-sensitive. Non-recombinants: ampR AND tetR-resistant. Replica plating on both antibiotics identifies recombinants.

🔑 Key Definitions and Calculations

Palindromic sequence: reads same on both strands 5′→3′; e.g. 5′-GAATTC-3′ / 3′-CTTAAG-5′
Sticky ends: short single-stranded overhangs after restriction cut; allow annealing with complementary sticky ends
PCR product after 30 cycles: 2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹ copies of target sequence
Competent cells: cells able to take up foreign DNA (treated with CaCl₂ or by electroporation)
Ti plasmid (Agrobacterium tumefaciens): natural vector for plant transformation; T-DNA integrates into plant genome

🌿 Chapter 12: Biotechnology and Its Applications
GM Crops · Bt Cotton · Golden Rice · Transgenic Animals · Insulin · Gene Therapy · Biopiracy

📖 Core Theory

12.1 GM Organisms in Agriculture

Genetically Modified (GM) organisms contain an alien gene introduced by rDNA technology. Applications in agriculture: pest resistance, herbicide tolerance, improved nutrition, stress tolerance, delayed ripening.

Bt Cotton (Bollgard)
Cry1Ac gene from Bacillus thuringiensis inserted into cotton. Cry proteins (crystal proteins) = inactive protoxin → activated in alkaline insect gut (pH 10) → forms pores in gut epithelial cells → insect dies. Specific to Lepidoptera (cotton bollworm Helicoverpa). Not harmful to humans (acid stomach, no receptor).
Golden Rice
β-carotene (provitamin A) produced in rice endosperm (normally white/β-carotene absent). Genes introduced: psy (phytoene synthase from Narcissus daffodil) + crtI (lycopene desaturase from Erwinia uredovora). Yellow colour from β-carotene. Addresses vitamin A deficiency (leading cause of childhood blindness in developing countries).
Recombinant Insulin (Humulin)
Human insulin gene (A-chain + B-chain separately) cloned into E. coli (Eli Lilly, 1982). Proinsulin produced → C-peptide removed → A + B chains joined → active insulin. Replaced pig/cattle insulin (allergy, BSE risk). First approved GM pharmaceutical product.
Transgenic Animals
Rosie (transgenic cow, 1997): human alpha-lactalbumin in milk — more nutritious for human infants. Transgenic sheep: human protein alpha-1-antitrypsin in milk (for emphysema). Gene pharming (molecular farming): use transgenic animals to produce therapeutic proteins.

12.2 Gene Therapy and Bioethics

Gene Therapy
Introduction of functional gene into patient's cells to correct genetic defect. ADA (adenosine deaminase) deficiency — first gene therapy case (1990, Ashanti DeSilva, USA). Retroviral vectors. Somatic gene therapy (body cells — not heritable) vs germline (reproductive cells — heritable; major ethical concerns).
Biopiracy
Use of bioresources (plants, microbes, traditional knowledge) by multinational companies without proper compensation to originating community/country. Examples: Turmeric (wound healing patent granted to University of Mississippi → challenged and revoked by India); Basmati (US company patented certain varieties). Addressed by CBD (Convention on Biological Diversity) and TRIPS.

🔑 Applications Summary

Bt toxin: different Cry proteins for different pests — Cry1 (Lepidoptera), Cry2 (Lepidoptera+Diptera), Cry3 (Coleoptera)
RNAi (RNA interference): dsRNA → silences specific mRNA → prevents nematode infection of tobacco roots
Flavr Savr tomato: antisense RNA blocks polygalacturonase → delayed ripening; first GM food crop (1994, USA)
ADA gene therapy: lymphocytes extracted → retroviral vector with ADA gene → transformed cells reinfused; requires repeat treatments
Ethical issues: GM crops → ecological impact (gene escape to wild relatives), allergenicity, resistance development

🌿 Chapter 13: Organisms and Populations
Habitat · Population Attributes · Growth Models · Interspecific Interactions

📖 Core Theory

13.1 Ecology — Levels and Adaptations

Ecology = scientific study of the relationships of organisms with each other and their environment. Levels: organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere.

Adaptations
Morphological (cactus spines, camel hump), Physiological (kangaroo rat — metabolic water, no need to drink; desert animals concentrate urine), Behavioural (migration, hibernation, aestivation).
Population Attributes
Birth rate (natality), Death rate (mortality), Sex ratio, Age pyramid (expanding/stable/declining), Density. Population size (N) changes by: N = N₀ + (B−D) + (I−E) where B=births, D=deaths, I=immigration, E=emigration.
Logistic Growth
Most populations in nature show S-shaped (sigmoid) growth. dN/dt = rN[(K−N)/K]. K = carrying capacity. At N=K, growth rate=0. Lag phase → exponential → deceleration → plateau at K.
Interspecific Interactions
Mutualism (+/+): lichens (fungus+alga), mycorrhiza; Commensalism (+/0): orchid on tree; Parasitism (+/−): roundworm in intestine; Predation (+/−): leopard-deer; Competition (−/−): Gause's competitive exclusion; Amensalism (0/−): Penicillium secretes penicillin → kills bacteria.

✏️ Worked Examples

Logistic Growth (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Write the equation for logistic growth. Explain each term and draw the S-shaped curve.

1
dN/dt = rN[(K−N)/K]
2
N = current population size; t = time; r = intrinsic rate of natural increase (birth rate − death rate)
3
K = carrying capacity (maximum population that habitat can support)
4
(K−N)/K = unused portion of carrying capacity; → 0 as N approaches K
5
When N is small: (K−N)/K ≈ 1 → growth is nearly exponential (J-shaped)
6
As N → K: growth slows → S-shaped sigmoid curve; at N=K, dN/dt = 0 (population stable)
✅ dN/dt = rN[(K−N)/K]; S-curve: lag → log → deceleration → plateau at K

Gause's Principle (Board 2022, 2M)

Q: State competitive exclusion principle. Give an example.

1
Gause's competitive exclusion principle: two species competing for identical resources CANNOT coexist indefinitely
2
The competitively superior species will eventually exclude the other
3
Example: Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum when cultured together in same medium → P. aurelia (more efficient) wins; P. caudatum eliminated
4
Coexistence possible only through niche differentiation (different resources, times or spaces used)
5
MacArthur's warblers: 5 species coexist in spruce tree by using different parts of canopy
✅ Two species with identical niches cannot coexist; P. aurelia excludes P. caudatum

🎯 Quick Activity

Think and Answer

Q: What is the advantage of lichens being "composite organisms" (algae + fungi)?

▶ Show Answer
A: The fungal partner provides: physical structure, anchorage, moisture retention, mineral absorption from substrate. The algal (or cyanobacterial) partner provides: photosynthetic energy (carbohydrates). This mutualism allows lichens to colonise bare rocks, extreme temperatures and deserts where neither organism could survive alone. Lichens are pioneer species — their organic acids break down rock, beginning soil formation (ecological succession).
🌿 Chapter 14: Ecosystem
Structure · Energy Flow · Nutrient Cycling · Productivity · Succession

📖 Core Theory

14.1 Ecosystem Structure

An ecosystem is a functional unit where biotic and abiotic components interact through nutrient cycling and energy flow. Components: producers, consumers, decomposers + abiotic factors.

Energy Flow (10% Law)
Only ~10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to next (Lindemann 1942). Rest lost as heat (respiration, excretion). 3-4 trophic levels maximum in most ecosystems — beyond that, too little energy remains.
Productivity
GPP (Gross Primary Productivity) = total photosynthesis. NPP = GPP − respiration loss. Net Primary Production available to consumers. Tropical rain forests and estuaries = highest NPP; deserts and open ocean = lowest.
Nutrient Cycling
Gaseous cycles (C, N, O): reservoir = atmosphere/hydrosphere. Sedimentary cycles (P, S): reservoir = earth crust. Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) essential — release minerals from organic matter.
Ecological Succession
Directional change in community over time. Pioneer community → seral stages → climax community (stable). Primary succession: starts on bare rock/sand (no soil). Secondary succession: starts after disturbance (fire, flood) — faster. Hydrarch (wet → dry); Xerarch (dry → wet).

✏️ Worked Examples

Energy Pyramid (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Why is an energy pyramid always upright? Can it ever be inverted?

1
Energy pyramids represent energy at each trophic level; always taper toward apex (upright)
2
Energy lost (~90%) at each trophic level as heat → higher level always has less energy
3
Energy CANNOT flow backwards — entropy (2nd law of thermodynamics)
4
Energy pyramids are ALWAYS upright (never inverted)
5
Biomass pyramids can be inverted (phytoplankton biomass < zooplankton at any instant in aquatic systems)
6
Number pyramids can be inverted (one oak tree supports thousands of insects)
✅ Energy pyramids always upright (energy lost as heat at each level; entropy); biomass pyramids can invert

Carbon Cycle (Board 2022, 3M)

Q: Describe the main processes in the carbon cycle.

1
Carbon source: CO₂ in atmosphere/hydrosphere
2
Fixation (removal): photosynthesis (autotrophs fix CO₂ → organic matter)
3
Return: respiration (all organisms → CO₂); decomposition (decomposers → CO₂, CH₄)
4
Combustion of fossil fuels → CO₂ (major human impact, greenhouse effect)
5
Oceans: dissolve CO₂ → carbonate/bicarbonate; marine organisms → shells (CaCO₃) → limestone
6
Geological return: volcanic activity releases CO₂ from carbonate rocks over millions of years
✅ Photosynthesis = C fixation; respiration/decomposition/combustion = C release; oceans = major C reservoir

🎯 Quick Activity

Think and Answer

Q: Decomposers are sometimes called "reducers." What would happen to an ecosystem if all decomposers suddenly disappeared?

▶ Show Answer
A: Decomposers break down dead organic matter → release inorganic minerals (N, P, K, Ca) back into soil and water. Without decomposers: (1) Dead organic matter accumulates indefinitely; (2) Nutrients locked in organic form → not available to producers; (3) Mineral nutrient pools depleted → plant growth stops → ecosystem collapses. Decomposers are as essential as producers — ecosystems cannot function without nutrient cycling.
🌿 Chapter 15: Biodiversity and Conservation
Biodiversity Types · Threats · Conservation · Hotspots · IUCN Categories

📖 Core Theory

15.1 Types and Patterns of Biodiversity

Biodiversity (E.O. Wilson popularised term): variety of life at genetic, species and ecosystem levels. Current estimates: 1.5 million described species; true number 5-50 million.

Genetic Diversity
Variation in DNA among individuals of same species. High genetic diversity = better adaptability. Sahyadri range rice = 50,000+ varieties; India = >1000 mango varieties. Allelic diversity.
Species Diversity
Variety of species in an area. Measured by species richness (number) and species evenness (relative abundance). Tropics (equatorial) more species-rich than temperate/polar (latitudinal gradient in biodiversity).
Ecosystem Diversity
Variety of ecosystems in region. India: deserts, rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, grasslands — all within one country.
Biodiversity Hotspots
Norman Myers (1988). Regions with exceptionally high species richness AND high endemism AND severe threats. 34 hotspots globally, cover <2.5% Earth land but hold >50% terrestrial species. India has 4: Western Ghats & Sri Lanka, Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Sundaland.

🔑 Threats (HIPPO) and Conservation

H = Habitat loss/fragmentation (biggest threat); I = Invasive alien species; P = Pollution; P = Population explosion (human); O = Overexploitation
In-situ conservation: protect in natural habitat — biosphere reserves, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, sacred groves
Ex-situ conservation: protect outside habitat — zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, cryopreservation, tissue culture
IUCN Red List: Extinct, Extinct in Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern
India protected areas: 103 national parks, 544 wildlife sanctuaries, 18 biosphere reserves (10 in UNESCO MAB network)

Criterion 1: Must contain at least 1500 species of vascular plants as endemic species (endemism)
2
Criterion 2: Must have lost at least 70% of its original habitat (HIGH threat level, already mostly lost)
3
Both criteria must be met simultaneously

2

🎯 Quick Activity

Think and Answer

Q: Alexander von Humboldt observed that within a region, species richness increases with area. Express this as an equation and interpret each term.

▶ Show Answer
A: Species-Area relationship (Arrhenius, 1921): S = C × Aᶻ, or log S = log C + z log A. S = species richness; A = area; C = Y-intercept (related to taxa density); z = slope (0.1-0.2 for small islands, 0.6-1.2 for continental areas). Larger area → more resources → more habitats → more species. This is why habitat fragmentation (converting large forests into isolated patches) causes species extinction even if TOTAL area is same — edges, isolation effects reduce effective area per patch.
🌿 Chapter 16: Environmental Issues
Pollution · Greenhouse Effect · Ozone · Deforestation · Solid Waste

📖 Core Theory

16.1 Air Pollution

Air pollution = addition of contaminants to atmosphere beyond threshold. Major pollutants: CO, SOₓ, NOₓ, particulates, hydrocarbons, lead, CFCs.

Greenhouse Effect and Global Warming
CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, CFCs, water vapour = greenhouse gases. Trap IR radiation from Earth's surface → warming. CO₂ increased 270 ppm (pre-industrial) → 420+ ppm now. Predicted effects: melting ice caps, sea level rise, extreme weather, coral bleaching, species shifts.
Ozone Layer Depletion
Stratospheric ozone (15-35 km altitude) absorbs UV-B and UV-C. CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons — Freons in refrigerants, aerosols) release Cl· radicals which destroy O₃ catalytically. Antarctic ozone hole — CFC accumulation in polar vortex. Montreal Protocol (1987) — phase-out of CFCs.
Water Pollution
BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand): O₂ consumed by bacteria to decompose organic waste in 5 days at 20°C. High BOD = more pollution. Eutrophication: nutrient enrichment → algal bloom → O₂ depletion → fish death. Biomagnification: DDT concentrates up food chain.
Solid Waste
MSW (municipal solid waste): domestic, commercial, construction waste. Categories: recyclable, biodegradable, hazardous, electronic waste (e-waste). Sanitary landfill, composting, recycling, incineration (energy recovery). E-waste: improper disposal → heavy metal leaching (Pb, Hg, Cd).

✏️ Worked Examples

Ozone Depletion (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Explain how CFCs deplete the ozone layer.

1
CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) used in refrigerators, AC, aerosols are chemically inert at surface
2
Rise to stratosphere where UV radiation cleaves C-Cl bond: CF₂Cl₂ → CF₂Cl· + Cl·
3
Cl· attacks ozone: Cl· + O₃ → ClO· + O₂
4
ClO· reacts with another O₃: ClO· + O₃ → Cl· + 2O₂
5
Cl· is regenerated → catalytic cycle; ONE Cl· can destroy 100,000 ozone molecules
6
Result: ozone hole (especially over Antarctica) → increased UV-B reaching Earth → skin cancer, cataracts, crop damage
✅ CFCs → Cl· radicals (UV) → catalytic O₃ destruction; each Cl· destroys 100,000 O₃ molecules

Biomagnification (Board 2022, 2M)

Q: Explain biomagnification of DDT in an aquatic food chain.

1
DDT (dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane): persistent organic pollutant; fat-soluble, NOT excreted
2
Phytoplankton: 0.003 ppm DDT → zooplankton (0.04 ppm) → small fish (0.5 ppm) → larger fish (2 ppm) → fish-eating birds (25+ ppm)
3
Concentration increases at each trophic level (biomagnification, bioaccumulation)
4
Not diluted because DDT stored in fatty tissue; every time organism eats, DDT accumulates
5
Effects: osprey, bald eagle egg shell thinning (DDE inhibits Ca deposition) → reproductive failure → population decline
✅ DDT increases concentration up food chain: 0.003 ppm → 25+ ppm in fish-eating birds → reproductive failure

🎯 Quick Activity

Think and Answer

Q: Why is BOD a measure of water pollution? What does high BOD indicate?

▶ Show Answer
A: BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand) measures the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by bacteria to decompose organic matter in a water sample over 5 days at 20°C. High BOD = high organic load (sewage, industrial effluent) = more bacteria consuming O₂. This depletes dissolved O₂ → aquatic life (fish, invertebrates) suffocate and die. Clean rivers: BOD <2 mg/L; polluted: >10 mg/L. BOD is the standard parameter for monitoring sewage treatment efficiency — treated sewage should have BOD <20 mg/L before discharge.

❌ Mistake Bank

Common errors in Biology XII exams

Confusing embryo sac cells

Embryo sac = 7 cells, 8 nuclei. Central cell has 2 polar nuclei. Synergids assist in fertilisation. Antipodals have no direct role — just be clear on each function.

Interchanging codons and anticodons

mRNA has codons (5'→3'). tRNA has anticodons (antiparallel, reads 3'→5' of mRNA). Start codon: AUG on mRNA; corresponding anticodon on tRNA: UAC.

Lamarckism vs Darwinism

Lamarck: use and disuse of organs leads to inherited changes (giraffe neck — wrong). Darwin: variations already exist; natural selection acts on them. Don't mix these up.

In-situ vs Ex-situ confusion

In-situ = conservation at natural habitat (national park, biosphere reserve). Ex-situ = conservation outside natural habitat (zoo, botanical garden, seed bank).
