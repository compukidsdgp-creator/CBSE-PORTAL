🧪 Chapter 1: Solutions
Concentration Terms · Raoult's Law · Colligative Properties · van't Hoff Factor

📖 Core Theory

1.1 Types and Concentration

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute(s) in a solvent. The four concentration terms tested most in board exams:

Molarity (M)
Moles of solute per litre of solution. M = n/V(L). Temperature-dependent.
Molality (m)
Moles of solute per kg of solvent. m = n/W(kg). Temperature-independent — preferred for colligative properties.
Mole Fraction (χ)
χ₁ = n₁/(n₁+n₂). Dimensionless. χ₁ + χ₂ = 1 always. Used in Raoult's law.
Mass Percent (w/w)
Mass of solute × 100 / mass of solution. Independent of temperature.

1.2 Raoult's Law and Vapour Pressure

Raoult's law: Partial vapour pressure of a volatile component is proportional to its mole fraction in solution. For ideal solutions: p₁ = χ₁ × p₁°

🔑 Key Formulae — Solutions

p₁ = χ₁·p₁°    (Raoult's law for component 1)
Relative lowering of VP: (p°–p)/p° = χ₂ (mole fraction of solute)
Elevation of boiling point: ΔTb = Kb·m    Kb(water) = 0.512 K·kg/mol
Depression of freezing point: ΔTf = Kf·m    Kf(water) = 1.86 K·kg/mol
Osmotic pressure: π = iCRT (van't Hoff) where R = 0.0831 L·bar/mol·K
van't Hoff factor: i = observed colligative property / calculated (no assoc/dissoc)

1.3 Colligative Properties

Properties that depend only on the number of solute particles, not their nature. Four colligative properties: relative lowering of VP, elevation of Tb, depression of Tf, osmotic pressure.

Isotonic Solutions
Same osmotic pressure. No net flow of solvent across semi-permeable membrane.
Hypertonic
Higher osmotic pressure than reference. Causes cell shrinkage (crenation) in biology.
Hypotonic
Lower osmotic pressure. Causes cell swelling (haemolysis) in biology.
Abnormal Molar Mass
Electrolytes dissociate → more particles → i>1. Associates (e.g. acetic acid in benzene) → i<1.

✏️ Worked Examples

Molality and Elevation of Boiling Point (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: 18 g of glucose (MW=180) dissolved in 200 g of water. Find ΔTb. Kb(water)=0.512 K·kg/mol.

1
Moles of glucose = 18/180 = 0.1 mol
2
Mass of solvent = 200 g = 0.2 kg
3
Molality m = 0.1/0.2 = 0.5 mol/kg
4
ΔTb = Kb × m = 0.512 × 0.5 = 0.256 K
5
New boiling point = 100 + 0.256 = 100.256°C
✅ ΔTb = 0.256 K; Boiling point = 100.256°C

van't Hoff Factor (Board 2022, 3M)

Q: KCl (MW=74.5) when dissolved in water gives i=1.9. Find degree of dissociation α.

1

2

3

4
α = 0.9
5
Degree of dissociation = 90%
✅ α = 0.9 (90% dissociation)

Osmotic Pressure (Board 2024, 5M)

Q: Calculate osmotic pressure of 0.05 M NaCl solution at 27°C. R=0.0831 L·bar/mol·K, i=1.8.

1

🧪 Chapter 2: Electrochemistry
Electrochemical Cells · Nernst Equation · Electrolysis · Kohlrausch's Law

📖 Core Theory

2.1 Electrochemical Cells

An electrochemical cell converts chemical energy to electrical energy (galvanic cell) or vice versa (electrolytic cell). A galvanic cell has two half-cells connected by a salt bridge.

Anode
Electrode where oxidation occurs. In galvanic cell: −ve terminal. In electrolytic cell: +ve terminal.
Cathode
Electrode where reduction occurs. In galvanic cell: +ve terminal. In electrolytic cell: −ve terminal.
EMF of Cell (E°cell)
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Measured in volts. Positive E° means spontaneous reaction.
Salt Bridge
KCl or KNO₃ in agar-agar. Maintains electrical neutrality and completes the circuit without mixing electrolytes.

2.2 Nernst Equation and Gibbs Energy

🔑 Key Formulae — Electrochemistry

Nernst equation: E = E° − (RT/nF)·ln Q  at 298K: E = E° − (0.0592/n)·log Q
ΔG° = −nFE°    (relation to Gibbs energy; F = 96500 C/mol)
ΔG° = −RT ln K    (at equilibrium E_cell = 0)
Molar conductivity: Λm = κ × 1000/M  (κ = specific conductivity; M = molarity)
Kohlrausch's law: Λ°m = Σλ°(cations) + Σλ°(anions)
Faraday's 1st law: m = ZIt    Z = electrochemical equivalent = M/(nF)

2.3 Conductance

Electrolytic conductance increases with temperature (unlike metallic conductors). Strong electrolytes show Debye-Hückel limiting law: Λm = Λ°m − A√C.

Specific Conductance (κ)
Conductance of 1 cm³ of electrolyte between electrodes 1 cm apart. SI unit: S/m.
Molar Conductivity (Λm)
Conductance of solution containing 1 mol of electrolyte between electrodes 1 cm apart.
Kohlrausch's Law
At infinite dilution, molar conductivity = sum of individual ionic conductances. Used to find Λ°m for weak electrolytes.
Degree of Dissociation
α = Λm/Λ°m  for weak electrolytes. Ka = Cα²/(1−α) ≈ Cα² when α«1.

✏️ Worked Examples

Nernst Equation (Board 2023, 5M)

Q: Calculate E_cell for Zn|Zn²⁺(0.01M)||Cu²⁺(1M)|Cu. E°Zn²⁺/Zn=−0.76V, E°Cu²⁺/Cu=+0.34V.

1
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V
2
Cell reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, n=2 electrons
3
Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.01/1 = 0.01
4
E = E° − (0.0592/n)log Q = 1.10 − (0.0592/2)log(0.01)
5
E = 1.10 − 0.0296×(−2) = 1.10 + 0.0592 = 1.16 V
✅ E_cell = 1.16 V

Faraday's Law (Board 2022, 3M)

Q: How many grams of copper are deposited on passing 2A for 30 min through CuSO₄? (Cu=63.5, n=2)

1
Charge Q = I × t = 2 × 30 × 60 = 3600 C
2
Electrochemical equivalent Z = M/(nF) = 63.5/(2×96500)
3
Z = 63.5/193000 = 3.29×10⁻⁴ g/C
4
m = ZQ = 3.29×10⁻⁴ × 3600 = 1.18 g
5
Alternatively: m = (M×Q)/(n×F) = 63.5×3600/(2×96500) = 1.18 g
✅ m = 1.18 g of copper deposited

Molar Conductivity (Board 2024, 3M)

Q: Specific conductance of 0.02 M CH₃COOH = 2.9×10⁻⁴ S/cm. Λ°m=390.7 S·cm²/mol. Find degree of dissociation.

1
Λm = (κ × 1000)/M = (2.9×10⁻⁴ × 1000)/0.02
2
Λm = 0.29/0.02 = 14.5 S·cm²/mol
3
α = Λm/Λ°m = 14.5/390.7 = 0.037
4
Degree of dissociation α = 3.7%
5
Ka = Cα²/(1−α) ≈ 0.02 × (0.037)² = 2.74×10⁻⁵
✅ α = 3.7%; Ka = 2.74×10⁻⁵

🧪 Chapter 3: Chemical Kinetics
Rate of Reaction · Order · Half-Life · Activation Energy · Arrhenius Equation

📖 Core Theory

3.1 Rate of Reaction and Rate Law

Rate of reaction = decrease in concentration of reactant (or increase in product) per unit time. For A → B: rate = −d[A]/dt = +d[B]/dt.

Order of Reaction
Sum of exponents of concentration terms in rate law. Experimentally determined — not from stoichiometry.
Molecularity
Number of reacting species in elementary step. Always a whole number (1, 2, or 3). From mechanism.
Rate Constant (k)
Proportionality constant in rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. Units depend on order: (mol/L)^(1−n)/s for nth order.
Pseudo-first Order
Higher-order reaction behaves as first-order when one reactant is in large excess (approx. constant). E.g. acid hydrolysis of esters.

🔑 Integrated Rate Laws and Half-Lives

Zero order: [A] = [A]₀ − kt; t½ = [A]₀/(2k); units of k: mol/L/s
First order: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt; t½ = 0.693/k; units of k: s⁻¹
Second order: 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt; t½ = 1/(k[A]₀); units of k: L/mol/s
Arrhenius equation: k = A·e^(−Ea/RT); ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT)
Two temperatures: ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)·(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)

3.2 Factors Affecting Rate

Temperature Effect
Rate roughly doubles for every 10°C rise (temp. coefficient ≈ 2). Arrhenius equation quantifies this through Ea.
Activation Energy (Ea)
Minimum energy reactants must possess to form products. Catalyst lowers Ea without being consumed.
Collision Theory
Reaction occurs when: (1) molecules collide (2) with correct orientation (3) with energy ≥ Ea.
Catalyst
Provides alternate pathway of lower Ea. Increases rate. Not consumed. Does NOT change ΔH or equilibrium position.

✏️ Worked Examples

First Order Half-Life (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: A first-order reaction has k = 6.93×10⁻² s⁻¹. Find t½ and time for 75% completion.

1
t½ = 0.693/k = 0.693/(6.93×10⁻²) = 10 s
2
For 75% completion: 25% remains, i.e., [A] = 0.25[A]₀
3
Using ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt: ln(1/0.25) = kt
4
ln 4 = kt → 1.386 = 6.93×10⁻² × t
5
t = 1.386/0.0693 = 20 s = 2 × t½
✅ t½ = 10 s; t(75%) = 20 s = 2 half-lives

Arrhenius Equation (Board 2022, 5M)

Q: Rate constant of a reaction at 300K is 2.5×10⁻³ s⁻¹ and at 350K is 8.5×10⁻² s⁻¹. Find Ea. R=8.314 J/mol/K.

1
ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)
2
ln(8.5×10⁻²/2.5×10⁻³) = (Ea/8.314)(1/300 − 1/350)
3
ln(34) = (Ea/8.314)(350−300)/(300×350)
4
3.526 = (Ea/8.314)(50/105000)
5
Ea = 3.526 × 8.314 × 105000/50 = 61,714 J/mol ≈ 61.7 kJ/mol
✅ Ea = 61.7 kJ/mol

Order from Data (Board 2024, 3M)

Q: For A → products: [A]=0.1M gives rate=2×10⁻³M/s; [A]=0.2M gives rate=4×10⁻³M/s. Find order and k.

1
Rate₁ = k[A]₁ⁿ and Rate₂ = k[A]₂ⁿ
2
Rate₂/Rate₁ = ([A]₂/[A]₁)ⁿ
3
4×10⁻³/2×10⁻³ = (0.2/0.1)ⁿ
4
2 = 2ⁿ → n = 1 (first order)
5
k = rate/[A] = 2×10⁻³/0.1 = 0.02 s⁻¹
✅ n = 1 (first order); k = 0.02 s⁻¹

🧪 Chapter 4: d and f-Block Elements
Transition Metals · Properties · Lanthanoids · Actinoids

📖 Core Theory

4.1 Transition Metals (d-Block)

Elements in which the last electron enters a d-orbital. Configuration: (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻². Note: Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and Cu = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ (half-filled and fully-filled d is extra stable).

Variable Oxidation States
d-block elements show multiple OS because 3d and 4s electrons have similar energies. Mn shows +2 to +7; Fe shows +2 and +3.
Paramagnetism
Due to unpaired d-electrons. Magnetic moment μ = √n(n+2) BM where n = number of unpaired electrons.
Colour
d-d transition of electrons absorbs visible light. Colour = complementary of absorbed wavelength. [Ti(H₂O)₆]³⁺ appears purple.
Catalytic Property
Variable OS + ability to adsorb reactants. Fe in Haber, V₂O₅ in Contact, Ni in hydrogenation, MnO₂ in KClO₃ decomp.

🔑 Important Reactions and Facts

MnO₄⁻ in acid: Mn²⁺ (colourless); in neutral/faintly basic: MnO₂ (brown); in conc. NaOH: MnO₄²⁻ (green)
Dichromate: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O (E° = +1.33V)
K₂Cr₂O₇ in H₂SO₄ solution turns green (Cr²(SO₄)₃) when reduced
Lanthanoid contraction: caused by poor shielding of 4f electrons; reason 4d and 5d elements are nearly same size

4.2 f-Block Elements

Lanthanoids (La to Lu, 4f): mostly +3 OS, paramagnetic, lanthanoid contraction (atomic/ionic radii decrease across series). Actinoids (Ac to Lr, 5f): wider range of OS than lanthanoids, radioactive, larger size due to poorer shielding by 5f than 4f.

✏️ Worked Examples

🧪 Chapter 5: Coordination Compounds
Werner's Theory · Nomenclature · Isomerism · Bonding · EAN Rule

📖 Core Theory

5.1 Terminology and Nomenclature

A coordination compound has a central metal atom/ion bonded to ligands by coordinate bonds. Written: [Central metal + ligands]^charge counter ion.

Coordination Number
Number of donor atoms bonded to central metal. [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺: CN=6. [Ni(CO)₄]: CN=4.
Ligands
Lewis bases donating lone pair(s) to metal. Monodentate (NH₃, Cl⁻), Bidentate (en, ox²⁻), Polydentate (EDTA⁴⁻: hexadentate). Ambidentate: SCN⁻ (via S or N).
EAN Rule
Effective Atomic Number = Z of metal − charge + (2 × ligands donated electrons). Stable complexes often have EAN of nearest noble gas.
Chelate Effect
Polydentate ligands form more stable complexes than monodentate due to increased entropy (releasing multiple water molecules per chelate ring closure).

5.2 Naming Coordination Compounds

Rules: ligands (alphabetical) before metal; anionic ligands get "-o" suffix (Cl⁻=chlorido, CN⁻=cyanido, NO₂⁻=nitrito); metal in cation named normally, in anion gets "-ate" suffix; oxidation state in Roman numerals in brackets.

🔑 IUPAC Names — Common Examples

[Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ → hexaamminecobalt(III) ion
[CoCl₃(NH₃)₃] → triamminetrichloridocobalt(III)
K₄[Fe(CN)₆] → potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II)
[Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ → tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate
[Ni(CO)₄] → tetracarbonylnickel(0)
[PtCl₂(NH₃)₂] → diamminedichloridoplatinum(II) — cisplatin when cis-

5.3 Isomerism and Bonding

Structural isomers: ionisation, linkage, coordination, solvate/hydrate. Stereoisomers: geometric (cis-trans) and optical (mirror images, non-superimposable).

VBT (Valence Bond Theory)
Metal provides empty hybrid orbitals; ligands donate electron pairs. sp³ (tetrahedral), dsp² (square planar), sp³d² outer / d²sp³ inner (octahedral).
CFT (Crystal Field Theory)
Ligands are point charges. d orbitals split: t₂g (lower) and eg (higher) in octahedral. CFSE determines high-spin vs low-spin.
Strong Field Ligands
Spectrochemical series high end: CO > CN⁻ > NO₂⁻ > en > NH₃ > ... Cause large crystal field splitting → low spin, diamagnetic.
Weak Field Ligands
Spectrochemical series low end: F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O. Small splitting → high spin, paramagnetic.

🧪 Chapter 6: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes
SN1 · SN2 · E1 · E2 · Nucleophilic Substitution

📖 Core Theory

6.1 Nucleophilic Substitution

Haloalkanes undergo nucleophilic substitution (SN1 or SN2) where a nucleophile replaces the halogen. Haloarenes are much less reactive due to resonance.

SN2 (Bimolecular)
One-step, concerted mechanism. Inversion of configuration (Walden). Rate = k[RX][Nu]. Primary > Secondary. Not tertiary.
SN1 (Unimolecular)
Two-step via carbocation. Racemisation. Rate = k[RX]. Tertiary > Secondary. Polar protic solvents favour SN1.
Optical Activity
Chiral compound rotates plane of polarized light. Enantiomers — non-superimposable mirror images. Racemic mixture is optically inactive.
Reactivity Order
RI > RBr > RCl > RF (bond dissociation energy decreases down group). Allylic/benzylic halides highly reactive in both SN1 and SN2.

🔑 Key Reactions

Finkelstein: RCl + NaI (acetone) → RI + NaCl (SN2)
Swartz: RCl + AgF → RF + AgCl
Haloarenes + NaOH (300°C, 300 atm) → phenol (nucleophilic aromatic substitution)
Grignard reagent: RX + Mg (dry ether) → RMgX

📝 Detailed Electrode Processes

6.3 Electrolytic Cells and Faraday's Laws

Faraday's First Law
Mass deposited (w) ∝ quantity of electricity (Q = It). w = ZIt where Z = electrochemical equivalent = M/(nF).
Faraday's Second Law
Same quantity of electricity deposits masses proportional to M/n (equivalent weights). w₁/w₂ = (M₁/n₁)/(M₂/n₂).
Kohlrausch's Law
At infinite dilution: Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋ (sum of ion conductances × stoichiometric coefficients). Used to calculate Λ°m of weak electrolytes (e.g. CH₃COOH) from strong electrolyte data.
Nernst Equation
E_cell = E°_cell − (RT/nF)ln Q = E°_cell − (0.0591/n)log Q at 25°C. At equilibrium: E_cell=0 → log Kc = n·E°_cell/0.0591.

🔑 Key Formulae

ΔG° = −nFE°_cell; ΔG° = −RT ln Kc → E°_cell = (RT/nF)ln Kc = (0.0591/n)log Kc
Specific conductance κ = (1/R) × (l/A) = conductance × cell constant
Molar conductance Λm = κ × 1000/M (in S·cm²·mol⁻¹; M in mol/L)
Degree of dissociation α = Λm/Λ°m (for weak electrolyte)

🧪 Chapter 7: Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers

📖 Core Theory

7.1 Alcohols — Classification and Reactions

Alcohols (−OH group on sp³ C) are classified as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°) based on the carbon bearing −OH. Higher the alkyl groups around C−OH, lower is the reactivity toward SN reactions.

Lucas Test
Lucas reagent (ZnCl₂/conc. HCl) differentiates 1°, 2°, 3° alcohols. 3° → immediate turbidity; 2° → turbidity in 5 min; 1° → no turbidity at room temp.
Esterification
Alcohol + carboxylic acid ⇌ ester + H₂O (H₂SO₄ catalyst, reversible). Mechanism: nucleophilic acyl substitution.
Phenols
−OH directly on benzene ring. More acidic than alcohols (phenoxide ion stabilised by resonance). Undergo Kolbe, Reimer-Tiemann, esterification with acid chlorides.
Ethers
R−O−R structure. Prepared by Williamson synthesis (RO⁻ Na⁺ + R'X → ROR' + NaX). Inert to most reagents. Cleaved by HI/HBr.

🔑 Reactions of Phenol

Kolbe's reaction: PhONa + CO₂ (125°C, 4–7 atm) → salicylate (aspirin precursor)
Reimer-Tiemann: PhOH + CHCl₃ + NaOH → salicylaldehyde
Williamson synthesis: RONa + R'X → ROR' + NaX (SN2)

📝 Rate Laws and Integrated Forms

7.3 Zero and First Order Kinetics

Zero Order
Rate = k; [A] = [A]₀ − kt. Half-life t₁/₂ = [A]₀/2k. Units of k: mol·L⁻¹·s⁻¹.
First Order
Rate = k[A]; [A] = [A]₀·e^(−kt); ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt. Half-life t₁/₂ = 0.693/k (independent of initial concentration). Units of k: s⁻¹.
Arrhenius Equation
k = Ae^(−Ea/RT). ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT). log(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/2.303R)×(1/T₁ − 1/T₂). Ea from two temperature measurements.
Collision Theory
Rate = PZe^(−Ea/RT) where P=steric factor (fraction of collisions with correct orientation), Z=collision frequency. Fraction of molecules with E≥Ea increases with temperature.

🔑 Integrated Rate Law Summary

Zero order: [A] = [A]₀ − kt; graph [A] vs t → straight line slope = −k
First order: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt; graph ln[A] vs t → straight line slope = −k
Second order: 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt; graph 1/[A] vs t → straight line slope = +k
Half-life: 0th order: [A]₀/2k; 1st order: 0.693/k; 2nd order: 1/(k[A]₀)

3
t = (2.303/k)log([A]₀/[A]) = (2.303/0.0231)log(100/10) = (2.303/0.0231)×1 = 99.7 min
✅ k = 0.0231 min⁻¹; t (90%) ≈ 100 min
🧪 Chapter 8: Aldehydes, Ketones and Acids
Nucleophilic Addition · Aldol · Fehling · Tollen's

📖 Core Theory

8.1 Aldehydes and Ketones

Carbonyl group (C=O): aldehydes have at least one H on carbonyl carbon (−CHO); ketones have two alkyl/aryl groups (R−CO−R'). Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones (less steric hindrance, electron density).

Nucleophilic Addition
Nu attacks electrophilic C of C=O. HCN, NaHSO₃, Grignard, RNH₂ (→ imine). Aldehydes > ketones in reactivity.
Aldol Condensation
Aldehyde with α-H in dil. NaOH → β-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol) which on heating gives α,β-unsaturated carbonyl. Mixed aldol possible.
Tollens' Test
Ammoniacal AgNO₃; aldehyde → silver mirror; ketone = no reaction. RCHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ → RCOO⁻ + 2Ag↓ + 4NH₃ + H⁺.
Fehling's Test
Alkaline CuSO₄ + sodium potassium tartrate (Cu²⁺ complex). Aliphatic aldehyde → brick red ppt (Cu₂O). Aromatic aldehydes and ketones don't respond.

🔑 Key Reactions

Cannizzaro (no α-H): 2HCHO + NaOH → CH₃OH + HCOONa (disproportionation)
Clemmensen: C=O → CH₂ (Zn-Hg/HCl)
Wolff-Kishner: C=O → CH₂ (NH₂NH₂, KOH, high T)
Carboxylic acids: RCOOH + NaOH → RCOONa + H₂O; with PCl₅ → RCOCl; with ROH (H₂SO₄) → ester

📝 Colloidal State and Emulsions

8.3 Colloids — Properties and Preparation

Tyndall Effect
Scattering of light by colloidal particles. Observed in colloidal solutions (not true solutions). Used to distinguish colloids from true solutions.
Brownian Movement
Zig-zag motion of colloidal particles due to unequal bombardment by solvent molecules. Prevents settling under gravity (helps stability).
Coagulation
Precipitation of colloid by adding excess electrolyte. Hardy-Schulze rule: higher charge on ion → greater coagulating power. Coagulation value = moles of electrolyte to coagulate 1L of colloid.
Adsorption
Accumulation of substance on surface. Physisorption: weak van der Waals; reversible; multilayer. Chemisorption: strong chemical bond; irreversible; monolayer; specific. High surface area (powders, colloids) enhances adsorption.

🔑 Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

x/m = k·P^(1/n) where x=mass adsorbed, m=mass adsorbent, P=pressure, k,n=constants
log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)·log P → straight line graph; slope=1/n; intercept=log k
At low P: x/m ∝ P; at high P: x/m = constant (saturation)
Emulsions: O/W (oil in water: milk, vanishing cream) vs W/O (water in oil: butter, cold cream)

🧪 Chapter 9: Amines

📖 Core Theory

9.1 Classification and Basicity

Amines are derivatives of NH₃ where one or more H is replaced by alkyl/aryl group. Classified as 1°, 2°, 3° amines or quaternary ammonium salt.

Basicity Order
In gas phase: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃. In aqueous solution: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (solvation effect). Arylamines much weaker than alkylamines (lone pair delocalised into ring).
Gabriel Synthesis
Phthalimide + KOH → potassium phthalimide + R-X → N-alkylphthalimide → hydrolysis → pure primary amine. Used to avoid secondary/tertiary amine formation.
Hoffmann Bromamide
RCONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → RNH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O. Product amine has one carbon less than the amide.
Diazonium Salt
ArNH₂ + NaNO₂ + HCl (0–5°C) → ArN₂⁺Cl⁻. Key intermediate for azo dyes, phenols, halides (Sandmeyer). Aliphatic diazonium salts are too unstable.

🔑 Diazonium Reactions (Sandmeyer)

ArN₂⁺ + CuCl → ArCl + N₂ (Sandmeyer)
ArN₂⁺ + CuBr → ArBr + N₂ (Sandmeyer)
ArN₂⁺ + CuCN → ArCN + N₂
ArN₂⁺ + HBF₄ → ArF + N₂ + BF₃ (Balz-Schiemann)
ArN₂⁺ + H₃PO₂ + H₂O → ArH + N₂ (deamination)
Coupling: ArN₂⁺ + PhOH/ArNH₂ (alk) → Ar−N=N−Ar' (azo dye)

📝 Coordination Chemistry Naming and Isomerism

9.3 IUPAC Naming and Types of Isomerism

IUPAC Naming Rules
1. Name ligands first (alphabetically), then central metal. 2. Ligand names end in -o (anion) or -ine, -o etc. (neutral). 3. Roman numeral = oxidation state. 4. Anionic complex ends in -ate.
Geometric Isomerism
Square planar MA₂B₂: cis (same groups adjacent) vs trans (same groups opposite). Only if two or more different ligands present. MA₄ or MA₂B₂ in octahedral also show cis/trans.
Optical Isomerism
Non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers). Shown by [Co(en)₃]³⁺ type complexes. Optically active = rotate plane-polarised light; d (+) or l (−).
Ionisation Isomerism
Same formula, different ions in solution. [Co(NH₃)₅Br]SO₄ vs [Co(NH₃)₅(SO₄)]Br. The ions outside brackets dissociate in water.

🔑 Crystal Field Theory

Octahedral field: d orbitals split into t₂g (lower, 3 orbitals, −0.4Δ₀) and eₘ (higher, 2 orbitals, +0.6Δ₀)
CFSE = number of electrons × energy per orbital
Strong field ligands (CO, CN⁻, NO₂⁻): large Δ, low spin; weak field (F⁻, Cl⁻, H₂O): small Δ, high spin
Spectrochemical series (weak→strong): I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO

🧪 Chapter 10: Biomolecules
Carbohydrates · Proteins · Nucleic Acids · Vitamins

📖 Core Theory

10.1 Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates = polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones (or compounds that give them on hydrolysis). Formula Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ (approximately). Classified by hydrolysis: mono-, di-, polysaccharides.

Monosaccharides
Cannot be hydrolysed. Glucose (aldohexose, open chain: HOCH₂(CHOH)₄CHO), Fructose (ketohexose). D and L forms based on −OH at penultimate C (relative to glyceraldehyde).
Disaccharides
Two monosaccharides linked by glycosidic bond (formed by dehydration). Sucrose (glucose+fructose, non-reducing), Maltose (glucose+glucose, reducing), Lactose (galactose+glucose, reducing).
Polysaccharides
Starch (amylose+amylopectin, α-glycosidic links, food storage), Cellulose (β-glycosidic links, cell wall — humans can't digest). Glycogen (animal storage, highly branched).
Reducing Sugars
Have free −CHO or −OH at anomeric C. Give +ve Fehling's and Tollens'. All monosaccharides + maltose + lactose are reducing. Sucrose is non-reducing.

🔑 Proteins and Nucleic Acids

Amino acids: H₂N−CHR−COOH. 20 standard; essential (must be in diet); non-essential (body synthesises).
Peptide bond: −CO−NH− formed between −COOH of one and −NH₂ of next amino acid
Protein structure: 1° (sequence), 2° (α-helix/β-sheet, H-bonds), 3° (folding, S-S bonds), 4° (multiple subunits)
DNA: deoxyribose + phosphate + bases (A-T, G-C Watson-Crick pairing). Double helix.
RNA: ribose + phosphate + bases (A-U, G-C). Single stranded (mostly). mRNA, tRNA, rRNA.

🧪 Chapter 11: Polymers
Classification · Addition · Condensation · Rubber · Fibres

📖 Core Theory

11.1 Classification of Polymers

A polymer is a large molecule built from repeating structural units (monomers). Classified by origin (natural/synthetic/semi-synthetic), structure (linear/branched/crosslinked), type of polymerisation, and thermal response.

Addition Polymers
Formed from monomers with C=C double bonds by chain growth. No byproduct. Example: polyethene (-CH₂-CH₂-)ₙ from ethene; PVC from vinyl chloride; Teflon from CF₂=CF₂.
Condensation Polymers
Formed by stepwise reaction with elimination of small molecules (H₂O, HCl etc). Bifunctional monomers. Nylon-6,6: hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid. Dacron/Terylene: ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid.
Thermoplastics
Soften on heating, re-mould. Linear or slightly branched chains. Examples: PVC, polythene, polystyrene, nylon.
Thermosetting
Once set, cannot be re-melted (crosslinked). Bakelite (phenol+formaldehyde), melamine-formaldehyde, urea-formaldehyde.

🔑 Important Polymers

Natural rubber: cis-1,4-polyisoprene (isoprene monomer). Vulcanisation with S improves elasticity/strength by S-S crosslinks.
Nylon-6,6: [−CO(CH₂)₄CO−NH(CH₂)₆NH−]ₙ — condensation polymer (amide bonds)
Nylon-6: from caprolactam (ring-opening polymerisation) — one type of monomer
Buna-S: styrene-butadiene copolymer (synthetic rubber)
Buna-N: acrylonitrile-butadiene copolymer (oil-resistant)

📖 Core Theory

12.1 Drugs and Drug-Target Interaction

A drug is a chemical substance that interacts with a biomolecular target (receptor, enzyme, DNA) and produces a biological response. Drugs are classified by pharmacological effect or target.

Antacids
Neutralise excess HCl in stomach. NaHCO₃, Mg(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃. Should not be taken in excess — may cause alkalosis.
Antihistamines
Block histamine receptors (H₁) — treat allergy, cold. Examples: benadryl (diphenhydramine), ranitidine (also H₂, for ulcer). Do NOT block histamine synthesis.
Antibiotics
Kill or inhibit bacteria. Bactericidal (kill): penicillin, aminoglycosides. Bacteriostatic (inhibit growth): sulphonamides, tetracycline, chloramphenicol. Broad vs narrow spectrum.
Analgesics
Relieve pain. Non-narcotic: aspirin (anti-inflammatory too), paracetamol. Narcotic: morphine, codeine (addictive, controlled). NSAIDs inhibit COX enzyme.

🔑 Detergents and Food Chemicals

Soaps: sodium/potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids. Cannot clean in hard water (Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ form scum).
Detergents: sodium alkylbenzenesulphonate (ABS) or sodium lauryl sulphate. Work in hard water. Biodegradable if linear chain.
Antioxidants in food: BHA (butylated hydroxy anisole), BHT (butylated hydroxy toluene), vitamin C, vitamin E
Sweeteners: saccharin (350× sweet), aspartame (100×), sucralose (600×, sucrose-based)

📝 Aldehydes and Ketones — Reactions

12.3 Nucleophilic Addition and Oxidation-Reduction

Nucleophilic Addition
Carbonyl C⁺ attacked by nucleophile. Aldehydes more reactive than ketones (less steric, more electron-deficient). Nu: HCN, RMgX (Grignard), NaHSO₃, 2,4-DNP, H₂O (gem-diol).
Tollens Test
Silver mirror test: RCHO + [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ → RCOOˉ + Ag↓ (silver mirror). Positive for aldehydes, negative for ketones. Also positive for glucose (aldehyde group).
Fehling's Test
Deep blue Cu²⁺ complex + RCHO → brick-red Cu₂O precipitate. Positive for aliphatic aldehydes. Negative for aromatic aldehydes (benzaldehyde) and ketones.
Aldol Condensation
Aldehyde with α-H + dilute base → β-hydroxy aldehyde (aldol); on heating → α,β-unsaturated aldehyde (dehydration). Cross-aldol with two different compounds also possible.

🔑 Key Name Reactions

Cannizzaro Reaction: 2HCHO + NaOH → CH₃OH + HCOONa (no α-H; disproportionation)
Clemmensen Reduction: C=O + Zn-Hg/HCl → CH₂ (acidic medium)
Wolf-Kishner: C=O + NH₂NH₂/KOH/Δ → CH₂ (basic medium)
Rosenmund: RCOCl + H₂/Pd-BaSO₄ → RCHO (selective reduction of acid chloride)

🧪 Chapter 13: Solid State
Crystal Systems · Packing · Defects · Electrical Properties

📖 Core Theory

13.1 Crystal Systems and Packing

In crystalline solids, particles are arranged in a definite, repeating 3D pattern (unit cell). Amorphous solids have short-range order only (glass, rubber).

Unit Cell
Smallest repeating unit of crystal lattice. Simple cubic (1 atom), BCC (2 atoms), FCC/CCP (4 atoms). Contribution: corner = 1/8, face = 1/2, edge = 1/4, body centre = 1.
Packing Efficiency
% of unit cell volume occupied by atoms. SC = 52.4%, BCC = 68%, FCC/HCP = 74% (maximum; ABCABC or ABABAB stacking).
Crystal Defects
Frenkel defect: cation moves to interstitial (density unchanged); Schottky defect: paired vacancies of cation+anion (density decreases). Both increase with temperature.
Electrical Properties
n-type semiconductor: pentavalent impurity (P, As) donates extra electron. p-type: trivalent impurity (B, Al) creates electron hole. Band gap determines conductor/semiconductor/insulator.

🔑 Key Formulae

Density: ρ = (Z×M)/(a³×Nₐ) where Z=atoms/unit cell, M=molar mass, a=edge length
FCC: 4 atoms; a = 2√2 r; Packing efficiency = 74.05%
BCC: 2 atoms; a = 4r/√3; Packing efficiency = 68.02%
SC: 1 atom; a = 2r; Packing efficiency = 52.36%

Q: Distinguish between Frenkel and Schottky defects.

2

3

4

🎯 Quick Activity

Think and Answer

Q: Why does NaCl crystal have no Frenkel defect but AgCl does?

▶ Show Answer
A: Frenkel defect requires the cation to move to an interstitial void. In NaCl, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are nearly equal in size, so Na⁺ is too large to fit into the small tetrahedral interstitial sites without seriously distorting the lattice. In AgCl, Ag⁺ is much smaller than Cl⁻ and can easily move to interstitial positions, creating Frenkel defects.

📝 Carboxylic Acids and Derivatives

13.3 Reactions and Acidity

Acidity Order
Carboxylic acids stronger than phenol > water > alcohols. EWG on ring/chain increases acidity (stabilises carboxylate by induction). EDG decreases acidity. CCl₃COOH > CHCl₂COOH > CH₂ClCOOH > CH₃COOH (Cl withdraws e⁻).
Reactions of −COOH
With NaOH: salt + water. With Na₂CO₃: salt + CO₂ + H₂O. With PCl₅/SOCl₂: acid chloride RCOCl. With NH₃: amide (via ammonium salt). With ROH/H⁺: ester (Fischer esterification).
Hell-Volhard-Zelinsky (HVZ)
RCOOH + Cl₂/P → α-chloro acid (RCHClCOOH). Specific to carboxylic acids with α-hydrogen. The −COOH activates α-position.
Decarboxylation
RCOONa + NaOH → RH + Na₂CO₃ (soda lime, heating). Removes −COOH group. Used to descend the homologous series. CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ (methane).

🔑 Derivatives of Carboxylic Acids

Reactivity towards nucleophilic acyl substitution: acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide
Esterification: RCOOH + R′OH ⇌ RCOOR′ + H₂O (catalyst H⁺; reversible)
Saponification: RCOOR′ + NaOH → RCOONa + R′OH (irreversible; gives soap from fat)
Reduction: RCOOH + LiAlH₄ → RCH₂OH (primary alcohol)

🧪 Chapter 14: Surface Chemistry
Adsorption · Colloids · Emulsions · Catalysis

📖 Core Theory

14.1 Adsorption

Adsorption is accumulation of a substance (adsorbate) on the surface of another (adsorbent). Distinguished from absorption (penetration through bulk). Adsorption is always exothermic (ΔH < 0).

Physisorption
Weak van der Waals forces; low Ea (<40 kJ/mol); reversible; multilayer possible; decreases with temperature; example: N₂ on charcoal.
Chemisorption
Strong chemical bonds (covalent/ionic); high Ea (40–400 kJ/mol); monolayer; may increase then decrease with T (activated process at first); example: H₂ on Ni.
Colloids
Heterogeneous mixture; dispersed phase: 1–1000 nm. Sol (solid in liquid), Aerosol (solid/liquid in gas), Emulsion (liquid in liquid), Gel (liquid in solid), Foam (gas in liquid).
Tyndall Effect
Scattering of light by colloidal particles (visible beam). Absent in true solutions. Used to distinguish colloids from true solutions. Blue colour of sky is Tyndall effect.

🔑 Freundlich Adsorption Isotherm

x/m = k·P^(1/n) where x/m = amount adsorbed per gram, P = pressure
Taking log: log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)log P (straight line, slope = 1/n)
At low pressure: x/m ∝ P; at high pressure: x/m = constant (monolayer)
Coagulation: Hardy-Schulze rule — greater the valency of coagulating ion, greater the coagulating power

📝 Amines — Basicity and Reactions

14.3 Basic Character and Reactions of Amines

Basicity Order (Aliphatic)
In gas phase: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃. In aqueous solution: 2° > 1° > 3° > NH₃ (solvation effect reverses for 3°). Electron-donating groups increase basicity; EWG decrease it.
Aromatic vs Aliphatic
Aniline (C₆H₅NH₂) far less basic than aliphatic amines. Lone pair on N delocalised into benzene ring (resonance) → less available for protonation. Electron-withdrawing groups on ring further decrease basicity.
Diazotisation
ArNH₂ + NaNO₂ + HCl at 0–5°C → ArN₂⁺Cl⁻ (diazonium salt). Must be done below 5°C; diazonium salts are unstable above 5°C.
Coupling Reaction
ArN₂⁺ + ArH (activated) → Ar-N=N-Ar′ (azo dye; orange/yellow colour). Coupling at para position of activated ring (phenol, aniline).

🔑 Distinguishing 1°, 2°, 3° Amines

Hinsberg's test: benzene sulfonyl chloride (C₆H₅SO₂Cl). 1°: sulfonamide soluble in NaOH. 2°: sulfonamide insoluble in NaOH. 3°: no reaction.
Mustard oil reaction (carbylamine test): 1° amine + CHCl₃ + KOH → isocyanide (mustard smell). Negative for 2° and 3°.
With HNO₂ (NaNO₂+HCl): 1° → diazonium (unstable for aliphatic, stable for aromatic); 2° → N-nitrosoamine (yellow oil); 3° → no reaction

🧪 Chapter 15: General Principles of Isolation of Metals
Metallurgy · Ores · Refining

📖 Core Theory

15.1 Metallurgy — Principles

Metallurgy is the science of extracting metals from their ores and refining them for use. Key steps: mining, crushing, concentration (beneficiation), extraction, refining.

Concentration Methods
Froth flotation (sulphide ores), Magnetic separation (magnetite/chromite), Gravity separation (gold/tin), Leaching (Al₂O₃ by NaOH in Baeyer's process for Al).
Ellingham Diagram
Plot of ΔG° vs temperature for metal oxide formation. Lower on diagram = more stable oxide = stronger reducing agent. Carbon (as C or CO) is used as reductant when its line is below the metal oxide line.
Hall-Heroult Process
Electrolytic extraction of Al: Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆). Cathode: carbon lining (Al deposits). Anode: carbon (oxidised to CO₂). Temperature: ~950°C.
Refining Methods
Electrolytic refining (Cu, Ag, Au, Ni), Zone refining (Ge, Si — semiconductors), Vapour phase (Mond process for Ni: Ni + 4CO → Ni(CO)₄ → Ni), Van Arkel (Ti, Zr: TiI₄ decomp on W wire).

🔑 Extraction Reactions

Iron (Blast furnace): Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (at ~700°C)
Copper (froth flotation → roasting → self-reduction): Cu₂S + O₂ → 2Cu + SO₂ (partial oxidation)
Al (Hall-Heroult): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (cathode); 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (anode; C anode burns)
Baeyer's: Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O; then CO₂ + H₂O → Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃

📝 Polymers — Types and Properties

15.3 Classification and Important Polymers

Addition vs Condensation
Addition: monomers with C=C join without losing atoms (polyethylene, PVC, Teflon). Condensation: monomers with two functional groups; small molecule (H₂O, HCl) lost per step (nylon, Dacron, Bakelite).
Thermoplastic vs Thermosetting
Thermoplastic: linear/branched; softens on heating (reversible); recyclable. Examples: polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene. Thermosetting: cross-linked network; does not soften (irreversible). Examples: Bakelite, melamine-formaldehyde.
Copolymers
Two different monomers. Buna-S: styrene + butadiene. Buna-N: acrylonitrile + butadiene. Neoprene: chloroprene. SBR (Buna-S) is the most common synthetic rubber.
Biodegradable Polymers
PHBV (polyhydroxybutyrate-co-hydroxyvalerate): natural, biodegradable; used in surgery. Nylon-2-nylon-6: alternating amino acids; biodegradable. Contrast with non-biodegradable plastics (PVC, polythene).

🔑 Important Named Polymers (Board Favourites)

Nylon-6,6: hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid → [−NH(CH₂)₆NH−CO(CH₂)₄CO−]n; strong fibre
Nylon-6: caprolactam (ring opening) → [−NH(CH₂)₅CO−]n
Dacron/Terylene: ethylene glycol + terephthalic acid; polyester; clothing + bottles
Bakelite: phenol + formaldehyde; cross-linked; electrical switches, combs
Natural rubber: polyisoprene (cis-1,4); vulcanisation with S adds cross-links → harder, elastic

🧪 Chapter 16: p-Block Elements
Group 15, 16, 17, 18 · Properties · Reactions · Allotropes

📖 Core Theory

16.1 Group 15 — Nitrogen Family

Group 15: N, P, As, Sb, Bi. Valence configuration: ns²np³. Three p-electrons, many possible oxidation states (−3 to +5). Nitrogen unique: forms multiple bonds (N₂, triple bond), unlike heavier congeners.

Oxoacids of N
HNO₂ (nitrous, +3 OS) and HNO₃ (nitric, +5 OS). HNO₃: strong oxidising acid. Dilute → NO with Cu; concentrated → NO₂ with Cu. Aqua regia = 3:1 HCl:HNO₃ (dissolves noble metals).
Allotropes of P
White P (P₄ tetrahedron, reactive, spontaneously flammable in air, stored under water), Red P (polymeric, stable, less reactive), Black P (most stable, layered structure like graphite).
Group 16 — Chalcogens
O, S, Se, Te, Po. O forms 2 bonds; S forms 2, 4, or 6. Allotropes of S: rhombic (stable at RT) and monoclinic (stable 96–119°C). S₈ ring structure.
Group 17 — Halogens
F₂ (most reactive nonmetal, pale yellow gas), Cl₂ (yellow-green), Br₂ (reddish-brown liquid), I₂ (dark violet solid). Only F has OS −1 only; others also +1, +3, +5, +7. HF is weak acid (strong H-bonding).

🔑 Noble Gases and Key Reactions

Group 18: He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn. Octet complete → almost inert. Xe forms compounds: XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆, XeO₃, XeOF₄.
Interhalogen compounds: AB, AB₃, AB₅, AB₇ where A is heavier halogen (B is always F or the lighter one)
Bleaching powder: Ca(OCl)Cl — Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ (dry) at 40°C. Active agent: OCl⁻ (hypochlorite)
Ozone: O₃ formed at high altitude by UV; shield against UV-B/C. Depleted by Cl· (from CFCs): Cl· + O₃ → ClO· + O₂ (chain reaction)

Q: XeF₄ exists but NF₄⁺ does not — explain in terms of atomic properties.

▶ Show Answer

Define osmotic pressure. What happens when a potato strip is placed in concentrated NaCl solution?
Osmotic pressure is the minimum pressure needed to prevent osmosis across a semipermeable membrane. In concentrated NaCl: the solution is hypertonic → water flows OUT of potato cells by osmosis → the strip shrinks (plasmolysis).

Write Nernst equation. Calculate E_cell for Zn|Zn²⁺(0.01M)||Fe²⁺(0.001M)|Fe. E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = –0.76V; E°(Fe²⁺/Fe) = –0.44V.
E°cell = –0.44 – (–0.76) = 0.32V. Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Fe²⁺] = 0.01/0.001 = 10. E = 0.32 – (0.0591/2)log10 = 0.32 – 0.02955 = 0.290V.
2022 | 3 marks | Chemical Kinetics
Define order of reaction. A first order reaction has t½ = 30 minutes. What fraction remains after 90 minutes?
Order = sum of exponents in rate law (experimental). After 3 half-lives (90 min): (1/2)³ = 1/8 remains. So fraction = 1/8 = 0.125.

Name: [CoCl₂(en)₂]⁺ and write its formula for the compound with chloride counter ion. Does it show geometrical isomerism?
Dichloridobis(ethylenediamine)cobalt(III) chloride: [CoCl₂(en)₂]Cl. Yes, it shows cis-trans geometrical isomerism as it is an octahedral complex with two identical monodentate ligands and one bidentate ligand.

❌ Mistake Bank

Common errors students make in Chemistry XII exams

Using wrong concentration units in Nernst equation

Use molarity (mol/L), not molality. Standard state: all concentrations = 1M, all gases at 1 atm.

Confusing order and molecularity

Order is experimentally determined; molecularity is theoretical. For multi-step reactions, order is determined by the rate-determining (slowest) step.

van't Hoff factor (i) forgotten for electrolytes

Always apply i for ionic compounds: NaCl → i≈2, CaCl₂ → i≈3, Al₂(SO₄)₃ → i≈5. Colligative properties become i×K×m.

Ignoring hybridisation change in coordination compounds

sp³ = tetrahedral (outer, high spin). dsp² = square planar (inner). sp³d² = outer octahedral. d²sp³ = inner octahedral. Strong field = inner orbital complex.

Writing wrong products for aldol condensation

First aldol addition gives β-hydroxy carbonyl compound. Then on heating (or in base/acid) → α,β-unsaturated carbonyl (dehydration). Write both steps if asked.

Forgetting that sucrose is non-reducing

Sucrose (cane sugar) has no free hemiacetal group → non-reducing. All monosaccharides are reducing. Maltose and lactose are reducing (have free hemiacetal). Sucrose is not.

👁️ Examiner's Eye

What examiners look for in Chemistry XII answers

Write equations, not just words

For 3-mark questions, a balanced equation earns 1 mark automatically. Always write chemical equations for reactions — don't describe them in prose alone.

State units for all physical quantities

Molar conductivity in S cm² mol⁻¹, rate constant units depend on order. A numerical answer without units loses ½–1 mark.

Name IUPAC correctly, in full

Examiners check: multiplying prefixes (di, tri vs bis, tris for complex names), oxidation state in Roman numerals, anion suffix (-ate, -ite, -o). Don't abbreviate.

Draw structure for organic mechanisms

For mechanism questions, show arrow-pushing (curved arrows). Partial charge symbols (δ+ / δ−) are expected. Missing mechanism arrows = marks lost.

Distinguish between similar-sounding reactions

Aldol, Claisen, Cannizzaro, Perkin — examiners test if you know which substrate each requires. Memorise: Cannizzaro = no α-H, conc. NaOH.
