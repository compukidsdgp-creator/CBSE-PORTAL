Ch 1 — A Letter to God (G.L. Fuentes)

Lencho's Faith
Absolute, unshakeable trust in God. Writes a letter to God asking for 100 pesos after hail destroys crop. Never doubts God will provide.
Irony
The postmaster and employees collect 70 pesos out of goodwill. Lencho, convinced God sent 100, accuses the helpers of stealing 30 pesos — calls them "a bunch of crooks".
Theme
Ironical faith — deep faith in God vs unfounded suspicion of honest human beings who actually helped. Contrast between the divine and the human.
MCQ 1 What destroyed Lencho's crops?
(A) Flood   (B) Drought   (C) Hailstorm   (D) Locusts
Ans: (C) Hailstorm
MCQ 2 Why did Lencho call the post office employees "a bunch of crooks"?
(A) They read his letter (B) They sent 70 instead of 100 pesos (C) They didn't reply (D) They stole his crops
Ans: (B) — He believed God had sent 100 pesos; 30 were missing.
A-R A: Lencho had strong faith in God.
R: He wrote a letter to God expecting money to be sent directly.
Ans: (A) Both true; R correctly explains his unquestioning faith.
SA (3M) Describe the irony in "A Letter to God".
Ans: The irony is multi-layered. The postmaster and employees, moved by Lencho's faith, collected their own money and sent it anonymously. Yet Lencho, the man of great faith, suspected these honest helpers of stealing. He trusted the invisible God but distrusted the visible helpers. The very act of human kindness was perceived as dishonesty — this is the story's central irony.

Ch 2 — Nelson Mandela: Long Walk to Freedom

Apartheid
South Africa's racial segregation policy (1948–1994). Black people denied equal rights. Mandela imprisoned 27 years on Robben Island. ANC led the struggle.
Twin Obligations
Every man has twin obligations — to his family AND to his people/nation. Under apartheid, fulfilling the latter meant sacrificing the former.
Courage
"Courage is not the absence of fear, but the triumph over it." Mandela's most quoted line — the brave man conquers fear, not the one who never feels it.

🔑 Key Facts — Mandela

Inauguration: 10 May 1994 — South Africa's first black President
Key comrades: Walter Sisulu, Oliver Tambo, Chris Hani, Yusuf Dadoo
Quote: "I have walked that long road to freedom… I have discovered that after climbing a great hill, one only finds that there are many more hills to climb."
MCQ 1 When was Mandela inaugurated as President?
(A) 1990   (B) 1991   (C) 1994   (D) 1996
Ans: (C) 1994
MCQ 2 What, according to Mandela, are the twin obligations of every man?
(A) Work and play (B) Family and nation (C) God and country (D) Education and career
Ans: (B) Family and nation
A-R A: Mandela says that the oppressor is also imprisoned by apartheid.
R: Hatred and prejudice take away one's humanity — oppressor becomes prisoner of his own prejudice.
Ans: (A)
SA (4M) What does Mandela mean by "the oppressor must be liberated just as surely as the oppressed"?
Ans: Mandela argues that apartheid dehumanised not just its victims but also those who enforced it. A man who takes away another's freedom is a prisoner of hatred and prejudice. True freedom — the freedom to live freely, to love — is denied to both oppressor and oppressed under an unjust system. Liberation from apartheid thus freed all South Africans, regardless of race.
LA (5M) "The oppressor must be liberated just as surely as the oppressed." Elaborate on Mandela's vision of freedom and human dignity as expressed in "Long Walk to Freedom".
Ans: Mandela's vision transcends personal freedom. He defines freedom in two phases — first, as a youth, personal freedom: to be out after curfew, to read, to travel. But as he matured, he realised personal freedom alone was incomplete without the freedom of his people. His "Long Walk to Freedom" was not just physical (Robben Island to the Union Buildings) but moral — teaching that true humanity requires recognising dignity in all. He acknowledges his comrades' sacrifices, accepts the twin obligation of family and nation, and articulates that courage is not fearlessness but the conquest of fear. His inauguration was both a personal milestone and a national rebirth, representing the triumph of justice over systemic oppression.

Ch 3 — Two Stories About Flying

Part I: The Black Aeroplane
Pilot in storm clouds guided by mysterious black plane. Lands safely; no such plane is recorded. Mystery remains. Theme: grateful acknowledgement of a helpful stranger whose identity we may never know.
Part II: His First Flight
Young seagull afraid to fly. Family finally leaves him alone and hungry. Driven by hunger, he leaps and discovers he can fly. Theme: fear of the unknown is overcome by necessity and courage.
MCQ 1 What forced the young seagull to finally take flight?
(A) Brothers pushed him (B) Mother brought food just out of reach (C) Storm wind (D) Falling off the ledge
Ans: (B)
MCQ 2 What does the mysterious black aeroplane symbolise in Part I?
(A) Death (B) Danger (C) Helpful unknown guidance (D) Technology
Ans: (C)
SA (3M) Compare the themes of Part I and Part II of "Two Stories About Flying".
Ans: Both stories deal with overcoming fear. In Part I, the pilot faces the physical danger of storm clouds but is guided to safety by a mysterious plane — the theme is trusting unknown help in a crisis. In Part II, the young seagull overcomes psychological fear of flight through hunger and necessity — the theme is that inner courage and the right push allow us to transcend our fears. Both affirm that what seems impossible becomes achievable with the right support or sufficient motivation.

Ch 4 — From the Diary of Anne Frank

Anne Frank
Jewish girl hiding from Nazis in Amsterdam (1942–44). Wrote diary "Kitty". Believed a true friend is rare. Writes: "Paper is more patient than people."
Theme
Loneliness and the need for a trusted friend. Anne could not confide in people; her diary became her best friend. Writing as a therapeutic escape from fear and isolation.
MCQ 1 Anne Frank named her diary:
(A) Dear Diary (B) Kitty (C) Paper (D) Friend
Ans: (B) Kitty
SA (2M) Why did Anne Frank maintain a diary?
Ans: Anne felt she had no true friend to confide in. She believed "paper is more patient than people" — a diary would listen without judgment. She addressed it as "Kitty", treating it as a trusted companion to whom she could confide her deepest thoughts and feelings in the terrifying circumstances of hiding from the Nazis.

Ch 5 — The Hundred Dresses (Parts I & II) — Eleanor Estes

Wanda Petronski
Poor Polish girl. Claims she has a hundred dresses at home (to impress). Bullied by Maddie and Peggy about her worn dress. Her drawings of a hundred dresses win the art contest — but she has already moved away.
Maddie's Guilt
Maddie never participated in bullying but never stopped it either — her silence made her complicit. Her guilt drives the moral of the story: standing by in silence is as wrong as bullying.
MCQ 1 Why did Wanda say she had a hundred dresses?
(A) She loved fashion (B) To impress the girls (C) She actually owned them (D) To win a contest
Ans: (B) To impress the girls
A-R A: Maddie felt deeply guilty even though she never teased Wanda directly.
R: Silence in the face of injustice makes one equally responsible.
Ans: (A)

Ch 6 — Mijbil the Otter — Gavin Maxwell

Mijbil
Otter from Southern Iraq. Maxwell's pet. Playful, clever — learned to turn taps, play with marbles. "Mij" caused chaos on plane and in London streets (pedestrians stared, asked "Is that a baby alligator?")
Theme
Deep bond between humans and animals. Joy and enrichment that an unusual pet can bring. Also: the effort required to care for a wild creature responsibly.
MCQ 1 Where did Maxwell get Mijbil from?
(A) India (B) Southern Iraq (C) Scotland (D) London Zoo
Ans: (B) Southern Iraq
SA (3M) What incident on the flight to London shows Mijbil's personality?
Ans: Mijbil was travelling in a box in the aircraft hold. After about an hour, a stewardess informed Maxwell that "something" had got loose. Mijbil had torn through the box and was running wildly around the aircraft, causing panic. Maxwell caught him. The incident shows Mijbil's intelligence (escaped confinement), energy, and curiosity — qualities that also made him endearing as a pet.

Ch 7 — Madam Rides the Bus — Vallikkannan

Valli
8-year-old girl fascinated by the bus that passes her house. Saves money by refusing small treats. Takes a solo bus ride to town and back — her greatest achievement.
Life and Death
On her return journey, Valli sees the dead body of the young cow she admired on the way. The joy of life and the reality of death — both exist side by side. Mature realisation for a child.
MCQ 1 How did Valli save money for the bus fare?
(A) Borrowed from friends (B) Refused snacks, toys and fairs (C) Found it on road (D) Mother gave her
Ans: (B)
SA (3M) What does Valli's experience on the bus journey teach her about life?
Ans: Valli's journey is a metaphor for growing up. The thrill of her first solo bus ride symbolises newfound independence. But the sight of the dead young cow — the same one she had admired for its liveliness — teaches her that life is fragile and fleeting. Her mood changes from excited to sombre. She discovers, without being told, that joy and sorrow, life and death, coexist. This awakening is her real lesson, more profound than the bus ride itself.

Ch 8 — The Sermon at Benares

Kisa Gotami
Grieving mother carrying dead son. Goes to Buddha for medicine to revive him. Buddha asks for a mustard seed from a house where no one has died. She cannot find such a house — realises death is universal.
Theme
Death is universal and inevitable. Grief is natural but one must accept loss and move on. Buddha's teaching: sorrow comes from selfish desires and attachments.
MCQ 1 What did the Buddha ask Kisa Gotami to bring?
(A) Holy water (B) Mustard seed from a house where no one had died (C) Medicine (D) Flowers
Ans: (B)
A-R A: Kisa Gotami could not find a mustard seed as required by the Buddha.
R: Every house she visited had experienced death — death is universal.
Ans: (A)
SA (3M) How did the Buddha help Kisa Gotami overcome her grief?
Ans: Buddha did not give a direct lecture. Instead, he gave her a task — to bring a mustard seed from a house where no one had ever died. As she went door to door, she heard stories of loss from every family. Gradually she realised that death is universal; she was not alone in her grief. This indirect method helped her accept her son's death as part of the natural order. She returned to Buddha, understood his teaching, and found peace.

Ch 9 — The Proposal — Anton Chekhov (Play)

Plot
Lomov visits Kovalenko to propose to Natalya. But they quarrel about the Oxen Meadows. Reconciled, then quarrel again about hunting dogs. Lomov faints. Finally, engagement happens amid continued quarrelling — comic ending.
Satire
Chekhov satirises the Russian landed gentry — petty, quarrelsome, materialistic. Marriage seen as a property transaction, not love. Lomov's motivation is practical (he needs a wife for his household), not romantic.
MCQ 1 Why does Lomov visit Kovalenko?
(A) To settle a land dispute (B) To propose to Natalya (C) To discuss dogs (D) To borrow money
Ans: (B)
MCQ 2 What do Lomov and Natalya quarrel about first?
(A) Dogs (B) Money (C) Oxen Meadows (D) Marriage
Ans: (C) Oxen Meadows
LA (5M) "The Proposal" is a satirical play. Discuss what Chekhov is satirising through the characters of Lomov, Natalya, and Kovalenko.
Ans: Chekhov uses comedy to expose the shallowness of the Russian landed gentry. Lomov, a nervous hypochondriac, wants to marry not out of love but for the practical comforts of having a wife and a household that runs smoothly. Natalya, despite her interest in marriage, cannot resist quarrelling over trivial property matters. Kovalenko, the father, is delighted at the prospect of a son-in-law and desperately pushes Natalya to accept. The play satirises: (1) marriages as property alliances, (2) the pettiness of disputes over land and dogs among people of means, (3) the lack of romance or genuine affection, and (4) the irony that even a proposal of marriage cannot be completed without quarrelling. The comedy serves as a social critique of a class that has lost real values in favour of material concerns.

📦 Case-Based Passage — Prose

Passage (from A Letter to God): "The house — the only one in the entire valley — sat on the crest of a low hill. From this height one could see the river and the field of ripe corn dotted with the flowers that always promised a good harvest. The only thing the earth needed was a downpour or at least a shower. Throughout the morning Lencho — who knew his fields intimately — had done nothing but see the sky towards the north-east."

Q1 (1M) What was the setting of Lencho's house? Ans: On the crest of a low hill — the only house in the entire valley, with a view of the river and cornfields.
Q2 (1M) What did Lencho hope for? Ans: A downpour or at least a shower to water his crops.
Q3 (2M) What does "knew his fields intimately" tell us about Lencho? Ans: It shows Lencho was a hardworking, attentive farmer deeply connected to his land and its needs. His livelihood depended entirely on the harvest and he watched the sky anxiously, showing his practical concern and experience.

Dust of Snow — Robert Frost

Central Idea
A crow shakes snow from a hemlock tree onto the poet. This small, unexpected act changes his mood and saves part of a "ruined day". Small natural acts can bring positive change.
Symbols
Crow = traditionally bad omen; Hemlock = poisonous plant. Yet together, they bring joy. Frost inverts expected negative symbols to show even negative-seeming things can uplift.
MCQ 1 What changed the poet's mood in "Dust of Snow"?
(A) A rainbow (B) Snow falling from a hemlock tree shaken by a crow (C) Meeting a friend (D) Sunshine
Ans: (B)
SA (2M) What is the significance of the crow and hemlock tree in "Dust of Snow"?
Ans: The crow is a traditional symbol of ill-omen and the hemlock is a poisonous plant — both carry negative connotations. Frost uses them deliberately to show that even seemingly negative things can bring about a positive change. The unexpected dusting of snow on the poet lifts his spirit and redeems a ruined day — nature, in its simplest form, heals the human soul.

Fire and Ice — Robert Frost

Fire
Symbol of desire, passion, greed. "From what I've tasted of desire / I hold with those who favour fire." Fire is destructive enough to end the world.
Ice
Symbol of hatred, indifference, cruelty. Cold, slow — but also sufficient to destroy the world. "I think I know enough of hate / To say that for destruction ice / Is also great / And would suffice."
MCQ 1 In "Fire and Ice", fire represents:
(A) Warmth (B) Desire/greed (C) Anger only (D) War
Ans: (B) Desire/greed
A-R A: Frost suggests that both fire and ice can bring about the end of the world.
R: Fire symbolises desire and ice symbolises hatred — both are destructive human emotions.
Ans: (A)

A Tiger in the Zoo

Caged Life
Tiger paces behind bars, "few steps of his cage." Quiet rage. Contrast: what he should be doing in the wild — lurking in shadows near waterholes, terrifying villagers.
Theme
Captivity vs freedom. Man's cruelty in imprisoning wild animals for entertainment. The tiger's helpless dignified suffering — "ignoring visitors" and staring at the stars at night.
MCQ 1 In "A Tiger in the Zoo", the tiger stares at the stars because:
(A) He enjoys astronomy (B) He longs for the freedom of the wild (C) The stars calm him (D) He is watching for prey
Ans: (B)
SA (3M) Contrast the tiger's life in the zoo with what it would have been in the wild.
Ans: In the zoo, the tiger is confined — a few steps of cage, quiet rage, ignoring visitors. In the wild, he would lurk in shadow near water, slide through long grass, terrorise the villages, snarl at passing deer at the jungle edge. The wild tiger is powerful, free, instinctive. The caged tiger is a reduced, humiliated creature who has lost his identity. The poem protests against man's arrogance in caging wild creatures for spectacle.

The Ball Poem — John Berryman

Central Idea
A boy loses his ball. The poet deliberately does not offer comfort — the boy must learn the "epistemology of loss." First irreplaceable loss is a rite of passage into adulthood.
Key Quote
"Money is external. / He is learning, well behind his desperate eyes, / The epistemology of loss." Loss cannot always be compensated with money — some things are irreplaceable.
MCQ 1 Why does the poet not offer to buy the boy a new ball?
(A) He is poor (B) He wants the boy to learn to cope with irreplaceable loss (C) Balls are expensive (D) The shop is closed
Ans: (B)
SA (2M) What does the lost ball symbolise?
Ans: The ball symbolises the joys of childhood — irreplaceable, carefree, innocent. Its loss represents the first encounter with something that cannot be bought back with money. It marks the beginning of adulthood, where losses must be accepted rather than compensated. The boy's grief teaches him the "epistemology of loss" — the fundamental truth that some things, once gone, are gone forever.

Amanda — Robin Klein

Amanda's Daydreams
She imagines herself as: an orphan roaming barefoot and free; Rapunzel (no nagging mother); a mermaid on a rock. All fantasies involve freedom from adult control.
Irony
The adult accuses Amanda of sulking — yet the adult's constant nagging creates the very sulking they complain about. The adult is unaware of the damage their control causes.
MCQ 1 In Amanda's Rapunzel daydream, she wishes:
(A) To be rescued (B) That no one would climb up to her (C) To have long hair (D) To live in a tower
Ans: (B) — She would not let her hair down; no visitors, absolute solitude.
A-R A: Amanda daydreams of being an orphan.
R: She craves freedom from constant adult instructions and nagging.
Ans: (A)
SA (3M) How does "Amanda" criticise the way adults treat children?
Ans: Through Amanda's persistent daydreams, the poem shows that children need psychological space to be themselves. The adult in the poem constantly nags Amanda about posture, eating habits, cleaning her room, and acne — all trivial matters. Amanda's retreats into fantasy are a natural response to this suffocation. The poem's irony lies in the adult complaining that Amanda is sulking — not realising the adult's own behaviour drives the sulking. Klein criticises the over-controlling adult attitude that suppresses a child's natural spirit and creativity.

Animals — Walt Whitman

Central Idea
The poet says he would like to "turn and live with animals." Animals are calm, self-contained, do not complain, do not weep for sins, do not bore him with discussions of duty. They represent a purer existence.
Theme
Critique of modern human life — full of anxiety, guilt, materialism, greed. Animals possess "tokens" of virtues that humans have lost or discarded through civilisation.
MCQ 1 In "Animals", the poet admires animals because:
(A) They are stronger (B) They are calm, honest and self-contained (C) They can talk (D) They live longer
Ans: (B)
SA (2M) What human qualities does Walt Whitman say animals possess?
Ans: Whitman says animals are placid, self-contained, do not lie awake weeping for their sins, do not make him sick discussing their duty to God, are not discontented or mad with mania for owning things. He suggests animals hold the "tokens" of virtues — honesty, contentment, self-sufficiency — that humans once had but have lost in the course of civilisation.

For Anne Gregory — W.B. Yeats

Central Idea
A man tells Anne Gregory that men only love her for her yellow hair (physical beauty), not for herself. Anne says she could dye her hair and make a man love her soul. The poet says only God can love a person for themselves alone, not for their physical beauty.
Theme
Physical beauty vs inner beauty. Superficiality of human love vs depth of divine love. God alone can love a person for "the soul" not for "the yellow hair."
MCQ 1 In "For Anne Gregory", what does the yellow hair represent?
(A) Wisdom (B) Physical/external beauty (C) Age (D) Wealth
Ans: (B) Physical/external beauty
SA (2M) What is the central message of "For Anne Gregory"?
Ans: The poem argues that human love is superficial — men love Anne for her physical beauty (yellow hair), not for her inner self. Anne proposes dyeing her hair to change this, but the poet concludes that only God can love a person for "themselves alone and not their yellow hair." The poem is a meditation on the difference between conditional human love and unconditional divine love.

📦 Case-Based Passage — Poetry (Fire and Ice)

Passage: "Some say the world will end in fire, / Some say in ice. / From what I've tasted of desire / I hold with those who favour fire. / But if it had to perish twice, / I think I know enough of hate / To say that for destruction ice / Is also great / And would suffice."

Q1 (1M) What does fire represent in this poem? Ans: Desire, passion, and greed — destructive human emotions.
Q2 (1M) What does ice symbolise? Ans: Hatred, indifference, and cruelty.
Q3 (2M) What is Frost's conclusion about the end of the world? Ans: Frost says the world could end in fire (desire) once; but if it had to perish twice, hatred (ice) is also sufficient for destruction. He critiques both extremes of human emotion — both are equally capable of bringing about ruin. The poem is a compact but powerful warning about unchecked human passions.

Ch 1 — A Triumph of Surgery — James Herriot

Tricki
Mrs Pumphrey's dog, overindulged with rich food and no exercise. Becomes very ill. Dr Herriot insists on hospitalisation. With exercise, diet and companionship of other dogs, Tricki recovers completely in a fortnight.
Theme
Overindulgence causes harm; a disciplined lifestyle restores health. Mrs Pumphrey learns that too much love expressed through food is not love but harm.
MCQ 1 What was the real reason for Tricki's illness?
(A) Lack of sunlight (B) Overfeeding and no exercise (C) Infection (D) Old age
Ans: (B)
MCQ 2 What did Herriot actually do with the food hampers Mrs Pumphrey sent for Tricki?
(A) Fed them to Tricki (B) Returned them (C) Ate them with his colleagues (D) Donated them
Ans: (C) — The story's comic element; the vet and friends ate the ham, wine, and eggs Mrs Pumphrey sent.
SA (3M) Dr Herriot called Tricki's recovery a "triumph of surgery" — was it really? Explain.
Ans: The title is ironic. Dr Herriot performed no surgery at all. He simply took Tricki away from Mrs Pumphrey's overindulgent household, put him on a strict diet, allowed him to play and exercise with other dogs, and the dog recovered naturally within a fortnight. The real triumph was of common sense over excessive love — of discipline over indulgence. Calling it a "triumph of surgery" is Herriot's gentle humour at Mrs Pumphrey's expense and at his own role.

Ch 2 — The Thief's Story — Ruskin Bond

Characters
Hari Singh — young thief. Anil — young writer, trusting, teaches Hari to read, write, cook. Hari steals Anil's money but is moved by Anil's trust and returns it before the train departs.
Theme
Trust and goodness can reform even a dishonest person. Hari realises that Anil's trust is more valuable than the stolen money. Education is the path to true freedom from a life of crime.
MCQ 1 Why did Hari Singh return the stolen money?
(A) He was caught (B) He was moved by Anil's trust and felt guilty (C) Money was too little (D) Train was delayed
Ans: (B)
A-R A: Hari Singh decided not to board the Lucknow Express with the stolen money.
R: He felt that Anil's trust and the education he was receiving were more valuable than the stolen money.
Ans: (A)
LA (5M) "The Thief's Story" shows that trust is a powerful force for change. Discuss how Anil's trust in Hari Singh changes him.
Ans: When Hari Singh meets Anil, he is a practised thief who befriends people in order to rob them. But Anil is different — he trusts Hari completely, teaches him to read, write, and cook, and pays him (even sharing his own meagre income). This uncalculated trust unsettles Hari. When he finally steals Anil's 600 rupees and prepares to flee, he stands at the station and thinks — the money could buy comfort, but Anil was also teaching him to write complete sentences, which could ultimately change his life. He could become someone. He returns the money, slipping it back under Anil's mattress. When Anil offers him a 50-rupee note the next morning with a smile, Hari feels shame — and the possibility of real change. The story demonstrates that genuine trust, freely given, is more transformative than punishment or lectures.

Ch 3 — The Midnight Visitor — Robert Arthur

Ausable
Portly, unimpressive secret agent. Uses quick wit to fool the intruder (Max) into believing a nonexistent balcony is below the window. Max falls to his death trying to escape to this "balcony."
Theme
Wit and intelligence are more powerful than physical appearance. Ausable's cleverness — improvising a story on the spot — defeats the armed Max.
MCQ 1 How did Ausable fool Max?
(A) Called the police (B) Grabbed his gun (C) Invented a story about a balcony below the window (D) Locked him in
Ans: (C)
SA (2M) What does "The Midnight Visitor" tell us about the power of a quick mind?
Ans: Ausable, though physically unimpressive, defeats the armed Max through sheer wit. When Max appears in his room, Ausable immediately improvises a story about a "balcony" outside the window, making it sound like a standard feature and a nuisance. When police knock (who are actually bringing a message), Max panics and steps out the "balcony" — which does not exist. Ausable's ability to think and speak calmly under pressure — creating a believable fiction on the spot — is the story's central lesson: the mind is mightier than the weapon.

Ch 5 — Footprints Without Feet — H.G. Wells

Griffin
Scientist who discovers how to make body transparent. Becomes invisible. Commits crimes — steals, vandalises. Eventually caught when footprints in snow reveal his presence.
Theme
Science without ethics is dangerous. Misuse of brilliant discovery leads to crime and destruction. Invisibility is a metaphor for accountability — one without it loses their moral bearings.
MCQ 1 How was Griffin's presence first discovered in the opening of the story?
(A) He sneezed (B) Footprints appeared in snow (C) Shadow was visible (D) He bumped into someone
Ans: (B) Footprints in snow
A-R A: Griffin is described as a "brilliant scientist but a lawless person."
R: He misused his scientific discovery to commit crimes instead of benefiting humanity.
Ans: (A)

Ch 7 — The Necklace — Guy de Maupassant

Matilda / Mme Loisel
Ambitious woman who borrows a diamond necklace, loses it, spends 10 years in poverty repaying the debt for a replacement — only to discover the original was fake (paste/artificial).
Theme
Pride and vanity lead to ruin. A single night of social ambition costs ten years of hardship. The irony of the fake necklace is the story's devastating punch line.
MCQ 1 What was the value of the necklace that Matilda borrowed?
(A) Real diamonds — very expensive (B) Paste — practically worthless (C) Gold — moderately priced (D) Ruby — precious
Ans: (B) Paste — worthless (revealed at the end)
SA (3M) What is the irony in "The Necklace"?
Ans: The devastating irony is that Matilda and her husband spent ten years of grinding poverty, working day and night to repay the 36,000 francs borrowed to replace the diamond necklace — only to learn from Mme Forestier at the end that the original necklace was "paste" worth at most 500 francs. The very suffering that was meant to restore honour was caused by a trivial object. Had Matilda confessed the loss immediately, it could have been resolved in a day. Her pride destroyed her life — and the "diamonds" were never real. Maupassant punishes vanity with devastating precision.

Ch 8 — Bholi

Bholi (Sulekha)
Born slow due to fever, disfigured by smallpox, stammers. Family considers her a burden. Teacher's encouragement transforms her. Refuses degrading marriage to old moneylender Bishamber Nath who demands dowry at the last minute.
Theme
Education as liberation — transforms a "dumb cow" into a confident, self-respecting woman. Social evils: dowry system, treatment of women with disabilities, child marriage.
MCQ 1 Why did Bholi refuse to marry Bishamber Nath?
(A) He was old (B) He demanded dowry at the wedding (C) She loved someone else (D) She wanted to study further
Ans: (B)
A-R A: Bholi's teacher is the most important character in her transformation.
R: The teacher gave Bholi books, encouragement and confidence, turning her from a "dumb cow" into an assertive young woman.
Ans: (A)
LA (5M) Trace the transformation of Bholi from a timid, overlooked girl to a confident woman who refuses a degrading marriage.
Ans: Bholi begins life as Sulekha, brain-damaged from a childhood fever, scarred by smallpox, and a stammerer. Her family considers her a burden and her siblings mock her. She is sent to school almost accidentally — her parents think it does not matter since no one will marry her anyway. But school changes everything. Her teacher recognises her pain and potential. She gives Bholi picture books, speaks gently, tells her she will be able to read and write better than anyone — and that no one will make fun of her then. This consistent encouragement builds Bholi's confidence. When Bishamber Nath arrives at the wedding and demands dowry mid-ceremony, it is the new Bholi who throws down the garland and refuses the match. She says she would rather be unmarried and teach at the village school than be bought by a greedy man. Education gave Bholi not just literacy, but dignity, voice, and the courage to stand against social evil.

📖 Formats and Rules

Formal Letter Format
Sender address → Date → Receiver address → Subject → Salutation (Dear Sir/Madam) → Body (3 paras) → Complimentary close (Yours faithfully) → Signature.
Notice Writing
School/Organisation Name → NOTICE (centered) → Bold Title → Date → Body (what/when/where/who) → Name + Designation. In a box. Max 50 words body.
Analytical Paragraph
Based on a pie chart, bar graph, or data table. Structure: opening sentence naming topic → key trends → notable comparisons → overall conclusion. No personal opinion. Use: "The graph depicts...", "It is evident that..."
Diary Entry
Date and day → Dear Diary → Personal, reflective tone → First-person narrative → Sign-off (Name/nickname). Express feelings, events, wishes freely.

📝 Grammar
Reported Speech · Active-Passive · Modals · Editing · Gap Fill · Determiners

📖 Key Grammar Rules

Reported Speech
Present → Past; "will" → "would"; "can" → "could"; "here" → "there"; "today" → "that day"; "tomorrow" → "next day". Yes/No question: if/whether. Wh-question: keep wh-word.
Active to Passive
Object → Subject. Verb → be (appropriate tense) + V3. Optional: "by + agent." "She writes a letter" → "A letter is written by her."
Modals
Can (present ability); Could (past ability/polite); May (permission/possibility); Might (remote possibility); Must (strong obligation); Should (advice); Would (polite request/habit in past); Ought to (moral duty).
Determiners
Articles (a, an, the), quantifiers (some, any, many, few, little), demonstratives (this, that, these, those), possessives (my, your, his, their). "Few" (negative — hardly any); "a few" (positive — some).

🔑 Passive — Tense Chart

Simple Present: is/are + V3 — "The letter is written."
Simple Past: was/were + V3 — "The letter was written."
Present Perfect: has/have been + V3 — "The letter has been written."
Future: will be + V3 — "The letter will be written."
Continuous: is/are/was/were being + V3 — "The letter is being written."

✏️ Worked — Reported Speech (Board 2023)

Q: "I am going to the market. Do you want anything?" he said to her.

1
Statement: "I am going" → He said that he was going to the market.
2
Yes/No Question: "Do you want anything?" → He asked her if she wanted anything.
✅ He said that he was going to the market and asked her if she wanted anything.

A Letter to God (2022, 2023)
Q: What does Lencho's faith in God reveal about his character? (3 marks)
Answer: Absolute, unwavering faith — wrote to God for 100 pesos, received 70, suspected post-office men of stealing. Irony: his faith was stronger than his gratitude. Theme: blind faith vs rational thinking.
Nelson Mandela (2020, 2021, 2024)
Q: What twin obligations does Mandela mention? (3 marks)
Answer: (1) To family, parents, wife — the private obligations. (2) To his people, community, country — the public obligations. He fulfilled the second by sacrificing the first.
His First Flight (2021, 2022)
Q: How did the young seagull overcome his fear of flying? (3 marks)
Answer: His mother held a piece of fish near but not within reach. Driven by hunger, the seagull leapt — and discovered he could fly. Hunger conquered fear. Theme: courage through necessity.
The Thief's Story (2020, 2021, 2023)
Q: Why did Hari Singh not steal from Amir Khan? (3 marks)
Answer: Amir Khan trusted Hari Singh completely — this trust was new to him. "It was a new experience for me." He realised he could have a better life if someone trusted him. Theme: power of trust and reformation.
Fire and Ice (Poem) (2021, 2022, 2024)
Q: What do 'fire' and 'ice' symbolise in Robert Frost's poem? (2 marks)
Answer: Fire = desire/passion/greed; Ice = hatred/cold indifference. Both are powerful enough to end the world. Poet says desire (fire) will be the primary cause but ice (hatred) is also capable.

❌ Mistake Bank — English Language & Literature
Most common board exam errors in English L&L — avoid these for full marks
Literature — Generic Answers
Mistake: "The character was brave and kind" — without connecting to the specific story/poem.
Fix: Always cite the event: "In 'His First Flight', the young seagull showed courage when hunger forced him to leap from the cliff, overcoming his fear." Event + character trait = full marks.
Poem — Not Identifying Poetic Devices
Mistake: Answering poem questions without mentioning metaphor, simile, alliteration, personification.
Fix: Every 3-mark poem answer should include at least one device. "Frost uses 'fire' as a metaphor for human desire..." This adds depth and shows literary awareness — examiner awards bonus impression marks.
Grammar — Editing (Wrong Identification)
Mistake: Changing a correct word in the editing exercise — only ONE error exists per sentence.
Fix: Read the sentence for meaning first. Ask: tense, subject-verb agreement, article, preposition — only one of these is wrong. Don't change what isn't broken.
Writing — Story/Paragraph Without Structure
Mistake: Writing a story without a beginning, conflict, and resolution — reads as a random sequence of events.
Fix: Story structure = Setup (who/where) → Problem → Climax → Resolution (moral/lesson). Even a short story must have all four in 200 words.
Footprints — Mixing Up Characters
Mistake: Confusing Griffin (Invisible Man) with Hari Singh (Thief's Story), or Bholi with other characters.
Fix: Create a quick character-story map: Griffin → Invisible Man → Iping; Hari Singh → Thief → Amir Khan; Bholi → village girl → school → stuttering cured. This prevents mix-up under exam pressure.
Formal Letter — Wrong Complimentary Close
Mistake: "Yours lovingly" or "Yours affectionately" in a formal letter — these are for personal/friendly letters only.
Fix: Formal letter (to unknown person) = Yours faithfully. Formal letter (to known person by name) = Yours sincerely. Informal (friend/relative) = Yours lovingly / Your friend.
📓 Literature Answer Formula: For every character or theme question: (1) State who/what — 1 line (2) Evidence from the story/poem — 2-3 lines with direct reference (3) What it reveals/means — 1 line conclusion. This 3-part structure earns full marks in 3-mark and 5-mark literature questions.

👁️ Examiner's Eye — English Language & Literature
What the CBSE English L&L examiner looks for in your answers
Literature (3-mark) — Expected Structure
CBSE Marking: Content (2) + Expression (1) = 3 marks.
Examiner expects: Story reference + character's action + theme connection. "Lencho's faith (character) was so strong that even after receiving only 70 pesos (event), he wrote a second letter (action), revealing both his faith and irony (theme)."
Poem (2-mark) — Expected Structure
CBSE Marking: Meaning (1) + Poetic device/theme (1) = 2 marks.
"Fire symbolises desire (meaning) — Frost uses metaphor to compare human greed to destructive fire (device). The poem warns that unchecked passion will end civilisation (theme)."
Formal Letter (5-mark) — Breakdown
CBSE Marking: Format (2) + Content (2) + Language (1) = 5 marks.
Format marks are automatic if you know the structure. Start writing format immediately — don't plan content first. 2 marks in 2 minutes.
Editing Exercise (1-mark each)
Examiner checks: Only ONE change per sentence is required. Write: (a) The error word (underlined) + (b) The correct word. Never explain why — just correct. Common errors: a/an/the, was/were/is, has/have, preposition choice.
Reading Passage — Answer Quality
Examiner rewards: Answers starting with the question's key word, complete sentences, paraphrasing (not copy-paste). Copying large chunks from passage loses expression mark. Rephrase in your own words — it signals understanding.
Footprints (Short Answer — 2 marks)
Examiner expects: 2 distinct points with story reference. "The Invisible Man, Griffin, was a scientist who discovered a formula (point 1, from story) and used invisibility for crime rather than science (point 2)." Two clear points = full marks.
🎯 English L&L Secret: The Reading section (20 marks) is purely skill-based — no memorisation needed. Practise 1 unseen passage daily for 2 weeks before exam. This section separates 70-scorers from 90-scorers.
