📊 Microeconomics — Consumer & Demand
Introduction · Utility · Consumer Equilibrium · Demand · Elasticity

📖 Core Theory

Utility
Satisfaction derived from consuming a good. Total Utility (TU) = sum of marginal utilities. Marginal Utility (MU) = change in TU from consuming one additional unit. Law of DMU: as consumption rises, MU falls.
Consumer Equilibrium (Cardinal)
MU/P = lambda (marginal utility of money). Condition: MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y. Consumer maximises utility when MU of last rupee spent on each good is equal.
Indifference Curve (IC)
Locus of combinations of two goods giving equal satisfaction. Properties: downward sloping, convex to origin (MRS diminishes), ICs never intersect, higher IC = more utility.
Budget Line
All combinations affordable at given income and prices. Equation: P_x × X + P_y × Y = M. Slope = -P_x/P_y. Shifts: income change shifts it parallel; price change rotates it.
MRS
Marginal Rate of Substitution = rate at which consumer gives up Y to get one more X, maintaining same utility. MRS = MU_x/MU_y. Consumer equilibrium (Ordinal): MRS = P_x/P_y.
Price Elasticity of Demand
Ed = % change in Qd / % change in P. Perfectly elastic: Ed = infinity (horizontal). Perfectly inelastic: Ed = 0 (vertical). Unitary: Ed = 1. Elastic: |Ed| > 1. Inelastic: |Ed| < 1.

🔑 Key Formulae — Consumer Theory

TU = MU_1 + MU_2 + ... + MU_n (sum of MUs)
MU = TU_n - TU_(n-1) = change in TU
Cardinal Equilibrium: MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y = lambda
Ordinal Equilibrium: MRS = P_x/P_y, and consumer is on budget line
Ed = (deltaQ/deltaP) × (P/Q) [point elasticity]
Ed = (Q2-Q1)/(P2-P1) × (P1+P2)/(Q1+Q2) [arc elasticity]

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1 — Numerical (Board 2023, 4M)

Q: When price of good X rises from Rs 10 to Rs 12, quantity demanded falls from 200 to 160 units. Calculate price elasticity of demand.

1
deltaQ = 160 - 200 = -40; deltaP = 12 - 10 = 2
2
Ed = (deltaQ/deltaP) × (P/Q) = (-40/2) × (10/200) = -20 × 0.05 = -1
3
|Ed| = 1 → Unitary elastic demand. A 1% rise in price causes exactly 1% fall in quantity demanded.
✅ Ed = 1 (unitary elastic). TR remains unchanged when price changes.

📝 Practice Questions — Consumer Behaviour & Demand

MCQ 1 When price of a good falls and demand rises, we move along the demand curve. This is called:
(A) Change in demand (B) Contraction of demand (C) Extension of demand (D) Shift in demand
Ans: (C) Extension of demand
MCQ 2 If demand for bread rises when income falls, bread is a:
(A) Superior good (B) Normal good (C) Giffen good / inferior good (D) Complementary good
Ans: (C) Giffen good / inferior good
A-R 1 A: The Law of Demand has exceptions — Giffen goods and Veblen goods are two examples.
R: For Giffen goods (inferior goods where income effect dominates substitution effect), demand rises when price rises — consumers can't afford other goods. For Veblen goods (luxury goods), higher price signals prestige — demand rises. Both violate the inverse price-demand relationship.
Ans: (A)

Case — Demand and Elasticity: A firm sells 100 units of a good at ₹50 each. When it reduces price to ₹40, it sells 140 units. Meanwhile, when income of consumers rises by 20%, demand for the good increases from 100 to 110 units at the same price.

Q1 (2M) Calculate Price Elasticity of Demand (PED).
Ans: PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) = (40/−10) × (50/100) = −4 × 0.5 = −2. |PED| = 2 > 1 → elastic demand.
Q2 (2M) Calculate Income Elasticity of Demand (YED).
Ans: YED = (%ΔQ/%ΔY) = (10%/20%) = 0.5. YED > 0 → normal good. 0 < YED < 1 → necessity good.
Q3 (1M) What should the firm do with price to increase total revenue?
Ans: Reduce price further — with elastic demand (PED > 1), a price fall increases total revenue (TR = P × Q; % increase in Q outweighs % fall in P).
SA (3M) Distinguish between 'change in demand' and 'change in quantity demanded'.
Ans: Change in quantity demanded: Movement along the same demand curve — caused ONLY by a change in the good's own price. Represented by extension (price ↓, quantity ↑) or contraction (price ↑, quantity ↓). Change in demand: Shift of the entire demand curve — caused by factors OTHER than price: income, price of related goods, tastes, expectations, population. Increase in demand = rightward shift; decrease = leftward shift. Key difference: one is a movement on the curve; the other is a shift of the curve.
LA (5M) Explain the Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility with a diagram. What are its limitations?
Ans: Law of DMU: As a consumer consumes more and more units of a good (other things remaining constant), the marginal utility (additional satisfaction from one more unit) eventually decreases. Example: First glass of water when thirsty → very high MU. Second glass → less. Third → even less. At some point MU = 0 (point of saturation). Beyond this, MU becomes negative. Diagram: X-axis = units consumed; Y-axis = Marginal Utility. MU curve slopes downward. TU curve rises but at a decreasing rate, reaches maximum when MU = 0, then falls when MU is negative. Limitations: (1) Does not apply to addictive goods (alcohol, cigarettes) — MU may initially rise. (2) Assumes utility can be measured cardinally (in utils) — not always realistic. (3) Assumes each unit is identical in quality. (4) Assumes no change in income, taste or related goods. (5) May not apply to goods consumed after long intervals or for rare goods (e.g. one painting).
🏭 Microeconomics — Production & Cost
Production Function · Returns to Factor · Cost Concepts · Revenue

📖 Core Theory

Production Function
Q = f(L, K). Relationship between inputs and output. Short run: one factor fixed. Long run: all factors variable. Returns to Factor (SR) vs Returns to Scale (LR).
Law of Variable Proportions
Short-run phenomenon. Stage I: TP, MP, AP all rising (underutilisation). Stage II: TP rises, MP falls (efficient stage). Stage III: TP falls, MP negative (overutilisation). Production in Stage II.
Cost Concepts
TC = TFC + TVC. AC = TC/Q. MC = change in TC / change in Q = change in TVC/change in Q. In LR: all costs variable, no fixed costs. AC and MC are U-shaped in SR.

🔑 Key Formulae — Production & Cost

MP = TP_n - TP_(n-1); AP = TP/L
TC = TFC + TVC; AC = TC/Q; MC = dTC/dQ = dTVC/dQ
TR = P × Q; AR = TR/Q = P; MR = TR_n - TR_(n-1)
Profit = TR - TC; Break-even when TR = TC (AR = AC)
Relationship: When MC < AC, AC falls; when MC > AC, AC rises; MC = AC at AC minimum

✏️ Worked Examples

Example 1 — Table Completion (Board 2022, 3M)

Q: TFC = Rs 60. TVC at 1,2,3 units = Rs 20, 50, 90. Find TC, AC, MC.

1
TC: 60+20=80; 60+50=110; 60+90=150
2
AC: 80/1=80; 110/2=55; 150/3=50
3
MC: at Q=1: 80-60=20; at Q=2: 110-80=30; at Q=3: 150-110=40
✅ TC: 80,110,150. AC: 80,55,50. MC: 20,30,40.

📝 Practice Questions — Production & Cost

MCQ 1 In the short run, which cost is always constant?
(A) Variable cost (B) Marginal cost (C) Fixed cost (D) Average cost
Ans: (C) Fixed cost
MCQ 2 When MC = AC, AC is at its:
(A) Minimum (B) Maximum (C) Increasing phase (D) Decreasing phase
Ans: (A) Minimum — MC cuts AC at AC's minimum point.
A-R 1 A: The Law of Variable Proportions operates in the short run.
R: In the short run, at least one factor (capital/land) is fixed. As more units of the variable factor (labour) are added to fixed factors, initially MP rises (increasing returns), then MP falls but remains positive (diminishing returns), then MP turns negative (negative returns).
Ans: (A)

Case — Cost Analysis: A firm's total fixed cost is ₹500. The variable costs for outputs 1–4 units are: ₹100, ₹180, ₹300, ₹500 respectively.

Q1 (2M) Calculate TC, TVC, MC for each output level.
Ans: TC = TFC + TVC: 600, 680, 800, 1000. MC: 100, 80, 120, 200 (change in TC for each extra unit). Note MC falls then rises — U-shape.
Q2 (2M) Calculate ATC and AVC at output = 3.
Ans: ATC = TC/Q = 800/3 = ₹266.67. AVC = TVC/Q = 300/3 = ₹100. AFC = ATC − AVC = ₹166.67.
Q3 (1M) Why does AFC always fall as output increases?
Ans: AFC = TFC/Q. Since TFC is constant, as Q increases, AFC must keep falling — fixed costs are spread over more units.
SA (3M) What is the relationship between Total Product, Marginal Product and Average Product?
Ans: (1) When MP > AP, AP is rising. When MP < AP, AP is falling. When MP = AP, AP is at maximum. (2) When MP is positive, TP rises. When MP = 0, TP is maximum. When MP is negative, TP falls. (3) MP curve cuts AP curve at its maximum point. TP curve: rises at increasing rate (stage 1), then rising at decreasing rate (stage 2), then falls (stage 3). AP first rises then falls. MP rises, reaches maximum before AP, then falls below AP.
LA (5M) Explain the concepts of Returns to Scale. How do they differ from the Law of Variable Proportions?
Ans: Returns to Scale (Long Run): When ALL factors are changed in the same proportion, the resulting change in output. Three types: (1) Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS): Output increases by a greater proportion than inputs — e.g., doubling inputs more than doubles output. Causes: specialisation, indivisibilities, technical economies. (2) Constant Returns to Scale (CRS): Output increases in exact proportion to inputs. (3) Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS): Output increases by a smaller proportion — e.g., doubling inputs less than doubles output. Causes: management difficulties, resource constraints. Difference from Variable Proportions: Law of Variable Proportions applies in the SHORT RUN (one factor fixed). Returns to Scale applies in the LONG RUN (all factors variable). Variable Proportions: sequential addition of variable factor to fixed factor. Returns to Scale: proportional change in ALL factors.
🏪 Microeconomics — Market Forms
Perfect Competition · Monopoly · Monopolistic Competition · Oligopoly

📖 Core Theory

Perfect Competition
Large buyers/sellers, homogeneous product, free entry/exit, perfect knowledge. Firm is price-taker: AR = MR = Price. SR equilibrium: MC = MR. Normal profit in LR (AR = AC).
Monopoly
Single seller, unique product, high barriers to entry. Firm is price-maker. AR > MR (MR falls faster). Equilibrium: MC = MR. Price discrimination possible. Supernormal profit possible even in LR.
Monopolistic Competition
Many sellers, differentiated products, easy entry/exit. Chamberlin. SR: supernormal profit; LR: normal profit (new firms enter → demand curve shifts left till AR = AC).

🔑 Market Form Comparison

Perfect Competition: P = AR = MR. Supernormal profit: AR > AC. Normal: AR = AC. Loss: AR < AC
Monopoly: AR > MR always. Equilibrium: MC = MR (profit maximisation)
Price elasticity and MR: MR = P(1 - 1/Ed). When Ed = 1, MR = 0; |Ed| > 1 → MR > 0

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Short Answer (Board 2023, 3M)

Q: Under perfect competition, at equilibrium, if AR = 50 and AC = 60, what type of profit does the firm earn? What will it do?

1
AR (Price) = 50 < AC = 60 → the firm is making a loss of Rs 10 per unit.
2
In SR, firm continues if it can cover variable costs (AR >= AVC). In the LR, some firms will exit the industry.
3
As firms exit, supply decreases, price rises, until remaining firms earn normal profit (AR = AC).
✅ Loss. SR: continue if AR >= AVC. LR: firms exit, price rises to restore normal profit.

📝 Practice Questions — Market Forms

MCQ 1 Under perfect competition, a firm is a 'price taker' because:
(A) Government fixes price (B) It is too small to influence market price (C) Other firms set the price (D) Demand curve is inelastic
Ans: (B)
MCQ 2 A monopoly firm's demand curve is:
(A) Horizontal (B) More elastic than competitive firm (C) The same as the market demand curve (D) Vertical
Ans: (C) — monopoly IS the entire market.
A-R 1 A: A monopolist always earns supernormal profits in the short run and long run.
R: A monopolist faces barriers to entry that prevent new firms from entering — so supernormal profits cannot be competed away, as they are in perfect competition. The monopolist can maintain P > AC indefinitely. However, even a monopolist can make losses in the short run if costs are very high or demand is very low.
Ans: (B) — Assertion is partially wrong (monopolist CAN make losses).
SA (3M) Compare Perfect Competition and Monopolistic Competition in terms of product, pricing, and long-run equilibrium.
Ans: Product: PC — homogeneous, identical; MC — differentiated (branding, packaging, quality). Pricing: PC — price = MR = AR (price taker); MC — P > MR (downward sloping demand, price maker). Long-run equilibrium: PC — normal profits only (P = AC); free entry eliminates supernormal profits completely. MC — normal profits only in long run (free entry eliminates supernormal profits), but P > MC (unlike PC). Efficiency: PC is productively and allocatively efficient; MC has excess capacity (firms don't produce at minimum AC).
⚖️ Microeconomics — Supply & Market Equilibrium
Law of Supply · Elasticity of Supply · Market Equilibrium · Price Ceiling & Floor

📖 Core Theory

Law of Supply
Direct relationship between price and quantity supplied (other things equal). Supply curve: upward sloping. Shifts: input prices, technology, expectations, number of sellers, govt policies.
Elasticity of Supply
Es = % change in Qs / % change in P. Perfectly elastic: horizontal (Es = infinity). Perfectly inelastic: vertical (Es = 0). Supply through origin: Es = 1. Elastic if supply curve cuts price axis; inelastic if cuts quantity axis.
Market Equilibrium
Qd = Qs. Equilibrium price clears market (no surplus, no shortage). Excess demand → price rises. Excess supply → price falls. Shifts in demand/supply change equilibrium price and quantity.

🔑 Formulae — Supply & Equilibrium

Es = (deltaQs/deltaP) × (P/Qs)
Equilibrium: Qd = Qs. Set demand and supply equations equal, solve for P.
Price Ceiling (below Pe): shortage = Qd - Qs (black market emerges)
Price Floor (above Pe): surplus = Qs - Qd (eg. Minimum Support Price for farmers)

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Numerical (Board 2022, 4M)

Q: Qd = 30 - 2P; Qs = 10 + 2P. Find equilibrium P and Q.

1
At equilibrium Qd = Qs: 30 - 2P = 10 + 2P
2
20 = 4P → P = 5
3
Q = 30 - 2(5) = 20. Check: Qs = 10 + 2(5) = 20. ✓
✅ Equilibrium: P = Rs 5, Q = 20 units.

📝 Practice Questions — Supply & Market Equilibrium

MCQ 1 Market equilibrium occurs when:
(A) Demand = Supply (B) Price = Average Cost (C) Revenue = Cost (D) Buyers = Sellers
Ans: (A) Demand = Supply
A-R 1 A: When supply increases (supply curve shifts right), equilibrium price falls and equilibrium quantity rises.
R: An increase in supply means more quantity is supplied at every price. At the old price, there is now a surplus (excess supply). Price falls until a new equilibrium is reached where quantity demanded equals the new (higher) quantity supplied — lower price, higher quantity.
Ans: (A)

Case — Market Equilibrium: In a hypothetical market: Demand: Qd = 100 − 5P. Supply: Qs = 20 + 3P.

Q1 (2M) Find the equilibrium price and quantity.
Ans: At equilibrium: Qd = Qs → 100 − 5P = 20 + 3P → 80 = 8P → P* = ₹10. Q* = 100 − 50 = 50 units.
Q2 (2M) If price is set at ₹12, identify the market situation and its consequence.
Ans: Qd = 100 − 60 = 40. Qs = 20 + 36 = 56. Qs > Qd → excess supply (surplus) = 16 units. Sellers will lower prices to clear the surplus → price will fall back to P* = ₹10.
SA (3M) Explain how a firm maximises profit in the short run under perfect competition using the MR-MC approach.
Ans: Under perfect competition, the firm is a price taker: P = AR = MR (constant, horizontal). Profit maximisation condition: MR = MC AND MC must be rising at that point. Logic: If MR > MC, producing one more unit adds more to revenue than cost → profit rises → produce more. If MR < MC, producing one more unit adds more to cost than revenue → profit falls → produce less. At MR = MC: profit is neither rising nor falling → maximum. The rising MC condition distinguishes maximum from minimum profit point.
🌐 Macroeconomics — National Income
GDP · GNP · NNP · NI · Circular Flow · Methods of Measurement

📖 Core Theory

GDP
Gross Domestic Product: market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's domestic territory in a year, regardless of who produces. GDP = C + I + G + (X - M).
GNP vs GDP
GNP = GDP + NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad). NFIA = factor income earned by residents abroad - factor income earned by non-residents in domestic territory.
National Income Aggregates
GNPMP - Depreciation = NNPMP. NNPMP - Net Indirect Tax = NNPFC (National Income). GDPMP = GNPMP - NFIA. Per capita income = NI / Population.

🔑 National Income Formulae

GNPMP = GDPMP + NFIA
NNPMP = GNPMP - Depreciation (CCA)
NNPFC (NI) = NNPMP - Net Indirect Taxes (NIT = Indirect Tax - Subsidy)
NDPMP = GDPMP - Depreciation; NDPFC = NDPMP - NIT
Value Added Method: GDPMP = Sum of GVA_MP of all sectors
GVA_MP = Value of Output - Intermediate Consumption

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Aggregate Calculation (Board 2023, 6M)

Q: GDPMP = 2000, Depreciation = 200, NIT = 150, NFIA = -50. Find GNPMP, NNPMP, and National Income.

1
GNPMP = GDPMP + NFIA = 2000 + (-50) = 1950
2
NNPMP = GNPMP - Depreciation = 1950 - 200 = 1750
3
NI (NNPFC) = NNPMP - NIT = 1750 - 150 = 1600 crore
✅ GNPMP = 1950; NNPMP = 1750; NI = Rs 1600 crore.

📝 Practice Questions — National Income

MCQ 1 GDP at Market Price = GDP at Factor Cost +
(A) Subsidies (B) Net Indirect Taxes (C) Depreciation (D) Net Factor Income from Abroad
Ans: (B) Net Indirect Taxes (indirect taxes − subsidies)
MCQ 2 Which of the following is NOT included in GDP?
(A) Government salaries (B) Sale of second-hand goods (C) Export of software (D) Construction of new houses
Ans: (B) Sale of second-hand goods — only first sale counts; resale does not create new value.
A-R 1 A: Net National Product (NNP) is always less than or equal to Gross National Product (GNP).
R: NNP = GNP − Depreciation. Depreciation (consumption of fixed capital) is always positive — it represents wear and tear on capital goods. Therefore subtracting it always makes NNP ≤ GNP. They are equal only when depreciation = 0, which is theoretical.
Ans: (A)

Case — GDP Calculation: From the data for Country X: Private final consumption = ₹800 cr; Government final consumption = ₹200 cr; Gross Investment = ₹150 cr; Depreciation = ₹30 cr; Exports = ₹120 cr; Imports = ₹90 cr; Net Factor Income from Abroad = ₹20 cr; Net Indirect Taxes = ₹40 cr.

Q1 (2M) Calculate GDP at Market Price.
Ans: GDP_MP = C + G + I + (X−M) = 800+200+150+(120−90) = 800+200+150+30 = ₹1180 cr
Q2 (2M) Calculate NNP at Factor Cost (National Income).
Ans: GNP_MP = GDP_MP + NFIA = 1180+20 = 1200. NNP_MP = GNP_MP − Depreciation = 1200−30 = 1170. NNP_FC = NNP_MP − Net Indirect Taxes = 1170−40 = ₹1130 cr
SA (3M) Explain the Income Method of measuring National Income. What are its precautions?
Ans: Income Method: Sums all factor incomes earned in production — wages (labour), rent (land), interest (capital), profit (enterprise). National Income = Compensation of Employees + Rent + Interest + Profit. Precautions (exclusions): (1) Transfer payments (pension, unemployment allowance) — not payment for any current production; exclude. (2) Income from sale of second-hand goods — not current production; exclude. (3) Windfall gains (lottery, gifts) — not factor incomes; exclude. (4) Illegal incomes — not included in official estimates. (5) Imputed rent of owner-occupied houses — INCLUDE (is factor income earned even if not paid in cash).
💰 Macroeconomics — Money & Banking
Money Supply · Commercial Banks · Credit Creation · Central Bank · RBI Functions

📖 Core Theory

Money Supply
M1 = Currency in circulation + Demand deposits. M2 = M1 + Post Office savings. M3 (Broad Money) = M1 + Time deposits. M4 = M3 + Post Office term deposits.
Credit Multiplier
Money Multiplier = 1/LRR (Legal Reserve Ratio). Total credit creation = Initial deposit / LRR. Banks create credit by keeping a fraction of deposits as reserves and lending the rest.
RBI (Central Bank)
Functions: Banker to government, banker's bank, note issuing authority, controller of credit (Monetary Policy). Instruments: CRR, SLR, Bank Rate, Repo Rate, Reverse Repo, Open Market Operations.

🔑 Money & Banking Formulae

Money Multiplier = 1/LRR; Total deposits = Initial deposit × (1/LRR)
If LRR = 10% and initial deposit = Rs 1000, total credit = 1000 × (1/0.10) = Rs 10,000
Repo Rate: rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks (raises rate → credit contracts)
CRR: cash reserves banks must keep with RBI as % of deposits
SLR: liquid assets banks must maintain as % of deposits (gold + approved securities)

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Credit Creation (Board 2022, 4M)

Q: A bank receives a fresh deposit of Rs 5,000. LRR = 20%. Calculate total deposit creation.

1
Money Multiplier = 1/LRR = 1/0.20 = 5
2
Total Deposit Creation = Initial Deposit × Money Multiplier = 5000 × 5 = Rs 25,000
3
Reserve kept = 20% of each deposit. Net credit created = 25,000 - 5,000 = Rs 20,000.
✅ Total deposits created = Rs 25,000; credit creation = Rs 20,000.

📝 Practice Questions — Money & Banking

MCQ 1 The Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) is maintained with:
(A) Commercial banks (B) Reserve Bank of India (C) SEBI (D) Finance Ministry
Ans: (B) Reserve Bank of India
MCQ 2 If CRR = 20%, a bank with ₹1000 of new deposits can create credit of:
(A) ₹200 (B) ₹800 (C) ₹4000 (D) ₹5000
Ans: (C) ₹4000 — Money Multiplier = 1/CRR = 1/0.2 = 5. Credit = 1000 × 4 = ₹4000 (deposit − reserve).
A-R 1 A: An increase in CRR reduces money supply in the economy.
R: When RBI raises CRR, banks must keep more cash with RBI. This reduces their lendable funds, reducing credit creation. Less credit = less money in circulation = money supply contracts. CRR is a quantitative tool of monetary policy used during inflation to reduce excess liquidity.
Ans: (A)
SA (3M) Explain the process of credit creation by commercial banks.
Ans: When a bank receives a primary deposit, it retains a fraction as reserve (CRR) and lends the rest. The borrower spends the loan; the recipient deposits it in a bank, which again keeps CRR and lends the rest. This chain continues — each loan becomes a deposit somewhere else. Total Credit Created = Initial Deposit × (1 − CRR) / CRR OR = Initial Deposit × Credit Multiplier − Initial Deposit. Example: Initial deposit ₹1000; CRR = 10%. Total deposits created = 1000 / 0.1 = ₹10,000. Credit created = ₹10,000 − ₹1,000 = ₹9,000. Limitation: People hold some cash outside banks; actual credit creation is less than theoretical maximum.
📈 Macroeconomics — Income Determination
Aggregate Demand · Aggregate Supply · Keynesian Cross · Multiplier · Deflationary & Inflationary Gap

📖 Core Theory

Aggregate Demand (AD)
AD = C + I + G + (X - M). Consumption function: C = a + bY (a = autonomous consumption; b = MPC). Investment is autonomous. AD shifts with changes in C, I, G, X, M.
Multiplier (K)
K = 1/(1-MPC) = 1/MPS. Shows by how much national income changes for a unit change in autonomous investment. deltaY = K × deltaI. Higher MPC → higher multiplier.
Output Gap
Deflationary gap: AD < AS at full employment (unemployment). Inflationary gap: AD > AS at full employment (inflation). Policy: fiscal policy (change G or T) to close gap.

🔑 Income Determination Formulae

C = a + bY; where b = MPC; MPS = 1 - MPC
Multiplier K = 1/(1-MPC) = 1/MPS
deltaY = K × deltaI (change in income = multiplier × change in investment)
Equilibrium: Y = AD, i.e., Y = C + I; or S = I
Deflationary gap = AS at FE - AD at FE (government must increase G or cut T)

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Multiplier Calculation (Board 2023, 4M)

Q: MPC = 0.75. Investment increases by Rs 400 crore. Calculate change in national income.

1
MPS = 1 - MPC = 1 - 0.75 = 0.25
2
Multiplier K = 1/MPS = 1/0.25 = 4
3
deltaY = K × deltaI = 4 × 400 = Rs 1600 crore
✅ National income rises by Rs 1600 crore.

📝 Practice Questions — Income Determination

MCQ 1 The Keynesian multiplier (k) formula is:
(A) 1/MPC (B) 1/(1−MPS) (C) 1/MPS (D) Both B and C
Ans: (D) — k = 1/MPS = 1/(1−MPC). Since MPC + MPS = 1.
MCQ 2 If MPC = 0.8, what is the value of the multiplier?
(A) 0.8 (B) 1.25 (C) 4 (D) 5
Ans: (D) 5 — k = 1/(1−0.8) = 1/0.2 = 5.
A-R 1 A: Deflationary gap occurs when Aggregate Demand is less than Aggregate Supply at full employment.
R: At full employment output, if AD < AS (full employment AS), there is insufficient demand — the economy produces below potential. This gap leads to unemployment (cyclical). To close it, government can increase G (government expenditure) or cut taxes to boost AD — expansionary fiscal policy.
Ans: (A)

Case — Multiplier Effect: An economy has MPC = 0.75. The government increases investment by ₹500 crore.

Q1 (1M) Calculate the investment multiplier.
Ans: k = 1/(1−MPC) = 1/(1−0.75) = 1/0.25 = 4
Q2 (2M) Calculate the total increase in national income.
Ans: ΔY = k × ΔI = 4 × 500 = ₹2000 crore
Q3 (2M) Explain how the initial ₹500 cr leads to ₹2000 cr increase.
Ans: Round 1: ₹500 cr invested → ₹500 cr income → saved ₹125, spent ₹375. Round 2: ₹375 cr spent → new income → ₹93.75 saved, ₹281.25 spent. This continues. Sum of all rounds = 500/(1−0.75) = ₹2000 cr. Each rupee earned leads to further spending by MPC, generating a chain reaction of income.
SA (3M) Differentiate between autonomous investment and induced investment.
Ans: Autonomous Investment: Does not depend on income level — it is made regardless of current income or profit. Examples: government infrastructure spending, R&D investment. In Keynesian model, shown as horizontal line. Induced Investment: Depends on level of income and output — when income rises, investment demand rises (businesses need more capacity). Represented by a line rising with income. In simple Keynesian model: We assume only autonomous investment for simplicity. In more complex models, induced investment creates the accelerator effect.
🏛️ Macroeconomics — Government Budget
Budget Components · Revenue & Capital · Fiscal Deficit · Primary Deficit

📖 Core Theory

Government Budget
Annual statement of estimated receipts and expenditures. Revenue Budget: revenue receipts + revenue expenditure. Capital Budget: capital receipts + capital expenditure.
Budget Deficits
Revenue Deficit = Rev Expenditure - Rev Receipts. Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure - Total Receipts (excl. borrowings). Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments.
Budget Objectives
Reallocation of resources, redistribution of income, economic stability, management of PSUs. Fiscal policy uses government expenditure and taxation to achieve macroeconomic goals.

🔑 Budget Deficit Formulae

Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure - Revenue Receipts
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure - (Revenue Receipts + Non-Debt Capital Receipts)
Fiscal Deficit = Borrowings (government must borrow to cover this gap)
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Net Interest Payments
If Primary Deficit = 0: government borrowing ONLY to pay interest on past debt

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — Deficit Calculation (Board 2022, 4M)

Q: Revenue Expenditure = 500; Capital Expenditure = 200; Revenue Receipts = 400; Capital Receipts (non-debt) = 100; Interest Payments = 60. Find Fiscal Deficit and Primary Deficit.

1
Total Expenditure = 500 + 200 = 700; Non-Debt Receipts = 400 + 100 = 500
2
Fiscal Deficit = 700 - 500 = Rs 200 crore
3
Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments = 200 - 60 = Rs 140 crore
✅ Fiscal Deficit = Rs 200 crore; Primary Deficit = Rs 140 crore.

Ans: (A)
SA (3M) What are the objectives of government budget? Explain with examples.
Ans: (1) Resource Allocation: Government provides public goods (defence, roads, streetlights) not provided by the private sector due to free rider problem. Budget allocates funds to these. (2) Redistribution of Income: Progressive taxation (rich pay higher %) and welfare expenditure (subsidies, MGNREGA, PDS) reduce income inequality — transfers from rich to poor. (3) Economic Stability: Expansionary budget during recession (↑G, ↓T) to boost demand; contractionary during inflation (↓G, ↑T) to reduce demand — stabilises business cycle. (4) Economic Growth: Capital expenditure on infrastructure (railways, ports, power) creates productive capacity for long-term growth.
🌏 Macroeconomics — Balance of Payments
BOP Components · Current Account · Capital Account · Foreign Exchange

📖 Core Theory

Balance of Payments (BOP)
Systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world in a year. Always balances in accounting sense. Components: Current Account + Capital Account.
Current Account
Exports/Imports of goods (Balance of Trade), exports/imports of services (invisibles: banking, tourism, software), and transfer payments (remittances, gifts). BOT = Export of goods - Import of goods.
Foreign Exchange Rate
Price of one currency in terms of another. Fixed exchange rate: government/RBI maintains. Flexible/floating: demand-supply determines. Depreciation vs Devaluation (depreciation = market; devaluation = government decision).

🔑 BOP & Forex

BOT = Exports of goods - Imports of goods (Trade Balance)
Current Account Balance = BOT + Invisibles (services, transfers)
BOP = Current Account + Capital Account + Official Reserves = 0
Demand for forex: imports, foreign travel, investments abroad → dollar demand → rupee depreciates
Supply of forex: exports, FDI inflows, NRI remittances → dollar supply → rupee appreciates

✏️ Worked Examples

Example — BOP Distinction (Board 2023, 4M)

Q: Distinguish between Balance of Trade (BOT) and Balance of Payments (BOP). Which is broader?

1
BOT covers only visible items — export and import of goods (merchandise). A surplus means exports > imports.
2
BOP is broader — covers visible items (BOT) + invisible items (services, transfers) + capital account (FDI, FII, loans, borrowings).
3
BOP always balances in accounting sense (credit = debit including official reserves). BOT may show surplus or deficit.
✅ BOP is broader (includes BOT + services + capital flows). BOP always balances; BOT may not.

2024 5 Marks Consumer Equilibrium
Explain the Law of Demand with the help of a demand schedule and demand curve. State any two exceptions to the law.
Law of Demand: Ceteris paribus, as price rises, quantity demanded falls (inverse relationship).

Demand Schedule: Price (₹): 10→8→6→4; Qty: 100→200→300→400
Demand Curve: Downward sloping — as P↓, Qd↑

Exceptions (Giffen goods & others):
1. Giffen Goods — inferior goods where income effect dominates substitution effect (more demanded at higher price)
2. Status/Prestige goods (Veblen goods) — luxury items where demand rises with price (higher price signals quality)
2023 4 Marks Production & Cost
Explain the Law of Variable Proportions with the help of a diagram. State the causes for the operation of each phase.
Law of Variable Proportions: As variable input is added to fixed input, MP first rises, then falls, then becomes negative.

Phase I (Increasing returns): MP rises — specialisation and better use of fixed factor
Phase II (Diminishing returns): MP falls but positive — fixed factor becomes scarce relative to variable
Phase III (Negative returns): MP negative — too many variable inputs, overcrowding, inefficiency

Rational producer operates in Phase II (MP positive but falling).

2024 5 Marks National Income
Explain the Expenditure Method of calculating National Income. What are its main components?
Expenditure Method: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

C — Private Final Consumption Expenditure (households)
I — Gross Domestic Capital Formation (investment by firms)
G — Government Final Consumption Expenditure
X − M — Net Exports (Exports minus Imports)

NNP at FC = NNP at MP − Net Indirect Taxes
2023 4 Marks Money & Banking
Explain the credit creation process by commercial banks. Give a numerical example.
Credit Creation: Banks keep only a fraction (CRR) of deposits as reserve and lend the rest. Each loan becomes a deposit elsewhere, multiplying money supply.

Formula: Money Multiplier = 1 / CRR
Example: Initial deposit ₹1,000; CRR = 10%
Money Multiplier = 1/0.10 = 10
Total credit created = ₹1,000 × 10 = ₹10,000

Process: Deposit → Reserve kept → Balance lent → New deposit elsewhere → Cycle repeats
2023 5 Marks Government Budget
Distinguish between Revenue Deficit and Fiscal Deficit. What are the implications of a large fiscal deficit?
Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts (shows govt. dissaving)
Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts excluding borrowings (shows total borrowing requirement)

Implications of large fiscal deficit:
1. Inflationary pressure — government borrowing increases money supply
2. Crowding out private investment — govt. competes for loanable funds, raises interest rates
3. Debt trap — future revenue used for interest payments
4. Exchange rate depreciation — foreign investors lose confidence
⚠️ Mistake Bank Common Errors

Movement Along vs Shift of Demand Curve

Most common error in consumer behaviour questions.

❌ WRONG: "When income increases, there is a movement along the demand curve."
✅ CORRECT: A change in price causes movement along the demand curve. A change in income (or any other factor) causes a SHIFT of the demand curve. Only price → movement; everything else → shift.

GDP vs GNP vs NNP Confusion

Students mix up the aggregates and their adjustments.

❌ WRONG: "GNP = GDP + Depreciation"
✅ CORRECT: GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA). NNP = GNP − Depreciation (consumption of fixed capital). Depreciation is subtracted, not added.

Market Price vs Factor Cost

The NIT (Net Indirect Tax) adjustment trips students every year.

❌ WRONG: "GDP at FC = GDP at MP + Net Indirect Taxes"
✅ CORRECT: GDP at FC = GDP at MP − Net Indirect Taxes. To go from MP to FC, subtract NIT (because market price includes taxes and excludes subsidies). Memory: FC = MP − NIT.

Monopoly: Writing AR = MR

A classic error that loses marks in market forms questions.

❌ WRONG: "Under monopoly, AR = MR because the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve."
✅ CORRECT: Under monopoly, AR > MR (MR lies below AR). AR = MR only in Perfect Competition. Under monopoly, to sell more, price (AR) must be reduced for all units, so MR falls faster than AR.

Inflationary Gap vs Deflationary Gap Direction

Students often confuse which gap requires which policy.

❌ WRONG: "During inflationary gap, the government should increase expenditure to boost output."
✅ CORRECT: Inflationary gap (AD > AS at full employment) → government should REDUCE expenditure / increase taxes (contractionary fiscal policy). Deflationary gap → expansionary policy.

Repo Rate vs Reverse Repo Rate Confusion

Students swap the direction of borrowing.

❌ WRONG: "Repo rate is the rate at which commercial banks lend to RBI."
✅ CORRECT: Repo Rate = rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks (for short term, against securities). Reverse Repo Rate = rate at which RBI borrows from commercial banks. Repo ↑ → credit costlier → money supply ↓.

Transfer Payments Included in National Income

Students wrongly add pension, scholarships in National Income calculation.

❌ WRONG: "Old-age pension and unemployment allowance are factor incomes and included in GDP."
✅ CORRECT: Transfer payments (pension, scholarship, dole) are NOT included in National Income — no goods/services are produced in return. They are one-sided payments with no corresponding production.

Aggregate Demand Components

Students confuse components of AD in a two-sector economy.

❌ WRONG: "AD = C + I + G + (X−M) in a two-sector economy."
✅ CORRECT: Two-sector (households + firms): AD = C + I. Three-sector (+government): AD = C + I + G. Four-sector (+foreign): AD = C + I + G + (X−M). Add components as sectors increase.

🎯 Examiner's Eye Scoring Tips

🎯 Tip 1 — Always Draw the Diagram

In Microeconomics, every 4- or 5-mark question on demand, supply, equilibrium, or market forms carries 1–2 diagram marks. A correctly labelled diagram with axes (Price on Y, Quantity on X), curve label, and equilibrium point E earns marks even if your written explanation is average.

🎯 Tip 2 — National Income: Always Show the Formula First

Before any national income calculation, write the formula (e.g., GDP at MP = C + I + G + NX), then substitute values step by step. Examiners award step marks. A wrong final figure with correct steps still scores 3 out of 4.

🎯 Tip 3 — Distinguish Clearly Using Tabular Format

For "differentiate between" questions (Revenue Deficit vs Fiscal Deficit, Repo vs Reverse Repo), always use a 3-column table: Basis | Term 1 | Term 2. List at least 3 bases: Meaning, Formula, Implications. This is faster and scores more than paragraphs.

🎯 Tip 4 — Money Multiplier — Show the Division

Write the formula MM = 1/CRR, then substitute, then compute. Even for 1-mark MCQs in credit creation, showing the formula proves you understand the concept. Examiners appreciate formula-first answers.

🎯 Tip 5 — State "Ceteris Paribus" in Law of Demand/Supply

Every statement of the Law of Demand or Supply must include "ceteris paribus" (other things being equal). Missing this phrase costs half a mark in 2-mark answers. It signals that the law holds only when other determinants are constant.

🎯 Tip 6 — BOP: Current vs Capital Account

Students mix up which transactions go in Current Account vs Capital Account. Rule: Current = trade in goods (visible), services (invisible), transfer payments, income. Capital = financial assets — investment, loans, banking capital. Visible items in Current Account is the most tested distinction.

🎯 Tip 7 — Budget Deficit Numericals: Correct Order of Subtraction

Revenue Deficit → Fiscal Deficit → Primary Deficit form a chain. Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments. Know this chain. In numericals, derive each one from the previous in this order to avoid errors.

🎯 Tip 8 — Income Determination: Multiplier Formula

Multiplier K = 1/(1−MPC) = 1/MPS. Change in income = K × Change in investment. Always write both formulas and state MPS = 1 − MPC. Examiners mark formula, substitution, and answer as three separate marks.

📝 Practice Questions — Balance of Payments & Exchange Rate

MCQ 1 Which of the following is recorded in the Current Account of BOP?
(A) FDI inflows (B) Portfolio Investment (C) Export of goods (D) External Commercial Borrowings)
Ans: (C) Export of goods
MCQ 2 Depreciation of Indian Rupee means:
(A) Rupee buys more dollars (B) Rupee buys fewer dollars (C) Dollar weakens (D) RBI reduces interest rates
Ans: (B) Rupee buys fewer dollars — ₹ loses value against $.
A-R 1 A: A Current Account Deficit (CAD) means a country is importing more than it is exporting.
R: The Current Account includes trade in goods (visible), services (invisible), income flows, and current transfers. A deficit means outflows exceed inflows — the country buys more from the world than it sells. India persistently runs a CAD partly due to high oil and gold imports.
Ans: (A)

Case — Exchange Rate: India's exchange rate with the US was ₹70/$1 in 2019. Due to higher US interest rates attracting dollar investment out of India, the rate changed to ₹83/$1 by 2023.

Q1 (1M) Has the rupee appreciated or depreciated? By what percentage?
Ans: Depreciated. % depreciation = (83−70)/70 × 100 = 18.6%
Q2 (2M) What is the effect of this change on Indian exporters and importers?
Ans: Exporters benefit — they receive dollars abroad, which now convert to MORE rupees (competitive advantage, exports become cheaper for foreign buyers). Importers suffer — they pay more rupees for the same dollar-priced goods (oil, machinery); import costs rise, contributing to inflation.
Q3 (2M) How does the BOP automatically adjust with a flexible exchange rate?
Ans: With a CAD, rupee depreciates → exports become cheaper (↑), imports more expensive (↓) → trade deficit narrows → BOP tends to correct itself. This is the automatic adjustment mechanism of a flexible (floating) exchange rate system.
LA (5M) Explain the components of the Balance of Payments (BOP) account. Why is BOP always in balance in accounting terms?
Ans: BOP Components: (1) Current Account: (a) Trade in Goods (Merchandise): exports − imports (visible trade balance). (b) Trade in Services: software, tourism, transport (invisible trade balance). (c) Income: dividends, wages received by residents from abroad. (d) Current Transfers: remittances, foreign aid. Current Account Balance = sum of above. (2) Capital Account: (a) Foreign Direct Investment (FDI). (b) Portfolio Investment (FII/FPI). (c) External Commercial Borrowings. (d) Official reserves (RBI forex interventions). Capital Account Balance = net capital inflows. (3) Errors and Omissions: Statistical discrepancy. Why BOP = 0 always: BOP = Current Account + Capital Account + Errors & Omissions = 0. Every international transaction has two sides (double-entry). If current account shows a deficit (importing more), this must be financed — either by capital inflows (borrowing, FDI) or by running down reserves. So the overall account always balances. A "BOP deficit/surplus" refers only to one component (usually current account), not the whole account.
