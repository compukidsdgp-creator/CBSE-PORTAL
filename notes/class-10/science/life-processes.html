At a glance

Living things must keep repairing and running their bodies even when they look still, and this takes energy. The processes that maintain life — nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion — are the life processes. This chapter compares how plants and humans carry out each one: photosynthesis and digestion, aerobic and anaerobic respiration, the heart and double circulation, xylem and phloem, the nephron and urine formation, and how plants get rid of wastes.

  • Photosynthesis: the equation, the three events, stomata, and the starch-test activities.
  • Digestion: the role of saliva, gastric juice, bile, pancreatic juice and villi.
  • The three pathways of glucose breakdown and why aquatic animals breathe faster.
  • The heart's four chambers and double circulation; arteries, veins, capillaries; lymph.
  • Structure of the nephron, how urine forms, and transpiration and translocation in plants.

Concepts, step by step

What are life processes?

The organised structures of living things are constantly being broken down by the environment, so the body must keep repairing itself, even during sleep — molecular movement is a basic sign of life. This needs energy from food (nutrition), released by breaking food down with oxygen (respiration). In multicellular organisms food and oxygen must reach every cell (transportation), and harmful by-products must be removed (excretion).

Autotrophic nutrition: photosynthesis

Autotrophs (green plants and some bacteria) make their own food from carbon dioxide and water using sunlight. Photosynthesis takes place mainly in the chloroplasts of leaf cells, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll. The carbohydrate made is used for energy, and the extra is stored as starch.

6CO₂ + 12H₂O sunlight, chlorophyll→ C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O

Three events occur: (i) absorption of light energy by chlorophyll; (ii) conversion of light energy to chemical energy and splitting of water into hydrogen and oxygen; (iii) reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates. Desert plants take in CO₂ at night and use it in the day.

Gases are exchanged through stomata, tiny pores on the leaf surface. Two guard cells surround each pore: when water flows into them they swell and the pore opens; when they lose water it closes. Stomata close when CO₂ is not needed, to cut water loss. Plants take nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and magnesium from the soil.

Activity — chlorophyll is necessary: a variegated-leaf plant is kept in the dark for three days (to use up stored starch), then in sunlight for about six hours. A leaf is boiled in alcohol to remove chlorophyll and tested with iodine. Only the parts that were green turn blue-black, showing starch formed only where chlorophyll was present.

Activity — CO₂ is necessary: two de-starched potted plants are placed under bell jars sealed with vaseline, one with a watch glass of potassium hydroxide (which absorbs CO₂). After a few hours in sunlight, the leaf from the jar with KOH shows no starch, while the other does.

Heterotrophic nutrition

Heterotrophs depend directly or indirectly on autotrophs. Saprophytes such as fungi (bread moulds, yeast, mushrooms) break down food outside the body and then absorb it. Parasites such as cuscuta (amar-bel), ticks, lice, leeches and tapeworms take nutrition from a living host without killing it.

In single-celled organisms food enters through the whole surface. Amoeba pushes out finger-like pseudopodia that surround a food particle to form a food vacuole, in which the food is digested and absorbed. Paramoecium has a definite shape and takes in food at a specific spot, moved there by beating cilia.

Nutrition in human beings

The alimentary canal runs from the mouth to the anus, each region specialised for one job.

  • Mouth: teeth crush food, the tongue mixes it, and saliva wets it. Saliva contains the enzyme salivary amylase, which breaks starch into sugar.
  • Oesophagus: rhythmic contraction of its muscles, called peristalsis, pushes food along.
  • Stomach: gastric glands release hydrochloric acid (makes the medium acidic, which activates pepsin), the protein-digesting enzyme pepsin, and mucus (protects the stomach lining from the acid). A sphincter muscle lets food into the small intestine a little at a time.
  • Small intestine: the longest part of the canal; it is longer in herbivores, which digest cellulose. The liver secretes bile, which makes the food alkaline and whose salts break fat into small globules (emulsification). The pancreas secretes trypsin (proteins) and lipase (emulsified fats). The intestinal wall's glands finally convert proteins to amino acids, complex carbohydrates to glucose, and fats to fatty acids and glycerol.
  • Villi: finger-like projections of the inner lining greatly increase the surface area for absorption and are richly supplied with blood vessels.
  • Large intestine: absorbs excess water; the rest leaves through the anus, controlled by the anal sphincter.

Activity — saliva digests starch: two test tubes of starch solution are taken and saliva is added to one. After 20–30 minutes, iodine turns the tube without saliva blue-black, while the tube with saliva shows no blue-black colour, because the starch has been digested.

Dental caries: bacteria act on sugars and produce acids that soften or demineralise the enamel. Brushing after eating removes the dental plaque of bacteria and food.

Respiration

Glucose is first broken down in the cytoplasm into pyruvate (a three-carbon molecule). What happens next depends on oxygen:

Glucose (6C) cytoplasm→ Pyruvate (3C) + energy

  • No oxygen (yeast, fermentation): pyruvate → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy.
  • Lack of oxygen (our muscle cells): pyruvate → lactic acid + energy. Its build-up causes muscle cramps.
  • With oxygen (in mitochondria): pyruvate → carbon dioxide + water + energy. This releases far more energy.

The released energy is stored as ATP, the energy currency of the cell. Breaking its terminal phosphate bond releases 30.5 kJ/mol for muscle contraction, nerve impulses and other work.

Activities: exhaled air turns lime water milky faster than ordinary air, so it has more CO₂. Sugar solution with yeast also turns lime water milky — fermentation releases CO₂.

Plants exchange gases by diffusion through stomata, lenticels and root surfaces. At night CO₂ is given out; in the day the CO₂ from respiration is used for photosynthesis and oxygen is released. Aquatic animals use oxygen dissolved in water through gills; since this is much lower than in air, they breathe faster than terrestrial animals.

Human respiratory system: air is filtered by hairs and mucus in the nostrils, then passes through the pharynx, larynx and trachea, which has rings of cartilage so it does not collapse. It divides into bronchi and bronchioles, ending in balloon-like alveoli surrounded by blood capillaries. Breathing in, the ribs lift and the diaphragm flattens, enlarging the chest cavity so air rushes in. Alveoli give about 80 m² of surface. Haemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen; CO₂, being more soluble, travels mostly dissolved in blood.

Transportation in human beings

Blood has a fluid matrix, plasma, which carries food, CO₂ and nitrogenous wastes in dissolved form, along with red blood cells (oxygen), white blood cells and platelets, which help blood clot at an injury.

The heart is a muscular pump with four chambers, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood never mix. Oxygenated blood from the lungs enters the left atrium, passes to the left ventricle and is pumped to the body through the aorta. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium through the venae cavae, passes to the right ventricle and is pumped to the lungs. Ventricles have thicker walls than atria as they pump blood to distant organs. Valves prevent backflow. Blood passes through the heart twice in one cycle — double circulation — giving the body a highly efficient oxygen supply, which birds and mammals need to maintain body temperature. Fishes have a two-chambered heart; amphibians and many reptiles have three chambers.

  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart; they have thick, elastic walls as blood is under high pressure.
  • Veins bring blood back; they have thinner walls and valves.
  • Capillaries have walls one cell thick, where exchange with tissues happens.

Blood pressure is measured with a sphygmomanometer; normal is about 120 mm Hg systolic and 80 mm Hg diastolic. Hypertension is caused by constricted arterioles and can rupture an artery.

Lymph, or tissue fluid, is plasma that escapes through capillary pores into intercellular spaces. It is colourless with less protein. It carries digested and absorbed fat from the intestine and drains excess fluid back into the blood.

Transportation in plants

Xylem carries water and minerals from the soil; phloem carries food made in the leaves. Root cells take up ions actively, creating a concentration difference that draws water from the soil into the root (root pressure, important at night). In tall plants the main force is transpiration — loss of water vapour from leaves — which creates a suction, the transpiration pull, that lifts water up the xylem in the day.

Activity: of two similar pots, the one with a plant, covered in plastic, shows water droplets on the plastic, while the pot with only a stick does not — water was lost from the leaves.

Translocation moves sucrose, amino acids and other substances through phloem sieve tubes, upwards and downwards, using energy from ATP. Sucrose loaded into phloem raises its osmotic pressure; water enters, and the pressure pushes food to tissues with lower pressure — in spring, sugar stored in roots or stems moves to the buds.

Excretion

Excretion removes harmful metabolic wastes. The human excretory system has two kidneys, two ureters, a urinary bladder and a urethra. Each kidney contains many filtering units called nephrons. A nephron has a cup-shaped Bowman's capsule enclosing a knot of capillaries, the glomerulus, followed by a long tubule that drains into a collecting duct.

  1. Filtration: blood from the renal artery is filtered in the glomerulus; glucose, amino acids, salts, water and urea enter the tubule.
  2. Selective reabsorption: useful substances and a major amount of water are taken back by capillaries around the tubule, depending on the body's excess water.
  3. The remaining urine passes through the ureter to the bladder, which stores it until it is released through the urethra under nervous control.

If the kidneys fail, an artificial kidney (haemodialysis) filters blood through semi-permeable tubes in dialysing fluid, but with no reabsorption.

Excretion in plants: oxygen and CO₂ diffuse out through stomata; excess water is lost by transpiration. Other wastes are stored in leaves that fall off, in cell vacuoles, as resins and gums in old xylem, or excreted into the soil.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

What are the functions of the villi in the small intestine? (2 marks)

Villi are finger-like projections of the inner lining of the small intestine. (1) They greatly increase the surface area for absorption of digested food. (2) They are richly supplied with blood vessels, which carry the absorbed food to every cell of the body.

Why is the rate of breathing in aquatic organisms much faster than in terrestrial organisms? (2 marks)

Aquatic organisms use oxygen dissolved in water, and the amount of dissolved oxygen is much lower than the amount of oxygen in air. To get enough oxygen they must pass water over their gills rapidly, so they breathe faster.

Describe, with a flow chart, the breakdown of glucose by various pathways. (3 marks)
  1. In the cytoplasm, glucose (6 carbons) is broken into pyruvate (3 carbons) + energy. This step is common to all pathways.
  2. Absence of oxygen (yeast): pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂ + energy (fermentation).
  3. Lack of oxygen (muscle cells): pyruvate → lactic acid + energy.
  4. Presence of oxygen (mitochondria): pyruvate → CO₂ + H₂O + a large amount of energy.

Glucose → (cytoplasm) → Pyruvate + energy → (i) ethanol + CO₂ + energy, (ii) lactic acid + energy, or (iii) CO₂ + H₂O + energy.

Describe an experiment to show that carbon dioxide is necessary for photosynthesis. (3 marks)
  1. Take two healthy potted plants of about the same size and keep them in the dark for three days so that their leaves have no starch.
  2. Place each on a glass plate and cover with a bell jar, sealing the bottom with vaseline to make it airtight. Put a watch glass of potassium hydroxide beside one plant — KOH absorbs CO₂.
  3. Keep both in sunlight for about two hours, then pluck a leaf from each, boil it in alcohol to remove chlorophyll, wash and test with iodine.

The leaf from the plant without KOH turns blue-black (starch present); the leaf from the plant with KOH does not. Since that plant had no CO₂, it could not photosynthesise — so CO₂ is necessary for photosynthesis.

How is water transported from the roots to the top of a tall tree? (3 marks)
  1. Root cells in contact with soil take up ions actively. This creates a concentration difference between root and soil, so water moves into the root and on into the xylem, creating a steady push (root pressure) — important at night.
  2. Xylem vessels and tracheids of roots, stems and leaves are interconnected, forming a continuous water column.
  3. Water evaporates from leaf cells through stomata (transpiration). Water from the xylem replaces it, creating a suction — the transpiration pull — which is the main force lifting water in the day.

Water rises mainly by transpiration pull during the day, helped by root pressure at night.

Explain double circulation in human beings. Why is it necessary? (3 marks)
  1. Deoxygenated blood from the body enters the right atrium, passes to the right ventricle and is pumped through the pulmonary artery to the lungs, where it takes up oxygen.
  2. Oxygenated blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, passes to the left ventricle and is pumped through the aorta to the body.
  3. So blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle — double circulation.

Separating oxygenated from deoxygenated blood gives the body a highly efficient supply of oxygen. This is needed by birds and mammals, which use a lot of energy to maintain a constant body temperature.

(a) Describe the process of digestion of food in the stomach and small intestine. (b) Name the gland that secretes bile and state two functions of bile. (5 marks)

(a) Stomach — gastric glands in the wall release gastric juice containing:

  • Hydrochloric acid: creates the acidic medium needed for pepsin to act.
  • Pepsin: begins the digestion of proteins.
  • Mucus: protects the inner lining from the acid.

A sphincter muscle releases food into the small intestine in small amounts.

Small intestine — receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas. Pancreatic trypsin digests proteins and lipase digests emulsified fats. Glands in the intestinal wall secrete intestinal juice that completes digestion: proteins → amino acids, complex carbohydrates → glucose, fats → fatty acids and glycerol. The villi then absorb the digested food into the blood.

(b) Bile is secreted by the liver (stored in the gall bladder). Functions: (1) it makes the acidic food from the stomach alkaline so that pancreatic enzymes can act; (2) bile salts break large fat globules into smaller ones (emulsification), making lipase more effective.

Draw a labelled diagram of a nephron. Explain how urine is produced and how its amount is regulated. (5 marks)

Diagram: see Fig. 5 — label Bowman's capsule, glomerulus, tubule, collecting duct, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein.

  1. Blood enters the kidney through the renal artery, which branches into capillaries forming the glomerulus, enclosed by the cup-shaped Bowman's capsule.
  2. Filtration: water, glucose, amino acids, salts and nitrogenous wastes such as urea are filtered from the blood into the capsule.
  3. Selective reabsorption: as the filtrate passes along the tubule, useful substances — glucose, amino acids, salts and a major amount of water — are reabsorbed into the surrounding capillaries.
  4. The remaining fluid is urine, which flows into the collecting duct, then through the ureter to the urinary bladder.

The amount of water reabsorbed depends on how much excess water there is in the body and on how much dissolved waste must be removed. With little water in the body, more is reabsorbed and urine is concentrated; with excess water, less is reabsorbed and more dilute urine is produced.

Quick revision

  1. Life processes: nutrition, respiration, transportation, excretion — all need energy from food.
  2. Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O; extra glucose is stored as starch.
  3. Three events: light absorbed by chlorophyll → light to chemical energy and water split → CO₂ reduced to carbohydrate.
  4. Guard cells swell with water → stoma opens; lose water → stoma closes.
  5. Amoeba: pseudopodia → food vacuole. Paramoecium: cilia move food to a fixed spot.
  6. Saliva: salivary amylase, starch → sugar. Stomach: HCl, pepsin, mucus.
  7. Bile (liver) makes food alkaline and emulsifies fats; pancreas gives trypsin and lipase.
  8. Villi increase the surface area for absorption; large intestine absorbs water.
  9. Glucose → pyruvate (cytoplasm); then ethanol + CO₂ (yeast), lactic acid (muscles) or CO₂ + H₂O (mitochondria).
  10. ATP is the energy currency: 30.5 kJ/mol from its terminal phosphate.
  11. Aquatic animals breathe faster because dissolved oxygen is low; alveoli give about 80 m² for gas exchange.
  12. Four-chambered heart keeps blood separate; double circulation: body → right side → lungs → left side → body.
  13. Arteries: thick elastic walls; veins: thin walls with valves; capillaries: one cell thick. Normal BP ≈ 120/80 mm Hg.
  14. Lymph carries absorbed fat and returns tissue fluid to the blood.
  15. Xylem: water by transpiration pull (day) and root pressure (night). Phloem: translocation using ATP.
  16. Nephron: glomerulus + Bowman's capsule → tubule → collecting duct; filtration then selective reabsorption.
