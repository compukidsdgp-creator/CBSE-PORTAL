Metals and Non-metals

At a glance

Metals are usually hard, shiny, malleable, ductile and good conductors; non-metals are mostly the opposite. Chemically, metals lose electrons to form positive ions and basic oxides, while non-metals gain electrons and form acidic oxides. Arranging metals by how readily they react gives the reactivity series, which explains displacement reactions and decides how each metal is extracted from its ore. The chapter ends with corrosion and the ways we prevent it.

  • Write equations for metals reacting with oxygen, water/steam, dilute acids and salt solutions; explain amphoteric oxides.
  • Use the reactivity series to predict displacement reactions.
  • Show the formation of NaCl and MgCl₂ by electron-dot structures and list properties of ionic compounds.
  • Explain roasting, calcination, reduction (carbon, thermit), electrolytic reduction and electrolytic refining of copper.
  • Describe the experiment on conditions for rusting and methods to prevent corrosion; name common alloys.

Concepts, step by step

Physical properties of metals

Pure metals have a shining surface — metallic lustre (seen when iron, copper or magnesium is rubbed with sandpaper) — and are generally hard, though hardness varies.

  • Malleability: metals can be beaten into thin sheets. Gold and silver are the most malleable.
  • Ductility: metals can be drawn into thin wires. Gold is the most ductile — one gram of gold can be drawn into a wire about 2 km long.
  • Conduction of heat: wax on one end of a copper or aluminium wire melts when the other end is heated. Silver and copper are the best conductors.
  • Conduction of electricity: a bulb in a circuit glows when a metal is placed in the gap; this is why wires are coated with PVC or rubber.
  • Sonority: metals produce a ringing sound when struck — that is why school bells are made of metal.

Exceptions: mercury is liquid at room temperature; gallium and caesium melt on the palm; sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife and have low densities and melting points; lead and mercury conduct heat poorly.

Physical properties of non-metals

Non-metals are solids (carbon, sulphur, iodine) or gases (oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen); bromine is the only liquid non-metal. Sulphur and coal (carbon) crumble into powder when hammered — non-metals are brittle, neither malleable nor ductile, generally dull and poor conductors of heat and electricity.

Exceptions: iodine is lustrous; carbon as diamond is the hardest natural substance with a very high melting and boiling point, and as graphite is a conductor of electricity. Elements showing properties of both are metalloids. Physical properties alone cannot classify elements, so chemical properties are used.

Metals and non-metals with oxygen

Burnt magnesium ribbon gives white MgO, whose solution turns red litmus blue; burnt sulphur gives SO₂, whose solution turns blue litmus red. So metal oxides are basic and non-metal oxides are generally acidic.

Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide   |   2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (black)   |   4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Some metal oxides, such as Al₂O₃ and ZnO, react with both acids and bases to give salt and water. They are amphoteric oxides:

Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O   |   Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ (sodium aluminate) + H₂O

Most metal oxides are insoluble in water; the soluble ones form alkalis: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH and K₂O + H₂O → 2KOH.

Sodium and potassium catch fire in open air, so they are stored under kerosene. Mg, Al, Zn and Pb form a protective oxide layer. Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame; iron filings burn when sprinkled in a flame; copper only gets coated with black CuO; silver and gold do not react even at high temperature. Thickening aluminium's oxide layer by electrolysis is anodising.

Metals with water

Metal + Water → Metal oxide + Hydrogen; metal oxide + water → metal hydroxide

  • Potassium and sodium react violently with cold water; the heat ignites the hydrogen: 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂ + heat (similarly for Na).
  • Calcium reacts less violently and starts floating as hydrogen bubbles stick to it: Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂.
  • Magnesium reacts with hot water and also floats.
  • Aluminium, iron and zinc react only with steam, giving the oxide and hydrogen: 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂ and 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂.
  • Lead, copper, silver and gold do not react with water at all.

Metals with dilute acids

Metal + Dilute acid → Salt + Hydrogen

With dilute HCl, magnesium reacts most vigorously (the tube gets warmest), then aluminium, zinc and iron (Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂); copper does not react. Hydrogen is not evolved when metals react with nitric acid, because HNO₃ is a strong oxidising agent: it oxidises the H₂ to water and is itself reduced to an oxide of nitrogen. Only magnesium and manganese give H₂ with very dilute HNO₃.

Aqua regia (“royal water”) is a freshly prepared mixture of concentrated hydrochloric acid and concentrated nitric acid in the ratio 3 : 1. It dissolves gold and platinum, which neither acid can dissolve alone.

Metals with solutions of other metal salts: the reactivity series

An iron nail in blue copper sulphate solution gets a brown copper coating and the solution turns pale green; copper in iron sulphate solution shows no change. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution:

Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu   |   Metal A + Salt of B → Salt of A + Metal B (if A is more reactive)

Metals listed in decreasing order of reactivity form the reactivity series:

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au

Metals above hydrogen displace hydrogen from dilute acids; copper, mercury, silver and gold, below hydrogen, cannot.

How metals and non-metals react: ionic compounds

Noble gases have a filled valence shell (octet; helium a duplet) and are unreactive; other elements react to attain this stable configuration. Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses one electron to become Na⁺ (2, 8); chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains that electron to become Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8). The oppositely charged ions are held by strong electrostatic attraction:

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻   |   Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻   |   Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl

Magnesium (2, 8, 2) similarly gives one electron to each of two chlorine atoms (Mg²⁺, 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂). Compounds formed by electron transfer from a metal to a non-metal are ionic or electrovalent compounds. Their properties:

  • Physical nature: solid, hard and brittle because of strong forces between ions.
  • High melting and boiling points: much energy is needed to overcome the strong inter-ionic attraction (NaCl melts at 1074 K).
  • Solubility: generally soluble in water and insoluble in kerosene and petrol.
  • Conduction: no conduction as solids, where ions cannot move; they conduct when molten or dissolved in water, as the ions become free to move.

Occurrence and extraction of metals

The earth's crust is the main source of metals (sea water also has some). Naturally occurring compounds are minerals; a mineral from which a metal can be profitably extracted is an ore. Least reactive metals (Au, Ag, Pt, Cu) occur free; the most reactive (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) never do; middle ones (Zn, Fe, Pb) occur as oxides, sulphides or carbonates.

Enrichment: ores contain unwanted soil and sand called gangue, removed first. The extraction method then depends on reactivity.

Low in the series — oxides are reduced by heating alone. Cinnabar (HgS) is heated in air: 2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂, then 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂. Copper from Cu₂S: 2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂, then 2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂.

Middle of the series — metals are easier to obtain from oxides, so sulphide and carbonate ores are first converted to oxides. Roasting is heating a sulphide ore strongly in excess air: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂. Calcination is heating a carbonate ore strongly in limited air: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂. The oxide is then reduced with carbon: ZnO + C → Zn + CO. Reactive metals like aluminium can also reduce oxides; with iron(III) oxide the reaction is so exothermic that molten iron forms — the thermit reaction, used to join railway tracks and cracked machine parts:

Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat   |   3MnO₂ + 4Al → 3Mn + 2Al₂O₃ + heat

Top of the series — carbon cannot reduce their oxides, as these metals have more affinity for oxygen. They are obtained by electrolytic reduction of their molten chlorides; the metal deposits at the cathode and chlorine is liberated at the anode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (cathode); 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (anode). Aluminium is obtained by electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide.

Refining: the metal obtained is impure. In electrolytic refining of copper, a thick block of impure copper is the anode, a thin strip of pure copper is the cathode, and acidified copper sulphate solution is the electrolyte. On passing current, copper dissolves from the anode and an equal amount of pure copper deposits on the cathode. Soluble impurities enter the solution; insoluble ones settle below the anode as anode mud.

Corrosion and its prevention

Silver turns black (silver sulphide), copper gains a green coat (basic copper carbonate, from moist CO₂) and iron gets brown flaky rust. This slow eating away of metals is corrosion.

Conditions for rusting: take clean iron nails in three test tubes. In A the nail is in ordinary water, open to air. In B it is in boiled distilled water (no dissolved air) with a layer of oil on top to stop air dissolving. In C it sits above anhydrous calcium chloride, which absorbs moisture, in a corked tube (dry air). After a few days only the nail in A has rusted. So iron rusts only when both air (oxygen) and water are present.

Prevention: painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (coating iron or steel with a thin layer of zinc — the article stays protected even if the zinc layer is broken, because zinc is more reactive and corrodes first), chrome plating, anodising and making alloys.

Alloys: a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, made by melting them together. Pure iron is soft; with a little carbon (about 0.05%) it becomes hard, strong steel. Stainless steel (iron with nickel and chromium) is hard and does not rust. An alloy of mercury is an amalgam. Brass = copper + zinc; bronze = copper + tin; solder = lead + tin, with a low melting point, used to weld electrical wires. Alloys have lower conductivity and melting points than the pure metals. Pure (24-carat) gold is too soft for jewellery, so 22-carat gold — 22 parts gold with 2 parts silver or copper — is used.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

An element X forms an oxide X₂O₃ that reacts with both dilute HCl and NaOH solution to give salt and water. (a) What type of oxide is it? (b) Identify X. (c) Write balanced equations for both reactions. (3 marks)
  1. An oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to give salt and water is an amphoteric oxide.
  2. A metal with oxide formula X₂O₃ that is amphoteric is aluminium (Al₂O₃).
  3. With acid: Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O — check: Al 2 = 2, O 3 = 3, H 6 = 6, Cl 6 = 6.
  4. With base: Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O — check: Al 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2, O 5 = 5 (3 + 2 on the left, 4 + 1 on the right), H 2 = 2.
Why is sodium kept immersed in kerosene oil? Why does calcium float when it reacts with water? (2 marks)

Sodium is so reactive that it catches fire when kept in open air, reacting vigorously with oxygen and moisture; kerosene keeps air and water away from it. Calcium floats because bubbles of hydrogen gas formed in the reaction (Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂) stick to its surface and make it buoyant.

Four metals W, X, Y and Z were tested with salt solutions. W displaces copper from CuSO₄ solution but does not react with FeSO₄. X displaces iron from FeSO₄ but does not react with ZnSO₄. Y displaces silver from AgNO₃ but does not react with CuSO₄. Z does not react with any of these solutions, including AgNO₃. (a) Arrange W, X, Y, Z in decreasing order of reactivity. (b) What would you observe if X is added to copper sulphate solution? (3 marks)
  1. A metal displaces only metals that are below it in the reactivity series.
  2. X displaces Fe but not Zn → X lies between Zn and Fe. W displaces Cu but not Fe → W lies between Fe and Cu.
  3. Y displaces Ag but not Cu → Y lies between Cu and Ag. Z cannot even displace silver → Z is below Ag.
  4. (b) X is more reactive than iron, and hence than copper, so it displaces copper: the blue colour of the solution fades and a reddish-brown deposit of copper forms on X.

(a) Decreasing reactivity: X > W > Y > Z.

Show the formation of Na₂O and MgO by the transfer of electrons. Name the ions present. (Atomic numbers: Na 11, O 8, Mg 12) (3 marks)
  1. Electronic configurations: Na 2, 8, 1; O 2, 6; Mg 2, 8, 2.
  2. Na₂O: each of two sodium atoms loses one electron and oxygen gains both: 2Na → 2Na⁺ + 2e⁻; O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻. Two Na⁺ (2, 8) and one O²⁻ (2, 8) attract each other → Na₂O.
  3. MgO: magnesium loses two electrons to one oxygen atom: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻; O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻ → Mg²⁺O²⁻.
  4. Ions: sodium ion Na⁺, oxide ion O²⁻, magnesium ion Mg²⁺. In electron-dot form, draw the metal electrons as crosses moving to the oxygen atom, which ends with an octet.
Why do ionic compounds have high melting points? Why do they conduct electricity in the molten state but not in the solid state? (2 marks)

There is a strong electrostatic force of attraction between the oppositely charged ions, so a large amount of energy is needed to break it — hence high melting points. In the solid state the ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move; in the molten state (or in solution) the ions become free to move towards the electrodes and carry current.

A metal M is found in nature as its sulphide ore MS. It is in the middle of the reactivity series. Describe the steps to extract M with equations. Why is the sulphide converted to an oxide? Name the method used to purify the metal and draw its labelled diagram for copper. (5 marks)
  1. A middle-series metal with a sulphide ore, e.g. zinc (ZnS, zinc blende). First the ore is concentrated to remove gangue.
  2. Roasting — heat strongly in excess air: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂.
  3. Reduction — heat the oxide with carbon (coke): ZnO + C → Zn + CO.
  4. The sulphide is converted to the oxide because it is easier to obtain a metal from its oxide than from its sulphide or carbonate.
  5. Electrolytic refining: impure metal as the anode, a thin strip of pure metal as the cathode, and a solution of a soluble salt of the metal as electrolyte. For copper: anode = impure copper, cathode = pure copper, electrolyte = acidified CuSO₄. Pure copper deposits on the cathode; insoluble impurities collect below the anode as anode mud (see the diagram above).
Three test tubes A, B and C contain iron nails: A in tap water open to air, B in boiled distilled water covered with oil, C with anhydrous calcium chloride and corked. In which tube does the nail rust? Give reasons for each, and state two methods of preventing rusting. (3 marks)
  1. A — the nail rusts, because both air (oxygen) and water are present.
  2. B — no rust: boiling removes dissolved air and the oil layer stops air from dissolving again, so only water is present.
  3. C — no rust: anhydrous CaCl₂ absorbs moisture, so only dry air is present.
  4. Prevention: painting/oiling/greasing, and galvanising (coating with zinc). Others: chrome plating, alloying.
A goldsmith says the bangle is made of 22-carat gold. (a) What percentage of the bangle is gold? (b) Why is 24-carat gold not used for jewellery? (c) Name the other metals usually alloyed with it. (2 marks)
  1. (a) Pure gold is 24 carat, so 22-carat gold = 22/24 × 100 = 91.67% gold (about 91.7%).
  2. (b) 24-carat (pure) gold is very soft, so ornaments would bend and lose shape easily.
  3. (c) The remaining 2 parts are silver or copper, which make the alloy harder.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat students writeWrite this instead
Wrong product with steam“3Fe + 4H₂O → 3Fe(OH)₂ + H₂” or “Fe + H₂O → FeO + H₂”.With steam, metals give the oxide: 3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂; 2Al + 3H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂.
Hydrogen from nitric acid“Zn + 2HNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + H₂.”HNO₃ is an oxidising agent and oxidises H₂ to water; only Mg and Mn give H₂ with very dilute HNO₃.
Roasting vs calcination“Calcination is heating a sulphide ore in air.”Roasting: sulphide ore, excess air. Calcination: carbonate ore, limited air.
Electrodes in refining“Pure copper is the anode and impure copper the cathode.”Impure copper = anode (thick block); pure copper = cathode (thin strip).
Ionic compounds conduct as solids“NaCl conducts electricity because it has ions.”Ions in solid NaCl cannot move; it conducts only when molten or dissolved in water.
Displacement direction“Cu + FeSO₄ → CuSO₄ + Fe.”Copper is less reactive than iron, so no reaction. Only Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu occurs.
Rusting needs only water“Iron rusts in water.”Rusting needs both air (oxygen) and water/moisture together.

Quick revision

  1. Metals: lustrous, hard, malleable, ductile, sonorous, good conductors. Gold most malleable/ductile; Ag and Cu best conductors.
  2. Exceptions: Hg liquid; Na, K soft, low density; Ga, Cs melt on palm; Pb, Hg poor heat conductors.
  3. Non-metals: brittle, dull, poor conductors; bromine is liquid; iodine is lustrous; graphite conducts; diamond hardest.
  4. Metal oxides are basic, non-metal oxides acidic; Al₂O₃ and ZnO are amphoteric.
  5. Na and K stored in kerosene; Mg, Al, Zn, Pb have protective oxide layers; anodising thickens the Al₂O₃ layer.
  6. Cold water: K, Na (violent, H₂ catches fire), Ca (floats); hot water: Mg; steam: Al, Zn, Fe (give oxides); no reaction: Pb, Cu, Ag, Au.
  7. Metal + dilute acid → salt + H₂ (Mg > Al > Zn > Fe; Cu none). HNO₃ gives no H₂ except with Mg, Mn (very dilute).
  8. Aqua regia = conc. HCl : conc. HNO₃ = 3 : 1; dissolves gold and platinum.
  9. Reactivity: K Na Ca Mg Al Zn Fe Pb [H] Cu Hg Ag Au. Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu.
  10. Ionic compounds: metal loses e⁻, non-metal gains e⁻; hard, brittle, high m.p., water-soluble, conduct only when molten/dissolved.
  11. Ore = mineral from which metal is extracted profitably; gangue = impurities removed first.
  12. Low reactivity: heat alone (HgS → HgO → Hg; Cu₂S → Cu). Medium: roast (sulphide) or calcine (carbonate) → oxide → reduce with C.
  13. Thermit: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃ + heat — joins railway tracks.
  14. High reactivity (K–Al): electrolysis of molten chloride/oxide; metal at cathode.
  15. Electrolytic refining of Cu: impure anode, pure cathode, acidified CuSO₄, anode mud below anode.
  16. Rusting needs air + water. Prevent: paint, oil, grease, galvanise (Zn), chrome-plate, anodise, alloy. Brass Cu+Zn, bronze Cu+Sn, solder Pb+Sn, 22-carat gold.
