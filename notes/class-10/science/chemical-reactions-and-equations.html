At a glance

A chemical reaction turns substances into new substances, and a chemical equation is the short, balanced way of writing it. This chapter teaches you to write and balance equations, sort reactions into five types, and spot oxidation and reduction.

  • Balance equations by changing coefficients, never subscripts.
  • Know one example and one equation for each type: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, redox.
  • Recognise the signs of a reaction: colour change, gas, precipitate, temperature change.
  • Identify the substance oxidised, the substance reduced, and the oxidising and reducing agents.
  • Explain corrosion and rancidity and how to prevent them.

Concepts, step by step

How do we know a reaction has happened?

A chemical change produces a new substance, unlike a physical change such as melting ice. In the laboratory we infer that a reaction has taken place from one or more of these observations:

  • Change in state — e.g. a solid forms or a gas is given off.
  • Change in colour — e.g. blue copper sulphate solution turns pale green when iron is added.
  • Evolution of a gas — e.g. zinc in dilute sulphuric acid gives bubbles of hydrogen.
  • Change in temperature — e.g. quicklime with water becomes hot.
  • Formation of a precipitate — e.g. lead nitrate and potassium iodide give a yellow solid.

Burning magnesium is the classic first example. A cleaned magnesium ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame and leaves a white powder, magnesium oxide. The ribbon is rubbed with sandpaper first to remove the layer of magnesium oxide that forms on its surface in air, which would slow the reaction.

2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)

Writing a chemical equation

A word equation names the substances: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. A chemical equation uses formulae instead. Reactants go on the left, products on the right, joined by an arrow that means “form” or “change into”.

  • Physical states are written in brackets: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous solution.
  • Conditions such as heat, light, pressure or a catalyst are written above or below the arrow, e.g. CO(g) + 2H₂(g) 340 atm→ CH₃OH(l).
  • An equation with unequal numbers of atoms on the two sides is a skeletal equation. It must be balanced.

Why equations must be balanced — and how to balance them

The law of conservation of mass says mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. Atoms are only rearranged, so every element must have the same number of atoms on both sides. Balancing is done by the hit-and-trial method:

  1. Write the skeletal equation with correct formulae.
  2. List the number of atoms of each element on both sides.
  3. Start with the compound that has the most atoms, and balance one element at a time by placing coefficients in front of formulae.
  4. Balance hydrogen and oxygen last, then re-check every element.
  5. Add the physical states and any conditions.

Never change a subscript to balance an equation: H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water. Only the coefficients change.

Combination reactions

Two or more substances combine to form a single product: A + B → AB.

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat

Calcium oxide (quicklime) reacts vigorously with water to form calcium hydroxide (slaked lime), releasing a lot of heat. A solution of slaked lime is used for whitewashing walls: it reacts slowly with carbon dioxide in the air to form a thin layer of calcium carbonate, which gives a shiny finish (Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O).

Other examples: burning of coal, C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); formation of water, 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l).

Reactions that release heat are exothermic. Burning natural gas and respiration are exothermic: glucose from digested food combines with oxygen in our cells and releases energy — C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + energy. The decomposition of vegetable matter into compost is also exothermic.

Decomposition reactions

A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products: AB → A + B. Breaking bonds needs energy, so decomposition reactions are usually endothermic. The energy can come from heat, electricity or light.

  • Thermal decomposition (heat). Green ferrous sulphate crystals lose water, then decompose to reddish-brown ferric oxide with the smell of burning sulphur: 2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g). Limestone decomposes on heating to quicklime, used to make cement: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). Heating lead nitrate gives brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide: 2Pb(NO₃)₂(s) → 2PbO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g).
  • Electrolytic decomposition (electricity). Passing a current through water (with a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid to make it conduct) gives hydrogen at the cathode and oxygen at the anode: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g). The volume of hydrogen collected is twice the volume of oxygen.
  • Photolytic decomposition (light). White silver chloride turns grey in sunlight as silver forms: 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g). Silver bromide behaves the same way, which is why these compounds are used in black-and-white photography and stored in dark bottles.

Displacement reactions

A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from a solution of its compound: A + BC → AC + B.

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

When iron nails are dipped in blue copper sulphate solution for about 20 minutes, a brownish coat of copper forms on the nails and the blue colour fades to pale green (ferrous sulphate). Iron displaces copper because iron is more reactive than copper.

Other examples: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s); Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc and lead are both more reactive than copper. Copper cannot displace iron, so the reverse reaction does not happen.

Double displacement reactions

Two compounds exchange ions to form two new compounds: AB + CD → AD + CB. Many of them produce an insoluble solid, a precipitate, so they are also called precipitation reactions.

Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)

A white precipitate of barium sulphate forms. Another favourite: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq), giving a bright yellow precipitate of lead iodide. Neutralisation of an acid by a base (NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O) is also a double displacement reaction.

Oxidation and reduction (redox)

For this chapter, use the oxygen and hydrogen definitions:

  • Oxidation = gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen.
  • Reduction = loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.

Heating copper powder in air turns its surface black as copper(II) oxide forms: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO (copper is oxidised). Passing hydrogen over hot copper oxide reverses it:

CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O

Here CuO loses oxygen and is reduced, while H₂ gains oxygen and is oxidised. Both happen together, so this is a redox reaction. The substance that gives oxygen (CuO) is the oxidising agent; the substance that takes it (H₂) is the reducing agent.

More examples: ZnO + C → Zn + CO (ZnO reduced, C oxidised); MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂ (MnO₂ reduced, HCl oxidised — it loses hydrogen).

Oxidation in everyday life: corrosion and rancidity

Corrosion is the slow eating away of a metal by air, moisture, acids or other substances around it. Iron gains a reddish-brown coat of rust (hydrated iron(III) oxide); silver turns black; copper develops a green coating. Rusting damages car bodies, bridges, iron railings and ships, and costs a great deal every year. It is prevented by painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (coating with zinc) and chrome plating.

Rancidity is the oxidation of fats and oils in food, which changes their smell and taste. It is slowed by adding antioxidants, storing food in airtight containers, refrigerating it, and flushing packets of chips with an unreactive gas such as nitrogen to keep oxygen out.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

Balance: Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ (2 marks)
  1. Atoms on the left: Fe 1, H 2, O 1. On the right: Fe 3, H 2, O 4.
  2. Oxygen first (Fe₃O₄ is the largest formula): put 4 before H₂O → Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Now H = 8 on the left.
  3. Hydrogen: put 4 before H₂ → H = 8 on both sides.
  4. Iron: put 3 before Fe.

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g) — check: Fe 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 4 = 4.

Why is a magnesium ribbon cleaned before burning? (1 mark)

To remove the layer of magnesium oxide on its surface, which forms when magnesium is exposed to air and prevents it from burning readily.

In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, name the substance oxidised, the substance reduced, the oxidising agent and the reducing agent. (2 marks)

H₂ gains oxygen, so it is oxidised and is the reducing agent. CuO loses oxygen, so it is reduced and is the oxidising agent.

Why is silver chloride stored in dark-coloured bottles? Write the equation. (2 marks)

Silver chloride decomposes in sunlight into silver and chlorine: 2AgCl(s) → 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g) (photolytic decomposition). Dark bottles keep light out.

A student heats green crystals in a boiling tube and notices the colour change to reddish-brown with a smell of burning sulphur. Identify the crystals, the type of reaction and write the equation. (3 marks)

The crystals are ferrous sulphate. They undergo thermal decomposition: 2FeSO₄(s) → Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g). The reddish-brown solid is ferric oxide; the smell comes from sulphur dioxide and sulphur trioxide. (The crystals first lose their water of crystallisation, which is why the green colour fades.)

Why is respiration considered an exothermic reaction? (2 marks)

During respiration glucose combines with oxygen in our cells to form carbon dioxide and water, and energy is released: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. A reaction that releases energy is exothermic.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat students writeWrite this instead
Changing subscripts to balanceWriting H₂O₂ to “balance” oxygen in H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.Change only coefficients: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
Leaving out physical statesZn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ when the question asks for states.Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g).
Mixing up displacement typesCalling Fe + CuSO₄ a double displacement reaction.One element swaps places: displacement. Two compounds swap ions: double displacement.
Oxidation only means oxygenNot seeing that HCl is oxidised in MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂.Loss of hydrogen is also oxidation, so HCl is oxidised.
Wrong volume ratio in electrolysisSaying equal volumes of hydrogen and oxygen collect.Hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂).
Calling all decomposition exothermicWriting that heating CaCO₃ releases heat.Decomposition usually needs energy: it is endothermic.

Quick revision

  1. A chemical equation must be balanced because mass is conserved; balance with coefficients only.
  2. States: (s), (l), (g), (aq); conditions go above or below the arrow.
  3. Combination: A + B → AB · CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (exothermic; whitewash).
  4. Decomposition by heat, electricity or light: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂; 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂.
  5. Heating FeSO₄ crystals: green → reddish-brown Fe₂O₃, smell of burning sulphur.
  6. Heating Pb(NO₃)₂: brown fumes of NO₂.
  7. Electrolysis of water: H₂ at cathode, O₂ at anode, volume ratio 2 : 1.
  8. Displacement: a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one — Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu (blue → pale green).
  9. Double displacement often gives a precipitate: BaSO₄ (white), PbI₂ (yellow).
  10. Oxidation: gain of O / loss of H. Reduction: loss of O / gain of H. Together: redox.
  11. Oxidising agent gives oxygen (and is reduced); reducing agent takes oxygen (and is oxidised).
  12. Respiration and burning are exothermic; most decompositions are endothermic.
  13. Corrosion: rust on iron, black on silver, green on copper — prevent by paint, oil, galvanising.
  14. Rancidity: oxidation of fats — prevent with antioxidants, airtight packing, nitrogen flushing, refrigeration.
