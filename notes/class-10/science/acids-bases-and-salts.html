Acids, Bases and Salts

At a glance

Acids taste sour and turn blue litmus red; bases taste bitter, feel soapy and turn red litmus blue. In water, acids give H⁺ ions and bases give OH⁻ ions, and when they react they cancel each other to form a salt and water. The pH scale tells you how acidic or basic a solution is. The chapter ends with five important salts made from common salt.

  • Write the general reactions: acid + metal, acid + carbonate, acid + base, acid + metal oxide, base + non-metal oxide.
  • Explain why acids conduct electricity and why HCl shows acidic behaviour only in water.
  • Read the pH scale and explain everyday uses of pH (stomach, teeth, soil, stings).
  • Give the equation and two uses each for NaOH (chlor-alkali process), bleaching powder, baking soda, washing soda and Plaster of Paris.
  • Explain water of crystallisation using copper sulphate crystals.

Concepts, step by step

Indicators: telling acids from bases

An indicator is a substance that shows a different colour or smell in acidic and basic solutions.

  • Litmus is a purple dye extracted from lichens. Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. Red cabbage leaves, turmeric and the petals of hydrangea, petunia and geranium are other natural indicators. Turmeric turns reddish-brown in a base, which is why a curry stain on a white cloth turns reddish when washed with soap (a base).
  • Synthetic indicators: phenolphthalein stays colourless in acid and turns pink in base; methyl orange is red in acid and yellow in base.
  • Olfactory indicators change smell. The smell of finely chopped onion, vanilla essence or clove oil disappears in a basic solution such as NaOH but stays in an acidic solution such as HCl. These help visually impaired students test solutions.

Reaction of acids and bases with metals

When zinc granules are added to dilute sulphuric acid, bubbles of a gas appear. If the gas is passed through soap solution, the soap bubbles it fills burn with a pop sound when a burning candle is brought near — the test for hydrogen.

Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)

Acid + Metal → Salt + Hydrogen gas

Some metals also react with strong bases: zinc heated with sodium hydroxide solution gives sodium zincate and hydrogen, 2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na₂ZnO₂(s) + H₂(g). Such reactions are not possible for all metals.

Reaction of acids with metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates

Adding dilute HCl to sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate gives brisk effervescence. The gas turns lime water milky, so it is carbon dioxide.

Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)

Lime water turns milky because insoluble calcium carbonate forms: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l). If excess CO₂ is passed, the milkiness disappears because soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate forms: CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq).

Metal carbonate / hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water

Neutralisation: acids and bases react with each other

Take dilute NaOH with two drops of phenolphthalein — it is pink. Add dilute HCl drop by drop: the pink colour disappears when all the base has been used up. Adding a drop of NaOH again brings the pink back. The acid and base cancel each other's effect.

NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)

Base + Acid → Salt + Water (neutralisation)

Metal oxides with acids and non-metal oxides with bases

Black copper(II) oxide dissolves in dilute HCl and the solution turns blue-green because copper(II) chloride forms:

CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)

Since metal oxides react with acids to give salt and water, just like bases, they are called basic oxides.

Carbon dioxide reacts with calcium hydroxide (a base) to give salt and water: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O. Since non-metal oxides react with bases in this way, they are acidic in nature.

What all acids and all bases have in common

Put two iron nails through a rubber cork in a beaker, connect them to a 6 V battery and a bulb, and pour in dilute HCl: the bulb glows. It glows with dilute H₂SO₄ too, but not with glucose or alcohol solutions, even though these also contain hydrogen. So acids produce ions that carry current, and the common ion is H⁺(aq). Glucose and alcohol do not ionise, so they are not acids.

Dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus but turns moist blue litmus red. Hydrogen ions form only in the presence of water. H⁺ cannot exist alone; it attaches to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion:

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻   |   H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺

Bases give hydroxide ions in water: NaOH(s) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq); likewise KOH and Mg(OH)₂. Bases that dissolve in water are called alkalis. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis. Alkalis are soapy, bitter and corrosive.

So neutralisation is really H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Diluting acids and bases safely

Dissolving a concentrated acid or base in water is highly exothermic. Always add the acid slowly to water with constant stirring. If water is added to concentrated acid, the heat can make the mixture splash out and cause burns, and the glass container may break from excessive local heating. Mixing an acid or base with water decreases the concentration of ions (H₃O⁺ or OH⁻) per unit volume; this is dilution.

How strong is an acid or base? The pH scale

A universal indicator is a mixture of several indicators that shows different colours at different concentrations of H⁺ ions. The pH scale (p from the German potenz, power) measures H⁺ ion concentration from 0 (very acidic) to 14 (very basic).

  • pH 7 = neutral (pure water); pH below 7 = acidic; pH above 7 = basic.
  • The higher the H⁺ concentration, the lower the pH. As pH rises from 7 to 14, OH⁻ concentration increases.
  • Acids that give many H⁺ ions are strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃); those giving fewer are weak acids (acetic acid, citric acid). Similarly there are strong bases (NaOH, KOH) and weak bases (NH₄OH).

Importance of pH in everyday life

  • Our body works within a narrow pH range of about 7.0–7.8. When rain water has pH below 5.6 it is acid rain; when it flows into rivers it lowers their pH and makes survival difficult for aquatic life.
  • Soil: plants grow best in a particular pH range, so soil pH is tested before treating it.
  • Digestion: the stomach makes hydrochloric acid, which helps digestion without harming the stomach. Too much acid causes pain and irritation (indigestion). An antacid, a mild base such as milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide), neutralises the excess acid.
  • Tooth decay starts when the pH in the mouth falls below 5.5. Tooth enamel (calcium hydroxyapatite) corrodes below this pH. Bacteria break down sugar and food particles left in the mouth into acids, so basic toothpastes help by neutralising them.
  • Self-defence by animals and plants: a bee sting injects methanoic acid; rubbing a mild base like baking soda gives relief. Nettle leaves have stinging hairs that inject methanoic acid; the leaf of the dock plant, often growing beside it, is rubbed on the area.

Natural acids to remember: vinegar — acetic; orange and lemon — citric; tamarind — tartaric; tomato — oxalic; sour milk (curd) — lactic; ant and nettle sting — methanoic.

Families of salts and the pH of salts

Salts with the same positive or negative radical belong to the same family: NaCl and Na₂SO₄ are sodium salts; NaCl and KCl are chloride salts. The pH of a salt solution depends on the acid and base it comes from:

  • Strong acid + strong base → neutral salt, pH 7 (NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄).
  • Strong acid + weak base → acidic salt, pH below 7 (NH₄Cl, CuSO₄).
  • Strong base + weak acid → basic salt, pH above 7 (Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃, CH₃COONa).

Chemicals from common salt

Common salt (NaCl) is formed from NaOH and HCl. It is obtained from sea water and from beds of rock salt, and is the raw material for the following chemicals.

Sodium hydroxide — chlor-alkali process. When electricity is passed through brine (aqueous NaCl), it decomposes:

2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g)

Chlorine gas is given off at the anode, hydrogen gas at the cathode, and NaOH solution forms near the cathode. The name comes from the products: chlor for chlorine, alkali for sodium hydroxide. Uses: NaOH for soaps and detergents, paper making, artificial fibres and de-greasing metals; Cl₂ for water treatment, swimming pools, PVC, disinfectants and pesticides; H₂ for fuels, margarine and ammonia for fertilisers; H₂ and Cl₂ together make HCl.

Bleaching powder is made by the action of chlorine on dry slaked lime:

Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O

Uses: bleaching cotton and linen in textile mills, wood pulp in paper factories and washed clothes in laundries; as an oxidising agent in chemical industries; and to disinfect drinking water.

Baking soda (sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃) is made from sodium chloride:

NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃

It is a mild, non-corrosive base. On heating during cooking it decomposes: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. Baking powder is baking soda mixed with a mild edible acid such as tartaric acid; when heated or wetted, CO₂ is released and makes bread and cake soft and spongy (the acid also removes the bitter taste of sodium carbonate). It is used as an antacid and in soda-acid fire extinguishers.

Washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) is made by heating baking soda to get sodium carbonate and then recrystallising it from water: Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O. Uses: in the glass, soap and paper industries, making borax, as a household cleaning agent, and to remove permanent hardness of water.

Are salt crystals really dry? Water of crystallisation

Heating blue copper sulphate crystals in a boiling tube gives water droplets on the cooler part of the tube and leaves a white powder. Adding a few drops of water to it brings back the blue colour. The water that is part of a crystal's structure is its water of crystallisation: CuSO₄·5H₂O has five molecules, washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O has ten, and gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O has two.

Plaster of Paris is made by heating gypsum at 373 K:

CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O

It is a white powder that sets into a hard mass of gypsum when mixed with water: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O. It must be stored in a moisture-proof container. “Half a water molecule” means two formula units of CaSO₄ share one molecule of water. Uses: plaster for supporting fractured bones, making toys and decorative materials, and making surfaces smooth.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

Five solutions A, B, C, D and E have pH 4, 1, 11, 7 and 9 respectively. Which is (a) neutral, (b) strongly alkaline, (c) strongly acidic, (d) weakly acidic, (e) weakly alkaline? Arrange them in increasing order of H⁺ ion concentration. (3 marks)
  1. pH 7 is neutral; the further below 7, the more acidic; the further above 7, the more basic.
  2. (a) D (pH 7) neutral; (b) C (pH 11) strongly alkaline; (c) B (pH 1) strongly acidic; (d) A (pH 4) weakly acidic; (e) E (pH 9) weakly alkaline.
  3. Higher pH means lower H⁺ concentration, so order the solutions from highest pH to lowest.

Increasing H⁺ concentration: C (11) < E (9) < D (7) < A (4) < B (1).

Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of dry blue litmus paper, while it turns moist blue litmus red? (2 marks)

Acidic behaviour is due to H⁺ ions, and HCl produces them only in the presence of water (HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻). Dry HCl gas does not ionise, so dry litmus is unaffected; on moist litmus it forms H₃O⁺ ions, which turn blue litmus red.

A compound X used in the kitchen makes pakoras crisp and is also an ingredient of antacids. Name X, give its formula, and write the equation for the reaction when it is heated. How is it useful in a soda-acid fire extinguisher? (3 marks)
  1. X is baking soda, sodium hydrogencarbonate, NaHCO₃.
  2. On heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. The CO₂ makes food soft and crisp.
  3. In a soda-acid fire extinguisher, NaHCO₃ reacts with an acid to release CO₂, which cuts off the oxygen supply to the fire.
A metal compound A reacts with dilute HCl and produces effervescence. The gas evolved puts out a burning candle. Write a balanced equation if one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride. How will you confirm the gas? (3 marks)
  1. A gas that extinguishes a flame and comes from a compound with acid is CO₂, so A is a carbonate. Since calcium chloride forms, A is calcium carbonate.
  2. CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) — check: Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 3, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2.
  3. Pass the gas through lime water: it turns milky as CaCO₃ forms, Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.
While diluting a concentrated acid, why is the acid added to water and not water to the acid? (2 marks)

Dilution of a concentrated acid is highly exothermic. If water is added to the acid, a large amount of heat is released at once in a small volume, so the mixture may splash out and cause burns, and the glass may crack. Adding acid slowly to water with stirring spreads the heat through a large volume of water.

A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk. (a) Why does he shift the pH of fresh milk from 6 to slightly alkaline? (b) Why does this milk take longer to set as curd? (2 marks)

(a) In slightly alkaline conditions the lactic acid formed by bacteria is neutralised, so the milk does not turn sour quickly and keeps longer. (b) The acid produced during curdling first has to neutralise the added baking soda, so it takes longer for the pH to fall enough for the milk to set.

Describe the chlor-alkali process. Write the balanced equation, name the products formed at the anode and cathode, explain the name of the process and give one use of each product. How is bleaching powder made from one of these products? (5 marks)
  1. When electricity is passed through an aqueous solution of sodium chloride (brine), it decomposes to form sodium hydroxide, chlorine and hydrogen.
  2. Equation: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g) + H₂(g).
  3. Chlorine is given off at the anode, hydrogen at the cathode, and sodium hydroxide solution forms near the cathode.
  4. Name: chlor from chlorine and alkali from sodium hydroxide, the two main products.
  5. Uses: NaOH — making soaps and detergents; Cl₂ — disinfecting drinking water (or PVC, pesticides); H₂ — making ammonia for fertilisers (or margarine, fuel).
  6. Bleaching powder is made by passing chlorine over dry slaked lime: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O.
How is Plaster of Paris prepared? Write the equation. What happens when it is mixed with water, and why should it be stored in a moisture-proof container? (3 marks)
  1. Gypsum is heated at 373 K: CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O.
  2. With water it changes back to gypsum and sets into a hard solid mass: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O.
  3. Moisture from the air would make it set into a hard mass during storage, making it useless, so it is kept in a moisture-proof container.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat students writeWrite this instead
Adding water to concentrated acid“Water is poured into the acid to dilute it.”Always add acid slowly to water with constant stirring; the process is highly exothermic.
Reading the pH scale backwards“pH 2 is less acidic than pH 5 because the number is smaller.”Lower pH means more H⁺ ions: pH 2 is more acidic than pH 5.
Treating all bases as alkalis“Every base dissolves in water and is an alkali.”Only water-soluble bases are alkalis (NaOH, KOH). All alkalis are bases, not all bases are alkalis.
Mixing up baking soda and washing sodaBaking soda = Na₂CO₃; washing soda = NaHCO₃.Baking soda is NaHCO₃; washing soda is Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
Wrong electrode products in chlor-alkali“Hydrogen at the anode, chlorine at the cathode.”Cl₂ at the anode, H₂ at the cathode, NaOH near the cathode.
Wrong indicator colours“Phenolphthalein turns red in acid.”Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in base. Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base.
Wrong formula of Plaster of ParisCaSO₄·H₂O or CaSO₄·2H₂O.Plaster of Paris is CaSO₄·½H₂O; gypsum is CaSO₄·2H₂O.

Quick revision

  1. Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. Phenolphthalein: colourless → pink in base; methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in base.
  2. Onion, vanilla and clove are olfactory indicators — their smell disappears in a base.
  3. Acid + metal → salt + H₂ (burns with a pop); Zn + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂.
  4. Acid + carbonate/hydrogencarbonate → salt + CO₂ + H₂O; CO₂ turns lime water milky, excess CO₂ clears it.
  5. Neutralisation: acid + base → salt + water, i.e. H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
  6. Metal oxides are basic (CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O, blue-green); non-metal oxides are acidic.
  7. Acids give H⁺ (as H₃O⁺) only in water — dry HCl does not affect dry litmus. Glucose and alcohol do not conduct.
  8. Bases give OH⁻ in water; water-soluble bases are alkalis.
  9. Dilute by adding acid to water, never water to acid — highly exothermic.
  10. pH < 7 acidic, 7 neutral, > 7 basic; more H⁺ = lower pH. Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators.
  11. Body pH 7.0–7.8; acid rain pH < 5.6; tooth decay below pH 5.5; antacid = milk of magnesia, Mg(OH)₂.
  12. Bee/nettle sting = methanoic acid → baking soda / dock leaf. Tamarind tartaric, tomato oxalic, curd lactic, vinegar acetic.
  13. Salt pH: strong acid + strong base neutral (NaCl); strong acid + weak base acidic (NH₄Cl); weak acid + strong base basic (Na₂CO₃).
  14. Chlor-alkali: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ (anode) + H₂ (cathode).
  15. Bleaching powder CaOCl₂ (Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂); baking soda NaHCO₃; washing soda Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
  16. CuSO₄·5H₂O blue → white on heating; Plaster of Paris CaSO₄·½H₂O from gypsum at 373 K, sets with water.
