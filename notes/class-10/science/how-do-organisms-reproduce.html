Chapter 7 · Class 10 Science

How do Organisms Reproduce?

At a glance

Reproduction does not keep an individual alive, but it keeps the species going. At its heart is the copying of DNA, which is never perfect, so small variations appear — the raw material that lets a population survive when its surroundings change. Single organisms reproduce asexually by fission, fragmentation, regeneration, budding, vegetative propagation or spores. Sexual reproduction joins germ cells from two parents and creates more variation. The chapter then follows sexual reproduction in a flowering plant and in human beings, and ends with reproductive health and family planning.

Concepts, step by step

Do organisms create exact copies of themselves?

Offspring resemble their parents because body design is coded in DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) in the chromosomes of the nucleus. DNA carries the information for making proteins; a change in that information gives a different protein and possibly a different body feature. So the basic event in reproduction is DNA copying: the cell makes two copies of its DNA and also an extra set of cell apparatus, then divides into two cells, each with a full copy.

No copying reaction is perfectly accurate, so the copies are similar but not identical. These small differences are variations. Drastic changes make the new cell unviable; minor ones give each offspring slight differences. Consistent DNA copying keeps the body design that suits an organism's niche (its place in the ecosystem).

Importance of variation: if the niche changes drastically, a population of identical individuals could be wiped out. For example, if the water of a pond warms up, most bacteria in it may die, but a few variants that can resist heat survive and multiply. Variation is thus useful for the survival of species over time.

Fission

Single-celled organisms such as bacteria and protozoa simply split into two halves during cell division. In binary fission the nucleus divides first and then the cytoplasm, giving two daughter cells. Amoeba can split in any plane, but Leishmania (which causes kala-azar) has a whip-like structure at one end and splits in a definite plane (lengthwise) with respect to it.

Activity — prepared slides: Amoeba is seen with its nucleus elongating and the cell constricting in the middle.

In multiple fission one cell divides into many daughter cells at once. The malarial parasite Plasmodium reproduces this way.

Fragmentation and regeneration

Fragmentation: a simple multicellular organism breaks into pieces, and each piece grows into a new individual. Spirogyra, a filamentous green alga in ponds, does this when mature.

Activity — Spirogyra: under a microscope its filaments are seen to be made of many similar cells, so any fragment can carry on all life processes and grow.

Fragmentation works only for organisms with little cell specialisation. Complex organisms cannot simply break apart, because their tissues form organs placed at definite positions. Many fully differentiated organisms can still regrow lost parts: this is regeneration. If Planaria or Hydra is cut into pieces, each piece grows into a complete organism. Specialised cells multiply and then change into different cell types and tissues in an organised sequence called development. Regeneration is not the same as reproduction, because most organisms do not normally depend on being cut up to reproduce.

Budding

In budding an outgrowth (bud) develops from the parent at a particular site because of repeated cell division. Hydra forms a bud on its body; the bud grows tentacles and a mouth, then detaches and becomes an independent individual. Yeast also buds.

Activity — yeast in sugar solution: about 10 mL of water with a teaspoon of sugar and a pinch of yeast is kept in a warm place for an hour. Under the microscope the yeast cells are seen with small buds attached, some in chains, because the cells have used the sugar for energy and multiplied.

Vegetative propagation

In many plants new plants grow from parts such as the root, stem or leaves. This is vegetative propagation.

  • Natural: a potato has notches (“eyes”) with buds that sprout into new shoots; Bryophyllum leaves bear buds in the notches along the margin, and these fall on soil and grow into new plants.
  • Artificial: layering and grafting are used in agriculture for sugarcane, roses and grapes; a money plant grows from a stem piece that includes a node with a leaf, but not from a piece cut between leaves.

Advantages: plants that have lost the ability to produce seeds, such as banana, orange, rose and jasmine, can still be grown; plants raised this way flower and fruit earlier than those grown from seed; and all the new plants are genetically similar to the parent, so desirable features are kept.

Spore formation

Activity — bread mould: moist bread kept in a cool, dark place for a few days develops a cottony growth of fine threads (hyphae) with tiny black-headed blobs: the mould Rhizopus.

The round blobs on erect hyphae are sporangia. Each contains many spores, which are covered by thick walls that protect them until they reach a moist surface; then they germinate and grow into new Rhizopus plants.

Why the sexual mode of reproduction?

Asexual reproduction copies DNA from a single parent, so variations are few. Sexual reproduction combines DNA from two individuals, producing new combinations and much more variation, which helps a species survive.

If each parent simply passed on its full DNA, the amount would double every generation. To avoid this, sexual organisms make special germ cells (gametes) with half the number of chromosomes (and half the DNA). When a male and a female gamete fuse, the normal number is restored, keeping it constant from generation to generation.

Germ cells are also specialised: one is motile and small (the male gamete) and the other is large and stores food for the developing embryo (the female gamete).

Sexual reproduction in flowering plants

The reproductive parts of an angiosperm are in the flower: sepals, petals, stamens (male) and pistil (carpel, female). The stamen has a filament and an anther that makes yellowish pollen grains. The pistil, in the centre, has a sticky stigma, an elongated style and a swollen ovary containing ovules, each with an egg cell.

A flower with only stamens or only a pistil is unisexual (papaya, watermelon); one with both is bisexual (Hibiscus, mustard).

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma. In self-pollination it reaches the stigma of the same flower; in cross-pollination it is carried to another flower by wind, water or animals.

Fertilisation: on the stigma the pollen grain grows a pollen tube, which passes down the style to the ovule and delivers the male germ cell, which fuses with the egg cell. The zygote divides many times to form an embryo within the ovule.

ovule → seed · ovary → fruit · petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma shrivel and fall

The seed holds the future plant. Under suitable conditions it germinates: the plumule grows into the shoot, the radicle into the root, and the cotyledons supply stored food. Activity: gram seeds soaked overnight, kept on a damp cloth for a day and then split open show these parts.

Reproduction in human beings: puberty

Humans reproduce sexually. During adolescence, at puberty, the reproductive tissues mature and the body changes.

  • Common to both: thick hair grows in the armpits and genital area; skin becomes oily and pimples may appear.
  • Girls: breasts begin to enlarge and menstruation starts.
  • Boys: facial hair (beard, moustache) appears and the voice begins to crack.

Male reproductive system

The testes, located in the scrotum outside the abdominal cavity, form the male germ cells (sperms). They hang outside because sperm formation needs a temperature lower than normal body temperature. The testes also secrete the hormone testosterone, which brings about the changes of puberty in boys.

Sperms travel through the vas deferens, which joins a tube coming from the urinary bladder; the urethra is thus a common passage for sperms and urine. The seminal vesicles and prostate gland add secretions so that sperms are in a fluid, making transport easier and providing nutrition.

Female reproductive system and what happens after fertilisation

The female germ cells (eggs) are made in the two ovaries, which also secrete hormones. A girl is born with thousands of immature eggs; at puberty some start maturing, and one egg is released each month by one of the ovaries. It is carried through the thin oviduct (fallopian tube) towards the uterus, a muscular bag that opens into the vagina through the cervix.

During mating sperms enter the vagina and travel upwards; fertilisation takes place in the oviduct. The zygote divides to form an embryo, which is implanted in the thick, blood-rich lining of the uterus. The embryo gets nourishment from the mother's blood through the placenta, a disc in the uterine wall whose villi give a large surface for passing glucose and oxygen to the embryo and removing its wastes. The baby develops for about nine months and is then born by rhythmic contractions of the uterine muscles.

If the egg is not fertilised: it lives for about a day. The uterus prepares every month by thickening its lining; since this is not needed, the lining slowly breaks down and comes out through the vagina as blood and mucus. This menstruation occurs roughly every month and lasts about two to eight days.

Reproductive health

Physical maturity alone does not mean readiness for sex or parenthood. Sexual contact can spread diseases: bacterial (gonorrhoea, syphilis) and viral (warts, HIV-AIDS). Using a condom helps prevent their transmission.

Pregnancy makes major demands on the woman's body, so contraception lets couples space births:

  • Mechanical barriers: condoms on the penis or similar coverings in the vagina stop sperms reaching the egg.
  • Hormonal: oral pills change the hormonal balance so eggs are not released; they can have side effects.
  • Intra-uterine devices: the loop or copper-T placed in the uterus; it may cause side effects from irritation of the uterus.
  • Surgical: blocking the vas deferens in males (sperm transfer prevented) or the fallopian tube in females (egg cannot reach the uterus).

Misuse of surgery for female foeticide after prenatal sex determination has sharply lowered the child sex ratio in some places; prenatal sex determination is prohibited by law in India.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

What is the importance of DNA copying in reproduction? (2 marks)

DNA in the nucleus carries the information for making proteins, which decide the body design. Copying DNA passes this information to the offspring, so they have the same basic body design as the parent and can live in the same niche. Because copying is not perfectly accurate, small variations also arise, which help the species survive if conditions change.

How does binary fission differ from multiple fission? (2 marks)
Binary fissionMultiple fission
Parent cell divides into two daughter cells.Parent cell divides into many daughter cells at the same time.
Example: Amoeba, Leishmania.Example: Plasmodium.
Why is vegetative propagation practised for growing some types of plants? (2 marks)

(i) It allows plants that have lost the capacity to produce seeds — such as banana, orange, rose and jasmine — to be grown. (ii) Plants raised this way bear flowers and fruits earlier than those grown from seed. (iii) All new plants are genetically similar to the parent, so its desirable characters are kept.

Why is the number of chromosomes in germ cells half that of other body cells? What would happen if this were not so? (3 marks)
  1. In sexual reproduction two germ cells, one from each parent, fuse to form the zygote.
  2. If each germ cell had the full number of chromosomes, the zygote would have double the number, and the amount of DNA would double in every generation.
  3. Germ cells therefore carry half the chromosomes, so that fusion restores the normal number.

This keeps the chromosome number (and DNA content) constant from one generation to the next, which is needed for the cell's machinery to work properly.

Describe the role of the placenta. Why does the embryo need it? (3 marks)

After implantation the embryo develops inside the uterus and cannot feed or breathe by itself. The placenta is a disc-shaped tissue embedded in the uterine wall. It has finger-like villi on the embryo's side, surrounded by blood spaces on the mother's side, giving a large surface area. Through it glucose and oxygen pass from the mother's blood to the embryo, and waste substances (such as carbon dioxide) pass from the embryo into the mother's blood for removal.

What are the different methods of contraception? Why is surgical method not always preferred? (3 marks)
  • Barrier methods: condoms stop sperms from reaching the egg and also protect against sexually transmitted diseases.
  • Hormonal methods: oral pills change the hormonal balance so that eggs are not released.
  • IUCD: loop or copper-T placed in the uterus prevents pregnancy.
  • Surgical methods: vas deferens blocked in males; fallopian tube blocked in females.

Surgery is safe in the long run, but if not done properly it can cause infection and other problems, and it is difficult to reverse.

(a) Draw a longitudinal section of a flower and label stigma, style, ovary, anther and filament. (b) Distinguish between self-pollination and cross-pollination. (c) Describe how fertilisation takes place and name the parts that develop into the seed and the fruit. (5 marks)
  1. (a) Draw Fig. 2 and label the stigma, style, ovary (with ovules), anther and filament; mark the stamen as male and the pistil as female.
  2. (b) Self-pollination: pollen from the anther reaches the stigma of the same flower. Cross-pollination: pollen is carried to the stigma of another flower of the same kind by agents such as wind, water or animals.
  3. (c) After pollination the pollen grain on the stigma grows a pollen tube, which travels through the style to reach the ovule in the ovary. The male germ cell in the tube fuses with the female germ cell (egg) in the ovule — this is fertilisation, and it forms a zygote, which divides to form the embryo.
  4. The ovule develops a tough coat and becomes the seed; the ovary grows and ripens into the fruit. Petals, sepals, stamens, style and stigma shrivel and fall off.
(a) Name the parts of the human female reproductive system where (i) eggs are made, (ii) fertilisation takes place, (iii) the embryo is implanted. (b) What happens to the uterine lining if the egg is not fertilised? (c) How do the male gamete and the female gamete differ? (5 marks)
  1. (a) (i) Ovary; (ii) oviduct (fallopian tube); (iii) uterus (its thick inner wall).
  2. (b) Each month the uterus thickens its lining and enriches it with blood to receive a fertilised egg. If the egg is not fertilised it lives for only about one day, the thick lining is not needed, and it slowly breaks down and is discharged through the vagina as blood and mucus. This is menstruation; it occurs roughly every month and lasts about 2–8 days.
  3. (c) The sperm (male gamete) is tiny and motile: it has genetic material in its head and a long tail for swimming. The egg (female gamete) is larger, non-motile and contains stored food. Both carry half the number of chromosomes.

Common mistakes

MistakeWhat students writeWrite this instead
Calling regeneration reproduction“Planaria reproduces by regeneration.”Planaria can regenerate from pieces, but regeneration is not the same as reproduction: organisms do not normally depend on being cut up.
Wrong plane of fission“Leishmania divides in any plane like Amoeba.”Amoeba splits in any plane; Leishmania splits in a definite plane with respect to its flagellum.
Mixing seed and fruit“The ovary becomes the seed.”Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit.
Wrong site of fertilisation“Fertilisation takes place in the uterus.”Fertilisation occurs in the oviduct (fallopian tube); the embryo is implanted in the uterus.
Confusing pollination with fertilisation“Pollination is the fusion of male and female gametes.”Pollination is transfer of pollen to the stigma; fertilisation is fusion of the male germ cell with the egg in the ovule.
Why testes are outside the body“Testes are in the scrotum for protection.”Sperm formation needs a temperature lower than normal body temperature.
Contraception and STDs“Oral pills and copper-T protect against HIV.”Only barrier methods such as condoms help prevent sexually transmitted diseases.

Quick revision

  1. DNA copying is the basic event of reproduction; copying errors give variations.
  2. Variation helps a species survive when its niche changes (heat-resistant bacteria in a warmed pond).
  3. Binary fission: Amoeba (any plane), Leishmania (definite plane); multiple fission: Plasmodium.
  4. Fragmentation: Spirogyra · Regeneration: Planaria, Hydra · Budding: Hydra, yeast.
  5. Spore formation: Rhizopus — sporangia release thick-walled spores.
  6. Vegetative propagation: potato eyes, Bryophyllum leaf buds; layering and grafting for sugarcane, rose, grapes.
  7. Banana, orange, rose, jasmine have lost seed-forming capacity — grown vegetatively.
  8. Germ cells have half the chromosomes; fusion restores the number in the zygote.
  9. Flower: stamen = anther + filament (male); pistil = stigma + style + ovary (female).
  10. Unisexual: papaya, watermelon · Bisexual: Hibiscus, mustard.
  11. Pollination ≠ fertilisation; pollen tube carries the male germ cell to the egg in the ovule.
  12. Ovule → seed, ovary → fruit; seed: plumule (shoot), radicle (root), cotyledons (food).
  13. Testes in scrotum (lower temperature), make sperms and testosterone; urethra carries both sperm and urine.
  14. Ovaries release one egg a month; fertilisation in oviduct; implantation in uterus; placenta with villi nourishes the embryo.
  15. No fertilisation → uterine lining breaks down → menstruation (about monthly, 2–8 days).
  16. Contraception: condom, pills, copper-T, vasectomy/tubectomy; prenatal sex determination is banned by law.
