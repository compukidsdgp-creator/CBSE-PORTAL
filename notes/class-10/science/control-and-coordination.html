Control and Coordination

At a glance

Every living thing responds to changes around it, and these responses must be controlled and timed. Animals use two systems for this: the fast nervous system of neurons, brain and spinal cord, and the slower endocrine system of hormones carried in the blood. Plants have no nerves, yet they respond to touch, light, gravity, water and chemicals using electrical-chemical signals and plant hormones. This chapter explains how each of these works and how the body keeps hormone levels in check by feedback.

Concepts, step by step

Why do organisms need control and coordination?

Living things move in response to their surroundings: we pull our hand away from a hot plate, a plant bends towards a window. Some movements are linked to growth, others are not. Either way, the movement must be a controlled response to a particular change (a stimulus) and must be carefully timed. In multicellular animals this job is done by two specialised systems working together: the nervous system (with muscular tissue to carry out the action) and the endocrine (hormonal) system.

Receptors, neurons and the synapse

Information from the environment is picked up by receptors, the specialised tips of nerve cells, usually located in sense organs: gustatory receptors on the tongue detect taste, olfactory receptors in the nose detect smell, and others lie in the eye, the inner ear and the skin.

Activity — taste with a blocked nose: sugar still tastes sweet when the nose is pinched shut, but a full meal eaten this way seems dull. The tongue detects only basic tastes; much of the flavour of food depends on smell, which is cut off.

The neuron (nerve cell) is the functional unit of the nervous system. Information travels through it as an electrical impulse in one direction only:

dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending

At the dendrite tip a chemical reaction starts the impulse; it passes through the cell body and runs along the long axon. At the axon's end the impulse causes the release of chemicals. These cross a tiny gap, the synapse, and start a new electrical impulse in the dendrite of the next neuron. A similar synapse passes the message from the last neuron to a muscle cell or gland. Because only the axon end releases chemicals, the impulse cannot travel backwards.

Reflex action and the reflex arc

A reflex action is a sudden, automatic response to a stimulus that happens without conscious thought — withdrawing a hand from a flame, blinking when something comes near the eye, the knee jerk. Thinking involves complex interaction among many neurons in the brain and is too slow for danger, so the body uses a shorter route.

The pathway of a reflex is the reflex arc:

receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector (muscle/gland)

Nerves from all over the body meet in a bundle in the spinal cord on their way to the brain, so the connections for a reflex are made right there. The input is processed in the spinal cord and the response is sent out at once. The message also continues to the brain, so we become aware of the event a moment later. Reflex arcs probably evolved in animals whose neurons could not support complex thought, and they remain efficient even in animals with a well-developed brain.

The human nervous system and the brain

The central nervous system (CNS) is made up of the brain and the spinal cord. The peripheral nervous system consists of cranial nerves arising from the brain and spinal nerves arising from the spinal cord; it links the CNS to every part of the body. The brain is the main coordinating centre. It allows voluntary actions (writing, talking, moving a chair), which are done after thinking, while involuntary actions such as heartbeat and salivation happen on their own.

  • Forebrain — the main thinking part (the cerebrum). It has separate areas that receive impulses from different receptors (hearing, smell, sight), association areas that interpret this information together with stored memory, and areas that send instructions to the voluntary muscles. A centre for hunger and feeling full is also located here. The hypothalamus also lies in the forebrain.
  • Midbrain — controls certain involuntary actions, such as reflex movements of the head, neck and eyes.
  • Hindbrain — has three parts. The medulla controls involuntary actions such as blood pressure, salivation and vomiting. The pons helps regulate breathing. The cerebellum gives precision to voluntary actions and maintains posture and balance — walking in a straight line, riding a bicycle, picking up a pencil.

How are the brain and spinal cord protected?

These delicate organs are well guarded. The brain sits inside a bony box, the skull (cranium), and within it is surrounded by a fluid-filled balloon that absorbs shocks (cerebrospinal fluid). The spinal cord is enclosed by the vertebral column (backbone).

How does nervous tissue cause action?

The final message reaches a muscle. Muscle cells contain special proteins that change both their shape and arrangement in the cell when a nerve impulse arrives. This new arrangement makes the muscle cells shorter, so the muscle contracts and the part moves. Voluntary muscles (attached to bones, under our control) and involuntary muscles (in the heart, gut and blood vessels) both work this way.

Coordination in plants: immediate response

Plants have neither a nervous system nor muscles, so they respond in two ways: some movements are immediate and do not involve growth, others depend on growth.

When the leaves of the sensitive plant (touch-me-not, Mimosa pudica) are touched, they fold up and droop within seconds. There is no growth involved. The plant cells pass the information from cell to cell by electrical-chemical means, since there is no specialised conducting tissue. The cells at the base of the leaflets then change shape by changing the amount of water in them: they lose water and become flaccid, and the leaflets fold.

Coordination in plants: movement due to growth (tropisms)

A tropic movement is a slow, directional growth of a plant part in response to a stimulus — towards it (positive) or away from it (negative).

  • Phototropism (light): shoots bend towards light (positive); roots grow away from it (negative).
  • Geotropism (gravity): roots grow downwards (positive); shoots grow upwards (negative).
  • Hydrotropism (water): roots grow towards water.
  • Chemotropism (chemicals): the pollen tube grows towards the ovule.
  • Tendrils of a pea plant are sensitive to touch. The side of the tendril away from the support grows faster than the side in contact, so the tendril coils around the support.

Activity — response to light: freshly germinated bean seeds are placed on a wire mesh over the neck of a flask of water, inside a box open on one side towards light. After two or three days the shoots bend towards the light and the roots grow down into the water. If the flask is turned so the shoots face away from the light, the shoots bend again towards it; the roots stay downwards.

Plant hormones

Growth responses are slow and are coordinated by chemical compounds called plant hormones (phytohormones), which spread from cell to cell.

  • Auxin — made at the shoot tip; helps cells grow longer. When light falls from one side, auxin diffuses towards the shaded side. Cells on the shaded side elongate more, so the shoot bends towards the light.
  • Gibberellins — help the stem grow longer.
  • Cytokinins — promote cell division; found in high concentration where cells divide rapidly, such as fruits and seeds.
  • Abscisic acid — inhibits growth; its effects include wilting of leaves.

Hormones in animals

Electrical impulses have limits: they reach only cells connected by nerves, and a cell needs time to reset before it can carry the next impulse, so it cannot send impulses continuously. Animals therefore also use chemical communication. Endocrine glands release hormones directly into the blood; the hormones reach every cell, and only target cells with special molecules on their surface respond. Hormonal control is slower but steady and widespread.

  • Adrenaline (adrenal glands, above the kidneys) prepares the body for an emergency — the fight-or-flight response. The heart beats faster to supply more oxygen to the muscles; muscles around small arteries of the digestive system and skin contract, diverting blood to the skeletal muscles; breathing becomes faster as the diaphragm and rib muscles contract.
  • Thyroxin (thyroid gland) regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism. Iodine is needed to make it; lack of iodine causes goitre, a swollen neck. Hence iodised salt is advised.
  • Growth hormone (pituitary) controls growth and development. Too little in childhood causes dwarfism; too much causes gigantism. The hypothalamus releases releasing hormones that trigger the pituitary, e.g. to secrete growth hormone.
  • Testosterone (testes, in males) and oestrogen (ovaries, in females) bring about the changes of puberty.
  • Insulin (pancreas) lowers blood sugar. If it is not secreted in proper amounts, blood sugar rises and causes diabetes.

Feedback mechanism

Both too much and too little of a hormone are harmful, so the timing and amount of secretion are controlled by feedback. When blood sugar rises after a meal, cells of the pancreas detect it and secrete more insulin; as the sugar level falls, insulin secretion is reduced. The result itself switches the gland down, keeping blood sugar within a steady range.

Worked board questions

Try each question first, then open it to check the model answer.

Which part of the brain maintains posture and balance of the body? (1 mark)

The cerebellum, a part of the hindbrain.

What is a synapse? How is a nerve impulse passed across it? (2 marks)

A synapse is the tiny gap between the nerve ending of one neuron and the dendrite of the next neuron (or a muscle/gland cell). The electrical impulse reaching the axon end causes the release of chemicals; these diffuse across the gap and start a new electrical impulse in the next neuron. So the message is chemical at the synapse, and it can only move in one direction.

Why is the use of iodised salt advisable? (2 marks)

The thyroid gland needs iodine to make the hormone thyroxin, which regulates carbohydrate, protein and fat metabolism. If the diet lacks iodine, thyroxin is not made in sufficient amounts and the thyroid swells, causing goitre. Iodised salt supplies the iodine needed.

How is the movement of leaves of the sensitive plant different from the movement of a shoot towards light? (3 marks)
Touch-me-not (Mimosa) leavesShoot bending towards light
Stimulus: touchStimulus: light
Immediate — happens in secondsSlow — takes hours or days
Not related to growth; cells change shape by losing waterCaused by unequal growth (auxin makes cells on the shaded side grow longer)
Not directional — the leaves fold whichever side is touchedDirectional — a tropic movement (phototropism)
Explain how auxin makes a shoot bend towards light. (3 marks)
  1. Auxin is synthesised at the tip of the shoot and helps cells grow longer.
  2. When light falls on the shoot from one side, auxin diffuses towards the shaded side.
  3. The higher concentration of auxin makes cells on the shaded side elongate more than cells on the lit side.

The shaded side grows longer, so the shoot curves towards the light — positive phototropism.

How does our body keep the blood sugar level steady? Name the hormone and gland involved. (3 marks)

The hormone insulin, secreted by the pancreas, lowers blood sugar. Its secretion is controlled by a feedback mechanism: when blood sugar rises (for example after a meal), cells of the pancreas detect the rise and release more insulin; as the sugar level falls back to normal, insulin secretion is reduced. If insulin is not secreted in proper amounts, blood sugar stays high and the person suffers from diabetes.

(a) What is a reflex action? (b) Trace the path of a reflex action with a flow chart. (c) Why are reflex actions controlled by the spinal cord rather than the brain? (d) Draw a labelled diagram of a reflex arc. (5 marks)
  1. (a) A reflex action is a sudden, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, done without thinking — e.g. pulling the hand away on touching a flame.
  2. (b) Stimulus (heat) → receptor in skin → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay neuron) → motor neuron → effector (arm muscle) → response: hand withdrawn.
  3. (c) Thinking in the brain involves many neurons and takes too long; damage would be done before the response. The connection between sensory and motor neurons is made in the spinal cord, so the response is quick. The message still goes on to the brain, which becomes aware of what happened.
  4. (d) Draw Fig. 2 with labels: receptor, sensory neuron, spinal cord, relay neuron, motor neuron, effector (muscle), and arrows for the direction of the impulse.
(a) Why do animals need hormones in addition to the nervous system? (b) Name the gland that secretes adrenaline and describe how this hormone prepares the body to face danger. (5 marks)
  1. (a) Nerve impulses reach only those cells that are connected to nerves. Also, after carrying an impulse, a cell needs time to reset its mechanism before it can carry another, so cells cannot create and send impulses continuously. Hormones travel in the blood to all cells and act on target cells over a longer period, providing slow, steady, widespread control.
  2. (b) Adrenaline is secreted by the adrenal glands (located above the kidneys) directly into the blood. In an emergency it:
    • makes the heart beat faster, supplying more oxygen to the muscles;
    • makes the muscles around small arteries of the digestive system and skin contract, diverting blood to the skeletal muscles;
    • increases the breathing rate through contractions of the diaphragm and rib muscles.
    Together these prepare the body to fight or run away.

Quick revision

  1. Two control systems in animals: nervous (fast, electrical) and endocrine (slower, hormones in blood).
  2. Receptors: gustatory (taste), olfactory (smell), plus eye, inner ear, skin.
  3. Neuron: dendrite → cell body → axon → nerve ending; impulse is electrical inside, chemical across the synapse.
  4. Reflex arc: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (relay) → motor neuron → effector.
  5. Reflexes are made in the spinal cord because thinking in the brain is too slow; the brain is informed later.
  6. CNS = brain + spinal cord; PNS = cranial nerves + spinal nerves.
  7. Forebrain (cerebrum): thinking, senses, voluntary muscles, hunger and satiety centre.
  8. Hindbrain: medulla (BP, salivation, vomiting), pons (breathing), cerebellum (posture, balance, precision).
  9. Brain protected by skull and shock-absorbing fluid; spinal cord by the vertebral column.
  10. Muscle cells contract when special proteins change shape and arrangement on receiving an impulse.
  11. Mimosa: immediate, no growth — cells change shape by changing water content.
  12. Tropisms: shoot +photo/−geo; root +geo/+hydro; pollen tube chemotropism; tendrils coil (touch).
  13. Auxin (shoot tip) → shaded side → cells elongate → shoot bends to light. Gibberellin: stem growth. Cytokinin: cell division. ABA: inhibits growth, wilting.
  14. Adrenaline (adrenal): fight or flight — faster heart and breathing, blood diverted to skeletal muscles.
  15. Thyroxin needs iodine → deficiency gives goitre. Growth hormone (pituitary) deficiency → dwarfism. Hypothalamus releasing hormones act on the pituitary.
  16. Insulin (pancreas) lowers blood sugar; controlled by feedback; deficiency → diabetes. Testosterone/oestrogen: puberty.
