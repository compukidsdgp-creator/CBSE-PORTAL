Why is dy/dx = 0 at a maximum or minimum?

MathsCBSE Class 12⏱ 6 min readUpdated 25 November 2026
BM
Biswarup Mukherjee · Teaching Administrator, CBSEMock20 years of teaching · Reviewed by subject teachers · About us

“Put dy/dx = 0 and solve” is the first step of almost every maxima–minima question in Class 12. But why does a maximum or minimum have to be at a point where the derivative is zero? The answer is a picture of a tangent sliding along a hill.

The short answer

The derivative dy/dx is the slope of the tangent. On the way up a hill the slope is positive; on the way down it is negative. At the very top it must switch from positive to negative, and a smooth curve can only do that by passing through zero — a flat tangent. The same happens, in reverse, at the bottom of a valley.

Watch the tangent

Take the curve y = x³ − 3x. It rises, reaches a peak, falls into a valley and rises again. Slide a tangent line along it from left to right:

  • Climbing towards the peak, the tangent tilts upward: dy/dx > 0.
  • At the peak, the tangent lies perfectly flat: dy/dx = 0.
  • Going downhill, the tangent tilts downward: dy/dx < 0.
  • At the bottom of the valley it is flat again: dy/dx = 0.
  • Climbing out, the slope becomes positive once more.

So the turning points — the local maximum and local minimum — are exactly where the tangent is horizontal. These are called critical or stationary points.

Finding them for y = x³ − 3x

dy/dx = 3x² − 3. Setting it to zero: 3x² − 3 = 0 ⇒ x² = 1 ⇒ x = −1 or x = 1.

At x = −1, y = −1 + 3 = 2 (the peak). At x = 1, y = 1 − 3 = −2 (the valley).

Which is the maximum? Two tests

First derivative test. Check the sign of dy/dx just before and just after the point. Positive → negative means a maximum; negative → positive means a minimum. For x = −1: at x = −2, dy/dx = 9 > 0; at x = 0, dy/dx = −3 < 0, so x = −1 is a local maximum.

Second derivative test. d²y/dx² measures how the slope is changing. Here d²y/dx² = 6x. At x = −1 it is −6 < 0: the slope is decreasing, the curve bends downward, so it is a maximum. At x = 1 it is 6 > 0: the curve bends upward, a minimum. If the second derivative is 0, the test fails and you fall back on the first derivative test.

A warning: zero slope is not always a turning point

For y = x³, dy/dx = 3x² is zero at x = 0, but the curve keeps rising on both sides — the slope is positive, touches zero, and stays positive. This is a point of inflection, not a maximum or minimum. dy/dx = 0 tells you where to look; the tests tell you what you found.

Exam tips

  • In word problems (largest area, least cost), write the quantity as a function of one variable before differentiating.
  • Always justify a maximum or minimum with the second derivative or a sign check — answers without justification lose marks.
  • For a closed interval, also compare the values at the endpoints to find the absolute maximum or minimum.

Try it yourself

Find the critical points of f(x) = x² − 6x + 5 and classify them.

f′(x) = 2x − 6 = 0 ⇒ x = 3. f″(x) = 2 > 0, so x = 3 is a local minimum, with f(3) = −4.

For f(x) = −x² + 4x, find the maximum value.

f′(x) = −2x + 4 = 0 ⇒ x = 2; f″ = −2 < 0 so it is a maximum. f(2) = 4.

Is x = 0 a maximum or minimum of f(x) = x³? Why?

Neither. f′(0) = 0, but f′(x) = 3x² ≥ 0 on both sides, so the function keeps increasing — it is a point of inflection.
