Why does adding a resistor in parallel lower the total resistance?

PhysicsCBSE Class 10 · 12⏱ 6 min readUpdated 18 November 2026
BM
Biswarup Mukherjee · Teaching Administrator, CBSEMock20 years of teaching · Reviewed by subject teachers · About us

It feels wrong at first. You add more resistors to a circuit and the total resistance goes down. Yet that is exactly what happens when resistors are connected in parallel — and it is why every appliance in your home is wired in parallel. Once you see current as traffic, the result becomes obvious.

The short answer

A resistor in parallel does not sit in the way of the current — it opens a new path beside the old one. With the same battery voltage pushing, more paths let more current flow. Since R = V / I, a bigger current at the same voltage means a smaller total resistance.

Think of current as cars on roads

Picture the battery as a city that sends cars (charge) out, and each resistor as a road. With one narrow road, only so many cars get through each second — say 1 ampere. Now build a second, identical road right beside the first. Each road still carries 1 A, because each one still has the full voltage across it. Together they carry 2 A. The city sees twice the traffic flowing for the same push, so the combination behaves like a road with half the resistance.

Compare this with series: putting a second road at the end of the first makes one longer road. Every car must travel both, so traffic slows and resistance adds up: R = R₁ + R₂.

Deriving the formula

  1. In parallel, every resistor is connected across the same two points, so each has the same voltage V.
  2. The current through each is given by Ohm's law: I₁ = V/R₁ and I₂ = V/R₂.
  3. The main current splits and rejoins, so I = I₁ + I₂ (charge is conserved).
  4. Write I = V/R for the whole combination: V/R = V/R₁ + V/R₂.
  5. Divide by V: 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂.

Because you are adding positive fractions, 1/R is bigger than either 1/R₁ or 1/R₂ alone — so R is always smaller than the smallest resistor in the group. That single fact lets you check your answers instantly.

Worked examples

Q1. Two 6 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance.

A. 1/R = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6, so R = 3 Ω. Two equal resistors in parallel always give half of one: n equal resistors give R/n.

Q2. 3 Ω and 6 Ω are in parallel across a 6 V battery. Find the total current.

A. 1/R = 1/3 + 1/6 = 3/6, so R = 2 Ω. I = V/R = 6/2 = 3 A. Check: 6/3 = 2 A through one branch and 6/6 = 1 A through the other, total 3 A. ✔

A handy shortcut for exactly two resistors: R = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂) — “product over sum”. For 3 Ω and 6 Ω: 18/9 = 2 Ω.

Why homes are wired in parallel

  • Every appliance gets the full mains voltage (220 V in India).
  • Each appliance can be switched on or off without affecting the others.
  • If one bulb fuses, the others keep working — unlike old series fairy lights.

The flip side: every appliance you switch on adds another path and the total current drawn from the mains rises. That is why overloading a socket is dangerous and why circuits need fuses or MCBs.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to flip at the end. If 1/R = 1/2, then R = 2 Ω, not 0.5 Ω.
  • Adding resistances in parallel. Only series resistances add directly.
  • Getting an answer larger than the smallest resistor. In parallel that is impossible — recheck.

Try it yourself

Three 9 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. What is the equivalent resistance?

n equal resistors in parallel give R/n = 9/3 = 3 Ω.

A 4 Ω and a 12 Ω resistor are in parallel. Find the equivalent resistance.

Product over sum: (4 × 12)/(4 + 12) = 48/16 = 3 Ω.

Why does a fused bulb not switch off the other bulbs in a house?

House wiring is parallel, so each bulb is on its own path across the mains; breaking one path leaves the others complete.
