Why is the area of a circle πr²?

MathsCBSE Class 105 min readUpdated 15 November 2026
BM
Biswarup Mukherjee · Teaching Administrator, CBSEMock20 years of teaching · Reviewed by subject teachers · About us

Every Class 10 student can write A = πr². Far fewer can say why it is true — and that is exactly what an examiner is testing when a question asks you to “justify” a formula or when a word problem looks unfamiliar. The good news: the reason fits into one picture.

The short answer

Cut a circle into very thin rings, straighten every ring into a strip and stack the strips. They form a triangle whose base is the circumference, 2πr, and whose height is the radius, r. The area of that triangle is ½ × 2πr × r = πr². Nothing was added or removed, so the circle has the same area.

Step 1: think of a circle as rings

Imagine the circle is made of very thin rings, like the layers of an onion or the grooves of a vinyl record. The innermost ring is tiny; the outermost ring runs right around the edge. The length of any ring is its circumference, so a ring at distance x from the centre has length 2πx. The outermost ring, at distance r, has length 2πr.

Step 2: cut and straighten every ring

Snip each ring at the bottom and pull it out straight. Each ring becomes a thin strip. Lay the strips on top of one another, the longest (outermost ring) at the bottom and the shortest (centre) at the top. Because the ring lengths grow evenly — 2πx grows in direct proportion to x — the stack narrows evenly from bottom to top. In other words, the stack is a triangle.

Step 3: read off the base and height

  • Base = length of the outermost ring = 2πr.
  • Height = number of rings × thickness = the distance from centre to edge = r.

So the area of the triangle is ½ × base × height = ½ × 2πr × r = πr². Since we only rearranged the pieces of the circle, the circle's area is also πr².

Why is this more than a trick?

This “slice it thin and add up the pieces” idea is the heart of integration, which you will meet properly in Class 12. Adding up rings of area 2πx · dx from x = 0 to x = r gives exactly πr². The picture you just saw is the integral in disguise. It also explains why area always involves r²: one factor of r comes from the size of the rings and the other from how many rings there are.

A worked example

Q. A circular park has a radius of 21 m. Find its area. (Take π = 22/7.)

A. Area = πr² = (22/7) × 21 × 21 = 22 × 3 × 21 = 1386 m².

Notice how choosing π = 22/7 makes the arithmetic clean when the radius is a multiple of 7 — a hint the board often gives deliberately.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using the diameter in place of the radius. If the question gives d = 14 cm, the radius is 7 cm, so the area is 154 cm², not 616 cm².
  • Mixing up area and circumference. Area (πr²) is in square units; circumference (2πr) is in plain units. Check your units.
  • Forgetting to square only the radius. πr² means π × r × r, not (πr)².

Try it yourself

Find the area of a circle whose circumference is 44 cm. (Take π = 22/7.)

2πr = 44 ⇒ r = 7 cm. Area = (22/7) × 7 × 7 = 154 cm².

If the radius of a circle is doubled, what happens to its area?

Area becomes π(2r)² = 4πr², so it becomes four times as large.

A wheel has a diameter of 28 cm. Find its area.

r = 14 cm. Area = (22/7) × 14 × 14 = 616 cm².
