How does a computer see an image?

AICBSE Class 10 · 125 min readUpdated 15 November 2026
BM
Biswarup Mukherjee · Teaching Administrator, CBSEMock20 years of teaching · Reviewed by subject teachers · About us

When you look at a photo of a friend, you recognise them instantly. A computer looking at the same photo starts with something much less helpful: a long list of numbers. Computer Vision, one of the three domains of AI in the CBSE syllabus, is the art of turning those numbers back into meaning.

The short answer

A digital image is a grid of pixels, and every pixel is stored as numbers. A computer “sees” by sliding small filters over these numbers to find places where they change sharply — edges. Layer by layer, edges combine into shapes and shapes into objects such as eyes, wheels or faces.

Step 1: an image is a grid of pixels

Zoom far enough into any picture and it breaks into tiny squares called pixels. A 1080 × 1080 image has over a million of them. Each pixel holds a single colour, and the computer stores that colour as a number.

Step 2: every pixel is a number

In a grayscale image each pixel is one number from 0 (black) to 255 (white); values in between are shades of grey. A colour image stores three numbers per pixel — the amounts of Red, Green and Blue (RGB), each from 0 to 255. Pure red is (255, 0, 0); white is (255, 255, 255). So a colour photo is really three grids of numbers stacked together.

Step 3: filters find edges

To find shapes, the computer slides a small grid of weights — a filter or kernel, often 3 × 3 — across the image. At each position it multiplies the filter's weights by the pixel values underneath and adds them up. In smooth regions the total stays small; where the numbers jump suddenly, such as the border between a dark eye and light skin, the total becomes large. Those large values mark edges.

Step 4: layers build understanding

A Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) stacks many such filter layers. The first layers detect simple edges and corners. Later layers combine edges into textures and parts (an eye, a nose), and the final layers combine parts into whole objects (a face). The network is not told which filters to use — it learns them from thousands of labelled example images during training.

Where you meet computer vision every day

  • Face unlock on phones and face-tagging in photo apps.
  • Scanning documents and reading text from images (OCR).
  • Self-driving cars detecting lanes, signs and pedestrians.
  • Medical imaging that highlights possible problems in X-rays and scans.

How this appears in exams

Board questions typically ask you to define a pixel, explain resolution, state what an RGB value represents, or identify the AI domain of an application (“A phone that unlocks when it sees your face uses which domain of AI?” — Computer Vision). Case-study questions may describe a scenario and ask how images are turned into data the model can use.

Try it yourself

What is the range of values for a pixel in an 8-bit grayscale image, and what do the ends represent?

0 to 255; 0 is black and 255 is white.

How many numbers are needed to store one pixel of a colour (RGB) image?

Three — one each for the red, green and blue intensity.

Name the AI domain used by a traffic camera that reads number plates.

Computer Vision (reading text from images is optical character recognition, a computer-vision task).
