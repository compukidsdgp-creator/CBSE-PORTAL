About this chapter

This chapter teaches you to write and balance chemical equations and to recognise the main types of reactions: combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, and oxidation–reduction. Everyday effects such as corrosion of iron and rancidity of fats are explained using oxidation. Board questions often give an observation — a colour change, a gas evolved, a precipitate — and ask you to identify the reaction type and write the balanced equation.

Answers & explanations — Chemical Reactions & Equations

Try the quiz above first, then open each question to check the answer and the reasoning.

Q1. A balanced chemical equation satisfies the law of:
  1. Conservation of energy
  2. Conservation of mass
  3. Conservation of momentum
  4. Definite proportions only

Answer: (B) Conservation of mass — Law of conservation of mass: atoms are neither created nor destroyed. Total mass of reactants = products.

Q2. Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu is an example of:
  1. Combination reaction
  2. Decomposition reaction
  3. Displacement reaction
  4. Double displacement

Answer: (C) Displacement reaction — Zinc displaces copper from copper sulphate — single displacement/substitution reaction.

Q3. 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ is an example of:
  1. Combination
  2. Decomposition
  3. Displacement
  4. Redox

Answer: (B) Decomposition — One compound breaking into simpler substances = decomposition reaction.

Q4. Rancidity of food is due to:
  1. Reduction of fats
  2. Oxidation of fats and oils
  3. Hydrolysis
  4. Fermentation

Answer: (B) Oxidation of fats and oils — Rancidity: slow oxidation of unsaturated fats in air produces unpleasant odour/taste. Prevented by antioxidants.

Q5. In the reaction Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe (thermite), Al is:
  1. Oxidised and acts as reducing agent
  2. Reduced and acts as oxidising agent
  3. Neither oxidised nor reduced
  4. A catalyst

Answer: (A) Oxidised and acts as reducing agent — Al goes from 0 to +3 (oxidised); Al reduces Fe³⁺ to Fe. Al is the reducing agent.

Q6. Corrosion of iron is called rusting. The chemical formula of rust is:
  1. Fe₂O₃
  2. FeO
  3. Fe₃O₄
  4. Fe₂O₃·xH₂O

Answer: (D) Fe₂O₃·xH₂O — Rust = hydrated iron(III) oxide: Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (reddish-brown). Requires both O₂ and H₂O.

Q7. NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O is an example of:
  1. Displacement
  2. Decomposition
  3. Neutralisation (double displacement)
  4. Combustion

Answer: (C) Neutralisation (double displacement) — Acid-base neutralisation is a double displacement reaction; H⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form water.

Q8. Precipitation reaction produces:
  1. Gas
  2. An insoluble solid (precipitate)
  3. Energy
  4. An acid

Answer: (B) An insoluble solid (precipitate) — When two solutions mix and form an insoluble product, it precipitates out. E.g., AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃.

Q9. Exothermic reactions release:
  1. Light only
  2. Heat (energy is given out)
  3. Absorb heat
  4. Only electrical energy

Answer: (B) Heat (energy is given out) — Exothermic: energy released to surroundings (ΔH < 0). E.g., combustion, neutralisation.

Q10. Which of the following is a combustion reaction?
  1. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
  2. C + O₂ → CO₂
  3. NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O
  4. Fe + S → FeS

Answer: (B) C + O₂ → CO₂ — Combustion: rapid reaction with oxygen releasing heat and light. C + O₂ → CO₂ is a combustion reaction.

Q11. The colour change in litmus from blue to red indicates:
  1. Alkali
  2. Acid
  3. Neutral
  4. Salt

Answer: (B) Acid — Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue.

Q12. Photolysis is decomposition by:
  1. Heat
  2. Electricity
  3. Light
  4. Water

Answer: (C) Light — Photolysis = decomposition by light. E.g., 2AgCl →(light) 2Ag + Cl₂. Used in photography.
