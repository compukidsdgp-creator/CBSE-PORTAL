About this chapter

Heredity explains how traits pass from parents to offspring. This chapter covers Mendel's experiments with pea plants, dominant and recessive traits, monohybrid and dihybrid crosses, and sex determination in humans. Board questions often ask you to predict the ratio of offspring from a cross or to explain why the father determines the sex of the child.

Answers & explanations — Heredity & Evolution

Try the quiz above first, then open each question to check the answer and the reasoning.

Q1. The character that is expressed in F1 generation (Tt) is called:
  1. Recessive
  2. Dominant
  3. Codominant
  4. Lethal

Answer: (B) Dominant — Dominant trait is expressed when only one copy of the dominant allele (T) is present.

Q2. The sex of a child is determined by:
  1. X chromosome of mother
  2. Y chromosome of father
  3. Both parents equally
  4. Mitochondrial DNA

Answer: (B) Y chromosome of father — Father gives either X (daughter) or Y (son). Mother always gives X. So Y from father determines male sex.

Q3. Mendel's laws apply to characters controlled by genes located on:
  1. Sex chromosomes
  2. Autosomes
  3. Both autosomes and sex chromosomes
  4. Mitochondria

Answer: (B) Autosomes — Classical Mendelian inheritance applies to autosomal (non-sex) genes with simple dominant-recessive relationship.

Q4. Fossils provide evidence for evolution because they show:
  1. Living organisms of the past
  2. Remains/traces of organisms that existed previously
  3. Mutations in DNA
  4. Current biodiversity

Answer: (B) Remains/traces of organisms that existed previously — Fossils are preserved remains/traces of past organisms, showing us forms that no longer exist and evolutionary progression.

Q5. Homologous organs suggest:
  1. Convergent evolution
  2. Divergent evolution from common ancestor
  3. Analogous development
  4. Acquired characters

Answer: (B) Divergent evolution from common ancestor — Homologous organs: same basic structure, different functions (e.g., whale flipper, bat wing, human arm) — divergent evolution from common ancestor.

Q6. Evolution is a change in the _____ of a population over time:
  1. Phenotype only
  2. Genotype only
  3. Allele frequency
  4. Body size

Answer: (C) Allele frequency — Evolution = change in allele frequencies in a population over successive generations.

Q7. Traits acquired during an individual's lifetime (like developed muscles):
  1. Are passed to offspring
  2. Are not heritable
  3. Become dominant
  4. Cause mutation

Answer: (B) Are not heritable — Lamarck was wrong — acquired characters are not inherited. Only genetic changes are heritable.

Q8. If a trait is controlled by a gene with two alleles T and t, the number of genotypes possible is:
  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 1

Answer: (B) 3 — Genotypes: TT, Tt, tt = 3 possible genotypes.

Q9. Natural selection acts on:
  1. Genotype only
  2. Phenotype
  3. DNA only
  4. Both genotype and phenotype equally

Answer: (B) Phenotype — Natural selection acts on phenotype (the expressed traits). Advantageous phenotypes survive and reproduce more.

Q10. Speciation occurs when:
  1. Two populations interbreed freely
  2. Reproductive isolation leads to separate gene pools
  3. Mutations occur frequently
  4. Migration increases

Answer: (B) Reproductive isolation leads to separate gene pools — Speciation: populations become reproductively isolated → separate gene pools evolve independently → new species.

Q11. If Mendel's law of independent assortment holds, dihybrid cross (RrYy × RrYy) gives ratio:
  1. 3:1
  2. 1:2:1
  3. 9:3:3:1
  4. 1:1:1:1

Answer: (C) 9:3:3:1 — Dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio: 9 R_Y_ : 3 R_yy : 3 rrY_ : 1 rryy = 9:3:3:1.

Q12. DNA is the genetic material, proved by:
  1. Mendel's experiments
  2. Griffith's transformation and Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiments
  3. Darwin's observations
  4. Morgan's fruit fly work

Answer: (B) Griffith's transformation and Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiments — Griffith (1928) showed transformation; Avery et al. (1944) proved the transforming principle was DNA.
